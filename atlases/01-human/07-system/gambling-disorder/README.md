# Gambling Disorder

## Overview

**Gambling Disorder** (previously termed "pathological gambling") was reclassified in DSM-5 (2013) from an impulse control disorder to a **behavioral addiction** — placed in the same chapter as substance use disorders — a landmark recognition that compulsive gambling shares core neurobiological mechanisms (dopamine reward dysregulation, impaired inhibitory control, cue-induced craving, and withdrawal-like states) with substance addiction despite involving no exogenous psychoactive substance.

**Epidemiology:**
- Lifetime prevalence: 0.5–3% (varies by jurisdiction and gambling availability)
- Past-year prevalence: 0.2–0.4% in general population; higher in problem gambling (subclinical): 1–3%
- Male:female ratio: ~2:1; women have more rapid escalation ("telescoping effect")
- Onset: typically adolescence to early adulthood; bimodal distribution (teens + 40s-50s)
- Risk factors: impulsivity, ADHD, family history of gambling/SUD, exposure to gambling environments, dopamine agonist medications (Parkinson's disease — dopamine dysregulation syndrome)
- High comorbidity: alcohol use disorder (73%), depression (50%), ADHD (25%)

**Why it matters beyond its prevalence:** Gambling Disorder is the **prototypical behavioral addiction** — it established that the addiction neuroscience framework (VTA-NAcc dopamine, PFC-striatum inhibitory control, opioid reinforcement, sensitization, withdrawal) applies to behaviors as well as substances. This opened the conceptual and clinical framework for internet gaming disorder, food addiction, and other proposed behavioral addictions.

## Structure

### DSM-5 Criteria (F63.0)

Persistent and recurrent problematic gambling behavior causing distress (≥4 of 9 in 12 months):

| Criterion | Description |
|:---|:---|
| **Preoccupation** | Spending much time thinking about gambling (past, future, or strategies) |
| **Tolerance** | Need to gamble with increasing amounts to achieve desired excitement |
| **Withdrawal** | Restless or irritable when attempting to cut down or stop |
| **Loss of control** | Repeated unsuccessful efforts to control, cut back, or stop |
| **Escape** | Gambling to escape problems or relieve dysphoria |
| **Chasing** | Returning after losses to "get even" (loss-chasing) |
| **Lying** | Concealing extent of gambling from family/others |
| **Illegal acts** | Committed fraud, theft, forgery to finance gambling |
| **Jeopardized relationships** | Relationships, job, or education threatened |
| **Bailout** | Relying on others to relieve financial crises from gambling |

**Note:** Criminal acts criterion distinguishes gambling disorder from other addictions by including behaviors driven by gambling-related financial desperation.

**Severity:** Mild (4-5), Moderate (6-7), Severe (8-9)

### Assessment Tools

| Tool | Purpose |
|:---|:---|
| **PGSI (Problem Gambling Severity Index)** | 9-item community screening; 0-27 score |
| **NODS (NORC DSM Screen for Gambling Problems)** | DSM-IV/5 based; 10 lifetime + past-year items |
| **SOGS (South Oaks Gambling Screen)** | 20-item; classic research tool |
| **G-SAS (Gambling Symptom Assessment Scale)** | Clinician-rated severity; used in pharmacotherapy trials |

## Function

### Neurobiology of Gambling Disorder

**Reward prediction error and dopamine:**
- Gambling outcomes generate **reward prediction error (RPE)** signals in VTA DA neurons:
  - Unexpected wins → positive RPE → ↑ DA firing → NAcc surge → reinforcement
  - Expected wins → no change
  - Losses → negative RPE → ↓ DA → aversive signal
- **Near-miss effect:** A near-miss (e.g., two matching symbols out of three on a slot machine) generates brain activity similar to a win in gambling disorder — without the reward — falsely reinforcing continued play; near-miss response is blunted in healthy controls but exaggerated in gambling disorder
- **D2/D3 receptor hypofunction:** PET imaging shows reduced striatal D2/D3R availability in gambling disorder — identical pattern to alcohol, cocaine, and methamphetamine use disorders; reduced D2R → less reward from natural reinforcers → vulnerability to high-stimulus gambling [^potenza-2014-gambling-neuroscience]

**Dopamine agonist-induced gambling (Parkinson's):**
- D2/D3 agonists (pramipexole, ropinirole) used for Parkinson's disease → 3–6% develop pathological gambling as a side effect (impulse control disorders)
- Mechanism: dopaminergic over-stimulation of mesolimbic D3R (predominant in ventral striatum) → excessive reinforcement of reward-seeking behaviors
- Resolves with agonist dose reduction; a natural experiment proving dopaminergic causality

**Impaired inhibitory control:**
- Ventromedial PFC/OFC dysfunction → impaired integration of loss history into future decisions → "gambler's fallacy" (belief random events have memory) and persistent loss-chasing
- dlPFC hypoactivation → reduced voluntary suppression of gambling urges; Go/No-Go paradigms show reduced No-Go activation in gambling disorder
- OFC hyperactivation to gambling cues → excessive salience attribution → cue reactivity; similar to substance cue reactivity fMRI findings

**Opioid system:**
- Endogenous opioids are released during gambling wins and near-misses → MOR → euphoric reinforcement → increased gambling motivation
- Naltrexone (MOR/KOR antagonist) reduces gambling urges and frequency in multiple RCTs [^grant-2008-naltrexone-gambling]; a minority with strong family history of AUD respond particularly well (high MOR activation pattern)
- Nalmefene (MOR antagonist + κ-partial agonist): Phase 3 data positive for gambling; reduces the opioid-mediated euphoria of gambling events

## Pathology

### Subtypes of Gambling Disorder

| Subtype | Characteristics | Neurobiological profile |
|:---|:---|:---|
| **Impulsive subtype** | Early onset, high impulsivity, ADHD comorbidity, frequent high-speed gambling | ↑ DA mesolimbic; ↓ 5-HT prefrontal; ↑ NE arousal |
| **Compulsive subtype** | More ritualistic; less emotional dysregulation; more OCD comorbidity | ↓ 5-HT prefrontal; altered OFC-striatum circuit; later onset |
| **Escape/avoidance subtype** | Women more common; gambling to escape depression/stress; often slot machines | ↑ stress axis; ↓ dopamine reward; ↑ negative reinforcement |

### Gambling and Suicide

- Gambling disorder carries some of the highest suicide attempt rates of any psychiatric disorder: ~17% lifetime attempts
- Mechanism: financial ruin + shame + impulsivity + depression + social isolation → acute crisis
- Financial debt is the primary proximal driver; mental health comorbidity amplifies risk

### Gambling Disorder in the Context of Digital/Mobile Gambling

- Smartphone gambling has dramatically reduced friction: constant availability, rapid betting cycles, loot boxes in video games (structural similarities to slot machines)
- Loot boxes: randomized reward mechanisms in video games meeting many WHO criteria for gambling; associated with problem gambling in adolescents; legislation in Belgium/Netherlands
- Sports betting legalization (US 2018 Murphy v. NCAA ruling) → rapid increase in gambling disorder presentations in young adult males

### Treatment

**First-line psychosocial:**
- **CBT (Cognitive Behavioral Therapy):** Modifies gambling-related cognitions (illusion of control, hot-hand fallacy, near-miss fallacy) + behavioral: stimulus control, relapse prevention, financial counseling; 4-12 sessions; RCT evidence
- **Motivational Interviewing (MI):** Single-session "brief intervention" effective for subclinical/mild cases
- **Gamblers Anonymous (GA):** 12-step peer support; widely accessible; modest but meaningful abstinence support; best for severe disorder

**Pharmacotherapy:**
- **Naltrexone (MOR antagonist):** Best evidence; 50-200mg/day; reduces gambling urges and frequency; NNT ~5-7 in RCTs; especially effective in patients with strong family history of AUD or subjective gambling "high" [^grant-2016-gambling-review]
- **Nalmefene:** Phase 3 positive results; as-needed dosing before gambling occasions
- **SSRIs (fluvoxamine, paroxetine):** Modest evidence for comorbid depression/OCD subtypes; weaker for pure gambling disorder
- **Lithium:** Reduces impulsivity; evidence in bipolar comorbidity; experimental
- **N-acetylcysteine:** Glutamate modulator; positive Phase 2 data; reduced gambling urges in one RCT
- **Bupropion (NE/DA reuptake inhibitor):** Modest evidence; case series and small trials
