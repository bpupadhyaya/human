# Binge Eating Disorder

## Overview

**Binge Eating Disorder (BED)** is a DSM-5 feeding and eating disorder (F50.81) characterized by recurrent episodes of binge eating — consuming large amounts of food in a discrete time period with a sense of **loss of control** — without regular compensatory behaviors (distinguishing it from bulimia nervosa). BED was first recognized as a distinct diagnosis in DSM-5 (2013), having previously appeared only as "eating disorder not otherwise specified."

**Epidemiology:**
- Prevalence: **2–3% lifetime** in US adults — making it the **most prevalent eating disorder** in the United States (more common than anorexia nervosa and bulimia nervosa combined)
- Female:male ratio: ~1.5:1 — far more equal than other eating disorders; BED affects men significantly
- Mean age of onset: 20s–30s; bimodal distribution (adolescence and 40s); later onset than AN/BN
- **Comorbidity:** obesity (50–65%), major depression (50%), anxiety disorders (65%), ADHD (30%), substance use disorders (25%)
- High rates of medical complications from associated obesity: type 2 diabetes, hypertension, sleep apnea

**Why it matters:**
- Unlike AN/BN, BED is not defined by extreme weight-control behaviors — patients may present at any weight
- BED is substantially under-diagnosed: many patients feel shame and do not disclose; clinicians often focus on weight rather than eating behavior
- **Only FDA-approved medication for any eating disorder in the US** is lisdexamfetamine (Vyvanse, 2015) — approved specifically for moderate-to-severe BED [^mcelhoy-2015-lisdexamfetamine-bed]
- BED drives substantial healthcare costs through obesity-related complications

## Structure

### DSM-5 Diagnostic Criteria (F50.81)

**A. Recurrent episodes of binge eating (both required):**
1. Eating, in a discrete period of time, an amount of food that is definitely larger than what most people would eat
2. A sense of **loss of control** over eating during the episode

**B. Episodes associated with ≥3 of the following:**
- Eating much more rapidly than normal
- Eating until uncomfortably full
- Eating large amounts when not physically hungry
- Eating alone because of embarrassment about amount consumed
- Feeling disgusted, depressed, or very guilty afterward

**C.** Marked distress regarding binge eating

**D.** Occurs on average ≥1 day/week for 3 months

**E.** Not associated with recurrent use of compensatory behaviors (if yes → Bulimia Nervosa)

**Severity:**
- Mild: 1–3 episodes/week
- Moderate: 4–7 episodes/week
- Severe: 8–13 episodes/week
- Extreme: ≥14 episodes/week

## Function

### Neurobiology of binge eating

**Dopamine reward dysregulation:**
- High-palatability food (high fat + high sugar) triggers VTA-NAcc dopamine via taste reward and post-ingestive caloric signal
- PET studies show **reduced striatal D2R availability** in BED — identical pattern to substance use disorders, gambling disorder, and internet gaming disorder
- Reduced D2R → less reward from normal food → drive to consume highly palatable foods to reach reward threshold → binge cycle
- Food cue-induced NAcc activation (fMRI) parallels drug cue reactivity in SUD; OFC hyperactivation to food cues reflects overvalued food reward

**Serotonin and hypothalamic satiety:**
- Hypothalamic 5-HT2C receptors on POMC neurons → melanocortin peptide (α-MSH) release → MC4R → satiety signaling
- 5-HT2C hypofunction → reduced α-MSH → impaired satiety → binge episodes continue beyond caloric need
- SERT polymorphisms (5-HTTLPR) are associated with BED in some studies
- Fluoxetine 60mg reduces binge frequency ~50% in BED (off-label; also approved for BN)

**Ghrelin and appetite dysregulation:**
- Fasting ghrelin is elevated in BED patients compared to non-binge-eating controls matched for weight
- Post-meal ghrelin suppression is blunted in BED → failure of the normal post-prandial "stop eating" signal
- Ghrelin → hypothalamic NPY/AgRP neurons → powerful appetite drive → triggers binge episodes
- High-palatability food consumption acutely normalizes ghrelin — creating a reward loop: hunger/craving → binge → temporary relief → recurrence

**Endocannabinoid contribution:**
- 2-AG in striatum increases during consumption of high-palatability foods → CB1R → enhanced dopamine release → increased palatability and continued eating despite satiety
- CB1R in hypothalamus signals energy deficit and drives feeding
- Rimonabant (CB1R inverse agonist): reduced binge eating and body weight in clinical trials but caused psychiatric adverse effects (depression, suicidality) → withdrawn worldwide
- This pathway explains why cannabis use (exogenous CB1R agonism) reliably triggers overeating ("munchies")

### Binge Eating vs Food Addiction

The "food addiction" concept (adapted from substance addiction) captures many BED features:
- Loss of control, continued despite harm, tolerance, withdrawal-like states
- Neurobiologically validated by shared D2R hypofunction with substance use disorders
- However, food addiction and BED are not identical: food addiction implies a specific addictive substance (highly processed food), while BED focuses on loss-of-control behavior
- Yale Food Addiction Scale (YFAS) measures food addiction-like symptoms and correlates ~0.5-0.7 with BED diagnosis

## Pathology

### Medical complications

| Complication | Mechanism | Notes |
|:---|:---|:---|
| **Obesity** | Caloric excess from binge episodes | ~50-65% of BED patients have obesity |
| **Type 2 diabetes** | Obesity → insulin resistance; high glycemic binge foods | Bidirectional: T2D-related dysphoria triggers binge eating |
| **Hypertension / metabolic syndrome** | Obesity; cortisol dysregulation; NE excess | Major cardiovascular risk |
| **Sleep apnea** | Obesity; upper airway compromise | Severity correlates with binge frequency |
| **Gastrointestinal** | Rapid eating → gastroparesis, GERD, bloating | Common and often underreported |
| **Shame / depression** | Post-binge guilt cycle → depression → more binge eating | High depression comorbidity (50%) |

### Psychological comorbidities

BED is among the most psychiatrically comorbid of all eating disorders:
- **Major depression (50%):** Often bidirectional — depression drives escape binge eating; binge eating shame worsens depression; treat both
- **Anxiety disorders (65%):** Social anxiety drives secretive binge eating; GAD amplifies stress-driven bingeing
- **ADHD (30%):** Shared impulsivity; reward sensitivity; impaired executive function → difficulty stopping eating; methylphenidate and lisdexamfetamine treat both conditions
- **PTSD:** Binge eating as a coping mechanism for trauma-related distress; trauma-focused treatment essential

### Treatment

**First-line psychosocial:**
- **CBT for BED:** Modifies binge-related cognitions (food restriction beliefs, catastrophic thoughts about weight), introduces structured eating, reduces dietary restraint, develops alternative coping; 20-24 sessions; ~50–60% abstinence rates in RCTs
- **Dialectical Behavior Therapy (DBT):** Addresses emotional dysregulation driving binge eating; emotion regulation, distress tolerance, interpersonal skills; particularly effective in emotionally triggered BED
- **Interpersonal Therapy (IPT):** Addresses interpersonal problems driving binge eating; similar efficacy to CBT in long-term studies
- **Guided self-help (GSH):** Structured CBT-based workbook with minimal therapist contact; effective for mild-moderate BED; suitable for primary care settings

**Pharmacological:**
- **Lisdexamfetamine (Vyvanse, 50–70mg):** FDA-approved (2015) — only medication approved for any eating disorder; reduces binge episodes ~60% vs ~30% placebo; mechanism: DAT + NET reuptake inhibition → normalizes DA/NE salience circuits; contraindicated in CVD, anxiety, substance use history [^mcelhoy-2015-lisdexamfetamine-bed]
- **SSRIs (fluoxetine 60mg, sertraline):** Reduce binge frequency off-label; particularly useful with comorbid depression or BN features
- **Topiramate:** Antiepileptic; reduces binge frequency and weight; poorly tolerated (cognitive side effects, paraesthesias) — reserved for BED with comorbid obesity
- **GLP-1 receptor agonists (semaglutide — off-label):** Emerging evidence for binge reduction via satiety and reward circuit effects; under investigation in dedicated BED trials
- **N-acetylcysteine (NAC):** Glutamate modulator; small positive pilot data for BED
