# Proton

## Overview

The proton (symbol p⁺ or H⁺) is a positively charged subatomic particle and the nucleus of the hydrogen atom. Its charge is +1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ coulombs and its mass is 1.007276 atomic mass units (u), approximately 1836 times greater than the electron. Unlike the massless electron, the proton is a **composite particle** made of three quarks (two up quarks and one down quark) bound by the strong nuclear force via gluon exchange — a structure confirmed by deep inelastic scattering experiments at SLAC in the late 1960s.

In biology, the proton functions not as a quark composite but as **H⁺** — a hydrogen ion that is continuously transferred between molecules (acids and bases), shuttled across membranes (the chemiosmotic mechanism), and used to modulate enzyme activity, protein conformation, and cell signalling via pH. Peter Mitchell's 1961 proposal of chemiosmosis — that proton gradients, not chemical intermediates, couple the electron transport chain to ATP synthesis — was initially controversial but ultimately earned him the 1978 Nobel Prize in Chemistry [^mitchell-1961-chemiosmosis]. Paul Boyer's biochemical dissection of the catalytic mechanism of ATP synthase, awarded the 1997 Nobel Prize in Chemistry jointly with John Walker, completed the molecular picture [^boyer-1997-atp-synthase].

The proton is also the unit of pH: pH = −log₁₀[H⁺]. The cytosolic pH (~7.2) and mitochondrial matrix pH (~8.0) are tightly regulated because enzyme active sites, protein–protein interfaces, and nucleic acid structures are all sensitive to [H⁺] at the nanomolar to micromolar level.

## Structure

### Subatomic Composition

| Property | Value |
|:---|:---|
| Symbol | p⁺, H⁺ |
| Charge | +1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C |
| Mass | 1.672 × 10⁻²⁷ kg = 1.007276 u |
| Composition | uud quarks (2 up, 1 down) |
| Spin | 1/2 (fermion) |
| Diameter | ~0.85 fm (femtometres) |

In biological contexts, the naked proton (bare H⁺) is never present in aqueous solution. Instead, H⁺ is solvated as **H₃O⁺ (hydronium ion)**, and proton transfer occurs via the **Grotthuss mechanism**: a proton hops along a chain of hydrogen-bonded water molecules, effectively conducting charge through the water lattice 10× faster than other ions by mass diffusion. This mechanism is critical for the rapid conduction of protons through the F₀ channel of ATP synthase and through bacteriorhodopsin.

### Proton in the Hydrogen Atom

In the hydrogen atom, the proton is the nucleus around which a single 1s electron is distributed. The proton's charge (+1) binds the electron (−1) via the Coulomb force, setting the ionization energy of hydrogen at 13.6 eV. When hydrogen forms covalent bonds with carbon, nitrogen, or oxygen, the electron density shifts toward the more electronegative partner (Pauling electronegativities: O = 3.44, N = 3.04, C = 2.55, H = 2.20), creating the partial positive charge (δ+) on hydrogen that underlies **hydrogen bond** donor capacity. Hydrogen bonds — N–H···O, O–H···N, O–H···O — are 5–30 kJ/mol weak but collectively determine protein secondary structure (α-helices, β-sheets), DNA base pairing (Watson-Crick AT and GC), RNA folding, and enzyme–substrate specificity.

## Function

### Acid–Base Chemistry and pH

Proton transfer (Brønsted–Lowry acid–base chemistry) underlies virtually all enzyme-catalysed reactions. Serine proteases (trypsin, chymotrypsin, thrombin) use a catalytic triad in which histidine acts as a general base to abstract a proton from serine's hydroxyl, activating it as a nucleophile. Aspartyl proteases (HIV-1 protease, renin, pepsin) position two aspartate residues to coordinate a catalytic water molecule, with one Asp acting as proton donor and one as acceptor.

The **pKₐ** of an ionisable group is the pH at which 50% is protonated. Histidine (pKₐ ~6.0–6.5) is the only amino acid that titrates near physiological pH, making it indispensable for proton relay at enzyme active sites. Small shifts in intracellular pH (±0.1–0.3 units) during metabolic stress or ischaemia can alter the protonation state of key histidines, modulating enzyme activity and, in cardiomyocytes, reducing myofilament Ca²⁺ sensitivity (the basis of acidosis-induced contractile failure).

### The Proton-Motive Force and Oxidative Phosphorylation

Mitchell's chemiosmotic hypothesis describes how the **proton-motive force (pmf)** couples the exergonic electron transport chain to endergonic ATP synthesis [^mitchell-1961-chemiosmosis]:

**pmf = Δψ + ΔpH·(RT/F·ln 10)**

In mammalian mitochondria at 37°C:
- **Δψ (membrane potential):** −150 to −180 mV (matrix negative)
- **ΔpH:** ~0.5–1.0 units (matrix more alkaline; pH ~8.0 vs. intermembrane space pH ~7.0–7.2)
- **Total pmf:** ~200–220 mV equivalent

**Generation of the gradient — the electron transport chain:**

| Complex | Reaction | Protons pumped |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Complex I (NADH-ubiquinone oxidoreductase) | NADH → NAD⁺ + electrons | 4H⁺/2e⁻ |
| Complex II (succinate dehydrogenase) | FADH₂ → FAD; electrons to CoQ | 0 (no proton pumping) |
| Complex III (cytochrome bc₁) | Ubiquinol → ubiquinone; e⁻ to cytochrome c | 4H⁺/2e⁻ (Q cycle) |
| Complex IV (cytochrome c oxidase) | Cytochrome c → O₂ + H₂O | 2H⁺/2e⁻ (plus consumption of 4H⁺ for water formation) |

**F₀F₁-ATP synthase — the molecular turbine [^boyer-1997-atp-synthase]:**

The F₀ sector (embedded in the inner mitochondrial membrane) contains a ring of 10–15 c subunits that rotate as protons flow through the interface between the c-ring and the a subunit, following the electrochemical gradient. Each complete 360° rotation is driven by 10–15 proton translocations and produces 3 ATP molecules (one per β subunit in the F₁ head) via Boyer's **binding-change mechanism**:

1. **Open (O) conformation:** ADP + Pᵢ bind loosely.
2. **Tight (T) conformation:** ADP + Pᵢ condense spontaneously to ATP (thermodynamically driven by the binding energy).
3. **Loose (L) conformation:** ATP is released as conformational energy from proton flow drives the next rotation step.

The stoichiometry depends on the c-ring size. In humans: ~10 protons per ATP, with ~2.5 ATP/NADH and ~1.5 ATP/FADH₂. Per molecule of glucose, oxidative phosphorylation yields approximately 28 mol ATP (with ~4 from substrate-level phosphorylation in glycolysis and the TCA cycle), giving a total of ~30–32 mol ATP/mol glucose.

### Proton Buffering and Bicarbonate

In blood and tissues, [H⁺] is buffered primarily by the bicarbonate system:

**CO₂ + H₂O ⇌ H₂CO₃ ⇌ H⁺ + HCO₃⁻** (pKₐ = 6.1)

The effective pKₐ in blood is 6.1, yet blood pH is 7.4 — the system operates far from its pKₐ, with the CO₂ tension maintained by respiration. The Henderson-Hasselbalch equation:

**pH = 6.1 + log([HCO₃⁻] / [CO₂])**

In normal blood: pH = 6.1 + log(24 mmol/L / 1.2 mmol/L) = 6.1 + log(20) = 7.4.

Proton accumulation (acidaemia, pH < 7.35) or depletion (alkalaemia, pH > 7.45) constitutes acid–base disorders with major physiological consequences.

### Proton Gradients Beyond Mitochondria

- **Lysosomal acidification:** Vacuolar H⁺-ATPase (V-ATPase) pumps protons into lysosomes, lowering pH to ~4.5–5.0, activating cathepsins (acid hydrolases) for intracellular digestion and autophagy.
- **Gastric acid secretion:** Parietal cells use H⁺/K⁺-ATPase to secrete HCl (pH ~1.5), activating pepsinogen to pepsin and sterilizing the stomach.
- **Bone resorption:** Osteoclasts seal a resorption lacuna against the bone surface and acidify it using V-ATPase to dissolve hydroxyapatite.
- **Bacterial pathogenesis:** Many bacterial toxins (diphtheria toxin, anthrax toxin) exploit endosomal acidification to trigger membrane insertion and translocation of their catalytic domains.

## Connections

- **Part-of** → [Hydrogen](../../02-atomic/hydrogen/README.md): The proton is the sole nucleus of the hydrogen atom. Every free H⁺ in solution was once (and will again become) bound to an electron in a hydrogen atom or covalent bond.

- **Modulates** → [ATP](../../03-molecular/atp/README.md): The proton gradient — built by the electron transport chain and quantified as the proton-motive force — drives F₀F₁-ATP synthase to phosphorylate ADP to ATP, producing ~28 mol ATP per mol glucose via oxidative phosphorylation.

## Pathology

| Condition | Proton mechanism |
|:---|:---|
| **Ischaemia / myocardial infarction** | Anaerobic glycolysis produces lactic acid; intracellular acidosis (pH ↓ to ~6.5) inhibits Na⁺/H⁺ exchanger reversal, causing cytosolic Na⁺ overload → NCX runs in reverse → Ca²⁺ overload → hypercontracture and cell death |
| **Metabolic acidosis** | Accumulation of H⁺ (diabetic ketoacidosis, lactic acidosis, renal tubular acidosis); compensatory hyperventilation (Kussmaul breathing) lowers pCO₂ |
| **Mitochondrial disease** | Complex I/III/IV mutations impair proton pumping; pmf falls; ATP synthesis insufficient for high-energy tissues (brain, heart, muscle) → encephalomyopathy |
| **Cancer — Warburg effect** | Many tumour cells produce lactic acid despite adequate O₂ (aerobic glycolysis); extracellular acidification (pH ~6.7–6.9) promotes invasion, immune evasion, and chemotherapy resistance |
| **GERD / peptic ulcer** | Excess gastric H⁺ (H⁺/K⁺-ATPase) erodes oesophagus or duodenal mucosa; treated by proton pump inhibitors (omeprazole, pantoprazole) |

## Open Questions

- **Proton conduction in ATP synthase:** The precise path of individual proton steps through the a–c subunit interface is not fully resolved by current cryo-EM structures.
- **Non-canonical proton gradients:** Mitochondria also use the pmf to drive protein import via TIM23, Ca²⁺ uptake via the mitochondrial Ca²⁺ uniporter, and heat production in brown adipose tissue via UCP1. The relative apportionment of pmf to these consumers under different physiological states is incompletely quantified.
- **Proton leak:** A fraction of protons re-enter the matrix without passing through ATP synthase (proton leak), generating heat rather than ATP. The proteins responsible (ANT, UCP1 in adipocytes, SLC25 family) and their regulation are active research areas.
