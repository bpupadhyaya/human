# Natural Killer Cell

## Overview

Natural Killer (NK) cells are bone-marrow-derived innate lymphocytes that provide rapid, antigen-independent cytotoxic surveillance against virus-infected, transformed, and cellular-stress-bearing cells. Discovered in 1975 by Kiessling et al. and Herberman et al. (independently), NK cells were named for their ability to kill tumour cell lines spontaneously in vitro without prior immunisation — a property that distinguished them from cytotoxic T lymphocytes, which require MHC-I-restricted antigen presentation.

NK cells constitute approximately 5–15% of circulating peripheral blood lymphocytes (~150–500 million cells/adult) and are enriched in the liver (30–50% of hepatic lymphocytes), spleen, lung, uterine decidua, and bone marrow. They are phenotypically defined as CD3⁻ CD19⁻ CD56⁺ lymphocytes; the CD56ᵈⁱᵐ CD16⁺ subset (~90% of blood NK cells) is cytotoxically dominant, while CD56ᵇʳⁱᵍʰᵗ CD16⁻/ˡᵒʷ NK cells (~10%) are the primary IFN-γ secretors and predominate in secondary lymphoid organs.

Unlike T and B cells, NK cells lack antigen-specific receptors generated by V(D)J recombination. Their target discrimination relies instead on the integration of activating and inhibitory signals from an array of germline-encoded receptors, embodying the "missing-self" hypothesis formulated by Klas Kärre: cells that lose MHC-I expression (as many tumour and virally-infected cells do) become NK-cell targets because inhibitory KIR receptors lose their MHC-I ligands.

## Structure

**Size and morphology.** NK cells are large granular lymphocytes (LGLs), 10–15 µm in diameter, characterised by a kidney-shaped or bilobed nucleus and abundant cytoplasm densely packed with azurophilic granules. The granules are specialised secretory lysosomes containing pre-formed perforin, granzymes A and B, granulysin, and LAMP-1 (CD107a, which traffics to the surface upon degranulation).

**Cytoskeletal polarity.** Upon target recognition, NK cells rapidly polarise: the microtubule-organising centre (MTOC) reorients toward the immune synapse, directing lytic granule secretion focally at the target cell interface. This polarity is essential for killing the target without collateral damage to bystander cells.

**Receptor repertoire.** NK cells express an extensive array of activating and inhibitory receptors:

- *Inhibitory:* Killer-cell immunoglobulin-like receptors (KIRs: 2DL1, 2DL2/3, 3DL1/2 — recognize HLA-A, -B, -C); CD94/NKG2A heterodimer (recognises HLA-E); LILRB1 (ILT2; broad HLA-I specificity); TIGIT (engages PVR/CD155).
- *Activating:* NKG2D (recognises stress ligands MICA, MICB, ULBP1–6); NKp46, NKp30, NKp44 (natural cytotoxicity receptors, NCRs; viral haemagglutinins, tumour ligands); CD16 (FcγRIII; mediates antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity, ADCC); DNAM-1 (CD226; binds PVR/CD155 and CD112/nectin-2); 2B4 (CD244; binds CD48).
- *Co-stimulatory:* LFA-1, NKG2C, NKp80, CRACC (CS1/SLAMF7).

**Signal integration.** Target killing depends on the net balance of activating minus inhibitory signals delivered through the immune synapse. Inhibitory KIRs and NKG2A recruit SHP-1 and SHP-2 phosphatases that dephosphorylate activating signalling intermediates. Activating receptors signal via ITAM-containing adaptors (DAP12, FcRγ, CD3ζ) and DAP10, activating Syk/ZAP-70, PI3K, and PLCγ → Ca²⁺ flux → exocytosis.

## Function

**Direct cytotoxicity — perforin-granzyme pathway.** NK cells recognise a target → form the immune synapse → polarise lytic granules → exocytose perforin + granzymes into the synaptic cleft. Perforin monomers (67 kDa, homologous to complement C9) oligomerise and insert into the target plasma membrane in a Ca²⁺-dependent manner, forming pores (16 nm diameter). Granzyme B enters through these pores (or via mannose-6-phosphate receptor-mediated endocytosis) → cleaves caspase-3 and Bid → rapid apoptosis (~2 hours). Granzyme A activates a caspase-independent pathway via SETDB1 nucleosomal complex disruption. NK cells can sequentially kill multiple targets (serial killing), retracting and reforming a new synapse within ~20 minutes.

**FasL-Fas apoptosis.** NK cells express Fas ligand (FasL/CD95L) and TRAIL; engagement of Fas (CD95) or TRAIL-R1/R2 on targets activates caspase-8 → caspase-3 → apoptosis. This pathway is particularly important against tumour cells that have upregulated death receptors.

**Antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC).** CD16 (FcγRIIIA) on cytotoxic NK cells binds the Fc region of IgG antibodies coating target cells → activates ITAM → perforin-granzyme degranulation. ADCC is the mechanism of action of therapeutic antibodies (e.g., trastuzumab, rituximab) and is a key effector mechanism in antibody-mediated antiviral immunity.

**Cytokine production.** CD56ᵇʳⁱᵍʰᵗ NK cells are major innate sources of IFN-γ (activated by IL-12 + IL-18 from macrophages/DCs) and TNF-α. NK-cell IFN-γ is an early innate signal that: (1) classically activates macrophages → enhanced microbicidal capacity; (2) drives Th1 polarisation of CD4⁺ T cells; (3) upregulates MHC-I on surrounding cells; (4) enhances DC maturation. NK cells also produce GM-CSF, MIP-1α/β, CCL5 (RANTES), XCL1, and FGF.

**NK-DC crosstalk.** NK cells sculpt the adaptive immune response through reciprocal interactions with dendritic cells: immature DCs are killed by NK cells (editing the APC pool, eliminating tolerogenic DCs); mature/activated DCs are spared and instead activate NK cells via contact-dependent signals and IL-12/IL-18. This NK-mediated DC editing shapes the quality and magnitude of adaptive immunity.

## Lifecycle

**Development.** NK cells arise from common lymphoid progenitors (CLPs) in the bone marrow via a stepwise maturation: CLP → NK precursor (NKP) → immature NK (iNK) → mature NK (mNK). Key transcription factors: Id2 (essential master regulator), Eomes (cytotoxic differentiation), T-bet (CD56ᵈⁱᵐ maturation), Tox (tissue residency), E4BP4/NFIL3. GATA-3 and TOX2 regulate tissue-resident NK lineages. IL-15 (trans-presented by DCs via IL-15Rα) is the non-redundant survival and maturation cytokine; IL-7 supports early progenitors.

**Peripheral maturation.** In blood, CD56ᵇʳⁱᵍʰᵗ NK cells are considered less mature and may give rise to CD56ᵈⁱᵐ NK cells; the precise lineage relationship remains debated. Peripheral maturation involves KIR acquisition via probabilistic expression of individual KIR genes (combinatorial diversity), licensing (NK cells become fully functional only if they express at least one KIR recognising a self-MHC-I ligand), and terminal maturation (CD57 expression, loss of NKG2A, acquisition of KLRG1).

**Tissue residency.** A subset of NK cells termed tissue-resident NK (trNK) cells occupy specific niches — uterine decidua (dNK cells, essential for spiral artery remodelling and placentation), liver (CXCR6⁺ liver-resident NK), lung — with distinct transcriptional identities, lower cytotoxic capacity, and tissue-specific functions.

**Memory NK cells.** Under cytomegalovirus (CMV) infection, NKG2C⁺ NK cells clonally expand, contract, and persist as long-lived memory-like NK cells with epigenetic imprints (FcRγ silencing, elevated ETS1/ZEB2). These virus-specific memory NK cells contribute to enhanced protective responses upon re-infection, blurring the classical innate/adaptive boundary.

**Lifespan.** Circulating NK cells have a half-life of ~7–10 days in blood. Tissue-resident NK cells are substantially longer-lived (weeks to months); uterine NK cells are tissue-resident for the duration of pregnancy.

## Connections

- **Upstream activators:** IL-15 (survival/maturation, trans-presented by DCs); IL-12 + IL-18 (synergistic IFN-γ induction); IL-2 (expansion); IFN-α/β (type I IFN priming during viral infection); surface ligands: MICA/B, ULBPs (NKG2D activation), PVR (DNAM-1).
- **Downstream targets:** Virus-infected cells (e.g., CMV, influenza, HIV); tumour cells (especially MHC-I-low); activated DCs (quality control); parasite-infected cells (e.g., Plasmodium-infected erythrocytes via NKp30); antibody-coated cells (ADCC).
- **Inhibitory checkpoints:** PD-1 (tumour microenvironment exhaustion); TIM-3; TIGIT; NKG2A (HLA-E); KIR2DL1/2/3 (HLA-C).
- **Clinical significance:** NK cell deficiencies → recurrent severe herpesvirus infections; impaired NK cell function in cancer (immune evasion by MHC-I upregulation or NKG2D ligand shedding); therapeutic NK cells (allogeneic NK infusions, CAR-NK cells in clinical trials); reproductive failure (inadequate uterine NK cell function in recurrent miscarriage).
