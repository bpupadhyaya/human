Kidney

Paired, bean-shaped retroperitoneal organs (~150 g each, ~11×6×3 cm) at vertebral levels T12–L3. Core functions: plasma filtration (GFR ~125 mL/min → 180 L/day filtered, 99% reabsorbed as ~1.5 L urine), tubular reabsorption and secretion, acid-base homeostasis, and endocrine roles (EPO for erythropoiesis, renin for RAAS blood pressure control, calcitriol for calcium homeostasis). Central to cardiorenal physiology — RAAS links renal perfusion directly to systemic blood pressure. Leading causes of kidney failure: diabetic nephropathy, hypertension, glomerulonephritis.

Overview

The kidneys are the primary regulators of body fluid composition, volume, and acid-base balance. At a normal GFR of 125 mL/min, the two kidneys filter 180 liters of plasma per day — more than 30 times the total blood volume. Yet only about 1.5 liters of urine is produced, meaning 99% of the filtrate is reabsorbed with exquisite selectivity by the tubular system.

Beyond filtration, the kidneys act as an endocrine organ: erythropoietin (EPO) drives red blood cell production; renin activates the RAAS to regulate blood pressure; calcitriol (active vitamin D) controls calcium and phosphate homeostasis. The right kidney sits 1–2 cm lower than the left (displaced by the liver). Each kidney receives ~20–25% of cardiac output (~1.1 L/min combined).

Each renal pyramid apex (renal papilla) drains into a minor calyx. A renal column (of Bertin) is cortical tissue extending between pyramids, carrying arcuate and interlobar vessels. Each pyramid plus its associated cortex constitutes one renal lobe.

The Nephron

The nephron is the structural and functional unit of the kidney. Each kidney contains ~0.7–1.4 million nephrons (average ~1 million). The nephron's five sequential segments perform distinct physiological tasks:

  Glomerulus (Bowman's capsule)
       │  filtration ~125 mL/min
       ▼
  Proximal Convoluted Tubule (PCT)
       │  60–70% Na⁺/H₂O reabsorbed; 100% glucose & amino acids
       │  SGLT1/2, NHE3, aquaporin-1
       ▼
  Loop of Henle
       │  ─ Descending limb: water-permeable (AQP1), salt-impermeable
       │    → tubular fluid concentrates
       │  ─ Thick ascending limb: NKCC2 pumps NaCl out (no water)
       │    → builds medullary osmotic gradient (~1200 mOsm/kg at papilla)
       ▼
  Distal Convoluted Tubule (DCT)
       │  ~5% NaCl; NCC (thiazide target); Ca²⁺ via TRPV5
       │  aldosterone target
       ▼
  Collecting Duct
       │  ADH (vasopressin) inserts AQP2 → variable water reabsorption
       │  ENaC (aldosterone target) → Na⁺ reabsorption
       ▼
  Urine → renal pelvis → ureter → bladder

Cortical nephrons (85%) have short loops extending only into outer medulla. Juxtamedullary nephrons (15%) have long loops reaching deep medulla — essential for maximal urine concentration. The parallel vasa recta capillaries act as countercurrent exchangers, preserving the medullary osmotic gradient without washing it out.

Glomerular Filtration

At the glomerulus, hydrostatic pressure (~60 mmHg) drives plasma ultrafiltration across a three-layer barrier:

Filtration barrier layers

(1) Fenestrated endothelium — charged glycocalyx excludes proteins; (2) Glomerular basement membrane (GBM) — collagen IV α3/α4/α5 chains, laminin, heparan sulfate; (3) Podocyte slit diaphragm — nephrin, podocin proteins. Together: protein-free ultrafiltrate of plasma.

GFR determinants

Filtration coefficient Kf (permeability × surface area); afferent arteriole tone (↑ constriction → ↓ GFR); efferent arteriole tone (↑ constriction → ↑ GFR acutely, then ↓ with severe constriction); oncotic pressure of plasma (↑ protein → ↓ GFR).

GFR = Kf × (P_GC − P_BS − π_GC)
P_GC = glomerular capillary hydrostatic pressure; P_BS = Bowman's space pressure; π_GC = glomerular capillary oncotic pressure. Normal GFR ≈ 125 mL/min (males), ~110 mL/min (females)

Acid-Base Homeostasis

The kidney is the only organ that can regenerate HCO₃⁻ and vary net acid excretion on a timescale of hours to days — the dominant long-term buffer system (lungs handle minute-to-minute):

HCO₃⁻ reclamation (PCT)

NHE3 secretes H⁺ into lumen → reacts with filtered HCO₃⁻ → H₂CO₃ → CO₂ + H₂O (carbonic anhydrase IV on luminal brush border) → CO₂ diffuses into cell → regenerated as intracellular HCO₃⁻ returned to blood. Reclaims 85% of filtered bicarbonate.

Net acid excretion (collecting duct)

α-intercalated cells: H⁺-ATPase and H⁺/K⁺-ATPase secrete H⁺ → new HCO₃⁻ returned to blood; titratable acid (HPO₄²⁻ + H⁺ → H₂PO₄⁻) and ammonium (NH₄⁺ from glutamine catabolism in PCT) are the two main H⁺ buffers excreted. β-intercalated cells correct alkalosis by secreting HCO₃⁻.

RAAS — Renin–Angiotensin–Aldosterone System

The RAAS is the primary hormonal loop linking renal perfusion to systemic blood pressure — the kidney's most powerful cardiovascular signal:

  Low renal perfusion pressure
  OR low NaCl at macula densa
  OR β1-adrenergic stimulation
           │
           ▼
  JG cells (afferent arteriole) release RENIN
           │
           ▼
  Renin cleaves angiotensinogen (liver)
    → Angiotensin I (inactive decapeptide)
           │
           ▼
  ACE (lung endothelium) converts Ang I
    → Angiotensin II (octapeptide)
           │
           ├──► AT1R on VSMCs: vasoconstriction → BP ↑
           │
           ├──► Adrenal cortex: ALDOSTERONE release
           │    → ENaC upregulation in collecting duct
           │    → Na⁺/H₂O retention → volume ↑ → BP ↑
           │
           └──► Efferent arteriole constriction
                → ↑ intraglomerular pressure → GFR maintained

ACE inhibitors interrupt this cascade at ACE, reducing Ang II, dilating efferent arterioles, lowering intraglomerular pressure, and slowing CKD progression in diabetic and non-diabetic nephropathy. ARBs block AT1R directly. Both are first-line renoprotective agents.

Pathology

Acute Kidney Injury (AKI)

KDIGO criteria: serum creatinine rise ≥0.3 mg/dL within 48 h, ≥1.5× baseline within 7 days, or urine output <0.5 mL/kg/h for ≥6 h. Staged 1–3 by severity. Major causes: prerenal (hypoperfusion), intrinsic (acute tubular necrosis, glomerulonephritis, AIN, rhabdomyolysis), postrenal (obstruction). COVID-19 AKI occurs in ~25–30% of ICU patients via direct ACE2-mediated tubular infection, cytokine storm, thrombotic microangiopathy, and collapsing glomerulopathy.

Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD)

GFR <60 mL/min/1.73 m² or markers of kidney damage for ≥3 months. KDIGO G-staging: G1 (≥90, normal/high) → G2 (60–89, mildly decreased) → G3a (45–59) → G3b (30–44) → G4 (15–29, severely decreased) → G5 (<15, kidney failure/ESRD requiring dialysis or transplant). Leading causes worldwide: diabetic nephropathy (40%), hypertensive nephrosclerosis (25%), glomerulonephritis (15%). CKD independently raises cardiovascular mortality.

Autosomal Dominant Polycystic Kidney Disease (ADPKD)

PKD1 (chr. 16, ~85%) or PKD2 (chr. 4, ~15%) mutations disrupt polycystin-1/2 complex. Bilateral cyst growth progressively replaces functional parenchyma — median ESRD age 54 (PKD1) vs. 74 (PKD2). Prevalence ~1:400–1000; most common life-threatening monogenic disease. Tolvaptan (V2R antagonist) slows cyst growth. ARPKD (PKHD1) presents in neonates/infants with pulmonary hypoplasia.

Nephrotic syndrome

Triad: proteinuria (>3.5 g/day), hypoalbuminemia, edema. Causes: minimal change disease (children, steroid-responsive; podocyte effacement without structural change), focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS; podocyte loss + scarring; primary or secondary to obesity/HIV/sickle cell), membranous nephropathy (anti-PLA2R antibodies in 70% of primary cases; subepithelial immune complex deposition). Membranous nephropathy is the leading cause of nephrotic syndrome in adults.

Renal cell carcinoma (RCC)

Clear cell RCC (~75%) driven by VHL tumor suppressor loss (chr. 3p); disrupts HIF degradation → VEGF/PDGF overexpression → angiogenesis. Papillary (types 1/2, ~15%) and chromophobe (~5%) subtypes. Often found incidentally on imaging. Susceptible to VEGF/mTOR targeted therapies and immune checkpoint inhibitors (PD-1/PD-L1). 5-year survival ~75% for localized disease, ~12% for metastatic.

Blood Supply and the Vasa Recta

The kidney receives ~20–25% of cardiac output via a hierarchical arterial tree:

Renal artery → segmental → interlobar → arcuate → interlobular (cortical radiate) arteries → afferent arterioles → glomerular capillaries → efferent arterioles → peritubular capillaries (cortex) / vasa recta (medulla)
The efferent arteriole creates a unique portal arrangement — two capillary beds in series. ACE inhibitors dilate the efferent arteriole, lowering intraglomerular pressure.

The vasa recta are hairpin-shaped capillaries descending into the medulla alongside the loops of Henle. Their countercurrent flow pattern preserves the medullary osmotic gradient by exchanging solutes and water with the interstitium without washing out the gradient — essential for urine concentration up to 1200 mOsm/kg.

Juxtaglomerular Apparatus

The JGA sits at the vascular pole of each glomerulus and is the anatomical locus of RAAS initiation and tubuloglomerular feedback:

JG (granular) cells

Modified smooth muscle cells in the afferent arteriole wall. Synthesize, store, and secrete renin in response to: (1) decreased afferent arteriole stretch (low BP); (2) reduced NaCl delivery to macula densa; (3) β1-adrenergic stimulation.

Macula densa

Specialized NaCl-sensing epithelial cells in the distal nephron wall adjacent to the JGA. When tubular NaCl is low → signal to JG cells → ↑ renin release. When NaCl is high → tubuloglomerular feedback → afferent arteriole constriction → ↓ GFR (auto-regulation).
