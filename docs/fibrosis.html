Fibrosis — Pathological Scarring

Fibrosis is the pathological outcome of dysregulated wound healing — excess deposition of extracellular matrix (collagen I/III, fibronectin) and architectural distortion driven by activated myofibroblasts, predominantly under TGF-β1 control. It affects lung (IPF), liver (cirrhosis), heart, kidney, and skin, and underlies an estimated 45% of all deaths in developed countries.

Key driver: TGF-β1 / SMAD2/3 Scale: L04 Cellular / Molecular Organs: Lung, Liver, Heart, Kidney, Skin Type: Pathological process Reviewed: 2026-06-05

Definition and Concept

Fibrosis refers to the excess accumulation of fibrous connective tissue — predominantly type I and type III collagens, fibronectin, laminin, and proteoglycans — within an organ as a response to sustained injury or chronic inflammation. In normal wound healing, injured tissue is temporarily replaced by a provisional fibrin/fibronectin matrix, invaded by myofibroblasts that contract and synthesize collagen, and then remodeled back to near-normal architecture as myofibroblasts undergo apoptosis. Fibrosis occurs when this resolution phase fails — myofibroblasts persist, collagen accumulates, and architectural distortion impairs organ function.

Fibrosis is not a single disease but a pathological endpoint reached by diverse organs through shared molecular mechanisms. Estimates suggest fibrotic diseases collectively account for approximately 45% of all deaths in the developed world (Wynn and Ramalingam, 2012), including:

Idiopathic Pulmonary Fibrosis (IPF)

IPF is the archetypal organ fibrosis — the most severe and best-characterized fibrotic lung disease. It is defined as a specific form of chronic, progressive, fibrosing interstitial pneumonia of unknown cause that occurs primarily in older adults, with the histological and/or radiological pattern of usual interstitial pneumonia (UIP).

Clinical and Radiological Features

IPF affects predominantly males >65 years of age, with risk factors including cigarette smoking (relative risk ~2), gastroesophageal reflux disease, and genetic susceptibility. The MUC5B promoter variant rs35705950 (common allele frequency ~11% in general population, ~38% in IPF patients) is the strongest genetic risk factor for IPF; it increases MUC5B expression in distal airways, though the mechanism connecting mucin overproduction to fibrosis is incompletely understood. Rare variants in telomere-related genes (TERT, TERC, RTEL1, PARN) are found in ~20% of familial IPF cases and a subset of sporadic IPF — short telomeres → AT2 cell senescence/apoptosis → impaired epithelial renewal.

The UIP pattern on HRCT is characterized by:

  • Honeycombing: Clustered cystic airspaces (3–10 mm) in a subpleural, basal-predominant distribution — the radiological hallmark that allows confident UIP diagnosis without surgical biopsy
  • Traction bronchiectasis / bronchiolectasis: Irregular bronchial dilation due to surrounding fibrotic distortion
  • Reticulation: Irregular interstitial thickening in basal and peripheral zones
  • Fibroblastic foci: On histology — small aggregates of actively proliferating myofibroblasts embedded in loose connective tissue at the leading edge of advancing fibrosis; their density correlates with disease progression rate

IPF clinical course: median survival without treatment is 2–3 years from diagnosis (range 1–5+ years); ~10–15% experience acute exacerbations (sudden deterioration mimicking ARDS superimposed on UIP) with 50% short-term mortality. Lung function tests show a restrictive pattern: ↓TLC, ↓FVC, ↓DLCO (reduced diffusing capacity — most sensitive to early IPF).

Incidence and epidemiology: IPF incidence is 3–9 per 100,000 per year in Europe; higher in North America (14 per 100,000). Overall prevalence ~50/100,000. Incidence is increasing — likely due to aging populations and improved recognition. Annual worldwide mortality ~40,000 in the USA alone.

Pathophysiology: Aberrant Repair Hypothesis

The current consensus model for IPF pathogenesis is the "aberrant alveolar repair" hypothesis, which centers on recurrent microinjury to the alveolar epithelium (particularly AT2 cells) rather than primary inflammation as the initiating event:

Initiating Injury and Epithelial Stress

Repetitive, subclinical injury to AT2 cells (from microaspiration, viral infections, oxidative stress, aging) → ER stress (unfolded protein response, UPR) → activation of PERK/IRE1α/ATF6 branches → ATF4/CHOP → AT2 apoptosis. ER stress in AT2 cells → release of TGF-β1 from its latent complex → activation of neighboring fibroblasts. Senescent AT2 cells (p21/p53/p16 activation → SASP — senescence-associated secretory phenotype) constitutively secrete TGF-β1, MMP-3, and CXCL1 — amplifying fibroblast activation even in the absence of ongoing injury.

TGF-β1 — Central Fibrogenic Mediator

TGF-β1 is the master regulator of fibrosis across all organs. In IPF specifically:

  • Latent TGF-β1 is sequestered in the ECM by LTBP-1/3; activation requires integrin αvβ6 (expressed on injured alveolar epithelial cells) or MMP2/9 or thrombospondin-1
  • Active TGF-β1 binds TGF-βRII → transphosphorylates TGF-βRI/ALK5 → SMAD2/3 phosphorylation → SMAD2/3 + SMAD4 heterotrimer → nuclear translocation → transcription of Col1A1, Col3A1, α-SMA (ACTA2), CTGF, fibronectin-1
  • SMAD7 provides negative feedback by targeting R-SMADs for SMURF1/2-mediated proteasomal degradation; in IPF, SMAD7 expression is reduced, removing this brake

Fibroblast and Myofibroblast Activation

Resident lung fibroblasts, upon TGF-β1/PDGF stimulation, acquire the myofibroblast phenotype: expression of α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA, incorporated into stress fibers), increased contractility, excessive collagen I/III production, and resistance to apoptosis. Myofibroblast origins are debated — resident fibroblasts (primary), bone marrow-derived fibrocytes (CD45+/Col1+, ~5% of myofibroblasts), and possibly epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) of AT2 cells (gaining vimentin/N-cadherin/α-SMA while losing E-cadherin) — though the contribution of EMT in vivo remains contested. Once activated, myofibroblasts are self-sustaining via autocrine TGF-β1 loops.

Mechanosignaling: YAP/TAZ and Matrix Stiffness

Fibrotic ECM is mechanically stiff — lung stiffness increases from ~1–2 kPa (normal) to >10–20 kPa in advanced IPF. This stiffness is sensed by fibroblasts via integrin-FAK-Rho/ROCK mechanosensing → activation of YAP (Yes-associated protein) and TAZ (transcriptional coactivator with PDZ-binding motif), which are Hippo pathway effectors. YAP/TAZ translocate to the nucleus on stiff matrices → transcriptional activation of pro-fibrotic genes (CTGF, CYR61, COL1A1) and inhibition of AT2 differentiation. This creates a positive feedback loop: fibrosis → stiffness → YAP/TAZ → more fibrosis — explaining why IPF is progressive even in the absence of ongoing epithelial injury.

Cellular Senescence and SASP

Senescent cells accumulate in IPF lungs — both AT2 cells (p21+/p16+) and fibroblasts. SASP cytokines secreted by senescent cells (TGF-β1, IL-6, IL-8, MMP-3) paracrinally activate neighboring fibroblasts. The senostatic/senolytic hypothesis in IPF: ABT-737, navitoclax, and dasatinib + quercetin (senolytic combinations) reduce fibrotic burden in bleomycin mouse models; clinical trials ongoing (ClinicalTrials.gov NCT02874989 — dasatinib + quercetin in IPF).

Antifibrotic Pharmacology

Two antifibrotic drugs are approved for IPF: pirfenidone and nintedanib. Both slow but do not halt disease progression; no drug reverses established fibrosis. Additional agents are in late-stage development targeting integrin αvβ6, CTGF, and galectin-3.

Liver Fibrosis and Cirrhosis

Hepatic fibrosis follows the same TGF-β/myofibroblast paradigm but with organ-specific effector cells. The primary myofibroblast in liver fibrosis is the hepatic stellate cell (HSC) — a perisinusoidal cell that stores vitamin A in lipid droplets under quiescent conditions. Upon liver injury (alcohol, NASH, viral hepatitis, cholestasis), Kupffer cells (hepatic macrophages) produce TGF-β1 and PDGF → HSC activation → loss of lipid droplets, acquisition of α-SMA, and massive collagen I/III secretion around sinusoids → sinusoidal constriction → portal hypertension → cirrhosis.

Key differences from pulmonary fibrosis: liver has greater regenerative capacity; early fibrosis (<F3 on METAVIR scale) can regress if the underlying cause is treated (antiviral therapy for HCV → fibrosis regression in 30–40% of patients; abstinence from alcohol → partial regression). F4 (cirrhosis) may be irreversible. Non-invasive fibrosis assessment: FIB-4 index (age × AST / [platelet count × √ALT]), APRI score, transient elastography (FibroScan — liver stiffness kPa).

  • occurs-inLung Alveolus — in IPF, the fibrotic process obliterates alveolar architecture; fibroblastic foci form at the interface between normal and fibrotic lung, destroying the gas-exchange surface
  • late-phase-ofARDS — fibroproliferative and fibrotic phases of DAD are mechanistically identical to early IPF pathogenesis; TGF-β1-driven myofibroblast activation in late ARDS explains the persistent lung stiffness and reduced compliance
  • visualized-atLung Slice — honeycombing and traction bronchiectasis in IPF/UIP pattern are recognizable on CT-equivalent lung slice anatomy; basal and subpleural distribution is anatomically explicable
  • amplified-byNLRP3 Inflammasome — NLRP3-derived IL-1β and IL-18 amplify fibroblast activation via NF-κB and STAT3; NLRP3 is activated in IPF by mtDNA, particulates, and ER stress signals from AT2 cells
  • endpoint-ofCKD — renal interstitial fibrosis and tubular atrophy (IFTA) is the histological correlate and final common pathway of all progressive CKD; TGF-β/SMAD3 drives pericyte-to-myofibroblast transition in the kidney
  • occurs-inSkin — dermal fibrosis (scleroderma, keloid) involves the same TGF-β/α-SMA/collagen I pathway in dermal fibroblasts; scleroderma also has autoimmune components (anti-topoisomerase I, anti-centromere antibodies)
