Liver

Largest internal organ (~1.5 kg), right upper quadrant. Dual blood supply: portal vein (75%) + hepatic artery (25%). Performs >500 functions: glucose/lipid homeostasis, protein synthesis, detoxification, bile secretion. Contains ~100,000 hepatic lobules and is the site of CYP450-mediated drug metabolism, first-pass metabolism, and bilirubin conjugation. Exceptional regenerative capacity after injury.

Overview

The liver is the largest internal organ of the human body and the metabolic hub of virtually every major biochemical pathway. Weighing approximately 1.2–1.6 kg in adults, it occupies the right hypochondriac region and much of the epigastric region of the upper abdomen, tucked beneath the right hemidiaphragm and protected by the lower rib cage.

Two features make the liver anatomically unique among abdominal organs. First, its dual blood supply: the hepatic portal vein (~75% of total flow, nutrient-rich but relatively O₂-poor, arriving from the gastrointestinal tract) and the hepatic artery proper (~25% of flow, O₂-rich from the aorta via the celiac trunk). This portal circulation ensures that absorbed nutrients, drugs, and gut-derived toxins all pass through the liver before entering the systemic circulation — the first-pass effect that enables the liver to buffer and regulate systemic blood chemistry. Second, its exceptional regenerative capacity: after a 70% hepatectomy (the amount donated in living-related transplantation), the remnant restores full functional mass within 4–8 weeks in humans.

Gross Anatomy — Couinaud Segments

The liver is classically described as having 4 lobes (right, left, caudate, quadrate), but the surgical gold standard is the Couinaud classification — 8 independent functional segments based on hepatic venous and portal pedicle territory. Each segment has its own portal pedicle (portal vein branch + hepatic artery branch + bile duct) and drains into a hepatic vein, making them independently resectable.

Porta hepatis ("gate of the liver"): transverse fissure where portal vein, hepatic artery, and common hepatic duct enter/exit. The Pringle maneuver compresses the hepatoduodenal ligament to temporarily stop inflow bleeding during liver surgery.

The dual supply creates a metabolic zonation gradient along the hepatic acinus: Zone 1 (periportal) receives the highest O₂ and nutrient concentrations — site of gluconeogenesis, β-oxidation, amino acid catabolism. Zone 3 (centrilobular/perivenous) is relatively hypoxic — site of glycolysis, lipogenesis, CYP2E1-mediated drug metabolism, and the greatest vulnerability to ischemia and acetaminophen toxicity.

Microscopic Structure

The liver contains approximately 100,000 hepatic lobules — hexagonal units with a central vein surrounded by hepatocyte cords and sinusoids, with portal triads (portal vein branch + hepatic artery branch + bile ductule) at each vertex.

Carbohydrate and Lipid Metabolism

The liver maintains blood glucose within 70–100 mg/dL through opposing processes regulated by insulin and glucagon:

Postprandial (insulin-dominant)

Glucose uptake via GLUT2 → glucokinase phosphorylation → glycogen synthesis. Surplus calories: glycolysis → acetyl-CoA → de novo lipogenesis → VLDL secretion.

Fasted state (glucagon-dominant)

Glycogenolysis → glucose-6-phosphatase (liver/kidney specific) → free glucose. Prolonged fast: gluconeogenesis from lactate, alanine, glycerol; ketogenesis (β-hydroxybutyrate, acetoacetate).

Cholesterol synthesis

Acetyl-CoA → HMG-CoA → (HMG-CoA reductase, HMGCR — statin target) → mevalonate → cholesterol. ~70% of total body cholesterol is synthesized here.

LDL clearance

Hepatic LDL receptors (LDLR) clear ~70% of plasma LDL. Statin-induced ↓intracellular cholesterol → SREBP2 activation → LDLR upregulation → ↓plasma LDL-C.

CYP450 Detoxification — Phase I / II / III

The liver intercepts xenobiotics and metabolic waste before systemic exposure. The three-phase system converts lipophilic compounds into water-soluble excretable metabolites:

Ammonia detoxification: The urea cycle (carbamoyl phosphate synthetase → citrulline → argininosuccinate → arginine → urea + ornithine) converts toxic NH₃ from amino acid catabolism and gut bacteria into non-toxic urea excreted by the kidneys. Hepatic failure → hyperammonemia → hepatic encephalopathy.

Jaundice (bilirubin >2–3 mg/dL → scleral icterus → skin yellowing): causes include unconjugated hyperbilirubinemia (hemolysis, Gilbert syndrome: UGT1A1 polymorphism, neonatal), conjugated hyperbilirubinemia (biliary obstruction: choledocholithiasis, pancreatic cancer, cholangiocarcinoma), or mixed (hepatocellular disease: hepatitis, cirrhosis, DILI).

Pathology

Cirrhosis

End-stage chronic liver disease: replacement of functional parenchyma with fibrotic scar (collagen I/III from activated HSCs) → nodular regenerative architecture → ↑intrahepatic vascular resistance → portal hypertension. Complications: esophageal varices (hemorrhage risk), ascites, hepatorenal syndrome, hepatic encephalopathy, spontaneous bacterial peritonitis (SBP). Child-Pugh and MELD scores predict mortality.

NAFLD / MASLD

Affects ~25% of the global adult population (~1.8 billion people). Spectrum: simple steatosis (≥5% hepatocyte fat, benign) → NASH/MASH (steatosis + ballooning + lobular inflammation) → advanced fibrosis → cirrhosis → HCC. Driven by insulin resistance; pathomechanism: lipotoxicity → ER stress → mitochondrial dysfunction → JNK/NF-κB → Kupffer activation → TGF-β → stellate cell fibrosis.

Hepatocellular Carcinoma (HCC)

Third most common cause of cancer death worldwide. Major risk factors: HBV (viral DNA integration + cirrhosis), HCV cirrhosis, NASH-cirrhosis, aflatoxin (CYP3A4 activation → aflatoxin B1-8,9-epoxide → TP53 R249S mutation), alcohol cirrhosis. Surveillance: AFP + liver ultrasound every 6 months in all cirrhotic patients.

Wilson Disease / Hemochromatosis

Wilson disease (ATP7B mutation): impaired hepatic copper export → copper accumulates in liver (cirrhosis), brain (neuropsychiatric), cornea (Kayser-Fleischer rings). Treated with chelation (penicillamine, trientine) or zinc. Hereditary hemochromatosis (HFE C282Y): iron overload → hepatic fibrosis/cirrhosis, cardiomyopathy, diabetes, hypogonadism. Treated by therapeutic phlebotomy.

Acute Liver Failure (ALF)

Rapid hepatocellular necrosis in a previously healthy liver: coagulopathy (INR >1.5) + encephalopathy within 26 weeks of illness onset. Most common causes: acetaminophen overdose (Western countries, >50% of ALF), viral hepatitis (HBV, HAV, HEV), drug idiosyncrasy, Wilson's disease (acute). High mortality without liver transplantation; bridging with N-acetylcysteine in acetaminophen ALF.

Viral Hepatitis

HAV and HEV: fecal-oral, self-limited, no chronicity. HBV: blood/sexual/perinatal — 5–10% adult chronicity (90% neonates); ~296 million chronic carriers; high HCC risk. HCV: blood-borne, 55–85% chronicity, ~58 million chronic; now curable (>95% SVR with 8–12 weeks direct-acting antivirals). Cirrhosis → HCC at ~1–4%/year.
