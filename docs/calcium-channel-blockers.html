Calcium Channel Blockers

L-type Ca²⁺ channel antagonists — two pharmacologically distinct subclasses that diverge in tissue selectivity, heart rate effects, and the clinical settings where they help or harm.

DHPs target vascular smooth muscle; non-DHPs target the heart. ALLHAT established amlodipine as first-line for hypertension. A decade of trials placed CCBs in angina, atrial fibrillation, and Raynaud's — while defining contraindications with beta-blockers and HFrEF.

Calcium Channel Blockers (CCBs)

Class: L-type Ca²⁺ Channel Antagonist  ·  Antihypertensive  ·  Antianginal  |  Route: Oral (most agents)  |  Status: First-line HTN, stable angina; rate control in AF (non-DHP)

Calcium channel blockers block voltage-gated L-type (Cav1) calcium channels — the main conduit for calcium entry into vascular smooth muscle and cardiomyocytes during depolarisation. Two pharmacologically distinct subclasses exist: dihydropyridines (DHPs: amlodipine, nifedipine) bind a different site on the channel and show overwhelming selectivity for vascular smooth muscle — excellent antihypertensives and antianginals with minimal cardiac rate/contractility effects. Non-DHPs (verapamil, diltiazem) bind the same core channel but with strong affinity for cardiac Cav1.2 and Cav1.3 (SA/AV node channels) — they lower heart rate, reduce AV conduction, and depress contractility, making them useful for rate control in atrial fibrillation and vasospastic angina, but dangerous in HFrEF and when combined with beta-blockers.

Subclass Comparison

Dihydropyridines (DHPs)

Agents: amlodipine, nifedipine, felodipine, lercanidipine, nicardipine, clevidipine (IV)

Tissue selectivity: Predominantly vascular smooth muscle (Cav1.2 VSM). Minimal cardiac effects at therapeutic doses.

HR: Reflex tachycardia (especially short-acting nifedipine) — baroreflex activation from vasodilation

Contractility: Minimal negative inotropy at therapeutic doses

AV conduction: Not significantly affected

Main use: Hypertension, stable angina (vasodilatory), Raynaud's, subarachnoid haemorrhage vasospasm (nimodipine)

Non-Dihydropyridines (Non-DHPs)

Agents: verapamil (phenylalkylamine), diltiazem (benzothiazepine)

Tissue selectivity: Cardiac > vascular smooth muscle (Cav1.2 cardiomyocytes + Cav1.3 SA/AV node)

HR: Negative chronotropy — slow heart rate; useful in rate control for AF

Contractility: Negative inotropy — reduce myocardial contractility; contraindicated in HFrEF

AV conduction: Slow AV node conduction — suppress re-entrant tachycardias; risk of high-degree block with beta-blockers

Main use: AF rate control, SVT, vasospastic (Prinzmetal) angina, hypertension

Mechanism of Action

Cav1 Channels — Molecular Targets

  L-type Ca²⁺ channel (Cav1.x) — voltage-gated, opens on membrane depolarisation
          │
          ├── Cav1.2 — expressed in: vascular smooth muscle, cardiomyocytes
          │   → VSM: Ca²⁺ entry → calmodulin → MLCK → myosin phosphorylation → contraction
          │   → Cardiomyocytes: Ca²⁺ trigger → Ca²⁺-induced Ca²⁺ release → systolic contraction
          │
          └── Cav1.3 — expressed in: SA node, AV node (pacemaker cells)
              → slower activation kinetics → sets heart rate and AV conduction velocity

  DHP binding site: extracellular/transmembrane face of α1-subunit (IIIS5–IIIS6)
  → stabilises inactivated state → channel blocked proportional to depolarisation frequency
  → VASCULAR SELECTIVITY: VSM maintains more depolarised membrane potential than cardiomyocytes
     → DHPs preferentially block VSM channels (more time in inactivated state)

  Non-DHP binding site: intracellular face / inner channel pore region
  → block open-channel state → USE-DEPENDENCE: more active at higher heart rates
  → higher affinity for cardiac channels → CARDIAC SELECTIVITY

  Use-dependence (Non-DHPs especially):
  → channel must open before drug can bind
  → faster heart rates → more channel openings → greater drug effect → greater HR slowing
  → explains why verapamil terminates SVT (rapidly firing AV node cells highly susceptible)

The use-dependence of non-DHPs is clinically important: at higher heart rates (tachyarrhythmias), the AV node fires more often, channels open more frequently, and non-DHP binding increases proportionally — slowing conduction most precisely when rate control is most needed. This is why verapamil and diltiazem are preferred for AF rate control but can produce profound bradycardia in patients with slow baseline rates or when combined with beta-blockers.

  1. DHP → VSM Cav1.2 blockade: Reduces Ca²⁺ entry → ↓smooth muscle contraction → arteriolar vasodilation → ↓systemic vascular resistance → ↓blood pressure and ↓afterload (antianginal)
  2. DHP reflex tachycardia: Vasodilation → baroreflex activation → sympathetic ↑HR and ↑contractility — especially with short-acting nifedipine. Minimised with amlodipine (long half-life, slow onset, gradual vasodilation)
  3. Non-DHP → SA node Cav1.3 blockade: ↓Pacemaker depolarisation rate → ↓heart rate (negative chronotropy)
  4. Non-DHP → AV node Cav1.2/1.3 blockade: Slows conduction velocity through AV node → ↓ventricular rate in AF; terminates AV nodal re-entrant tachycardia (AVNRT)
  5. Non-DHP → myocardial Cav1.2 blockade: Reduces Ca²⁺-triggered SR Ca²⁺ release → ↓systolic contraction (negative inotropy) — hazardous in HFrEF where contractile reserve is already depleted

Evidence Base — Landmark Trials

TrialDrugPopulationKey Result
ALLHAT (2002) Amlodipine vs lisinopril vs chlorthalidone 33,357 hypertensive patients aged ≥55, at least one additional CV risk factor Amlodipine equivalent to chlorthalidone and lisinopril for combined fatal CHD/non-fatal MI. Amlodipine superior in stroke prevention vs lisinopril in Black patients. Established CCBs as first-line for hypertension, particularly in Black patients where ACEi/ARB are less effective.
CAMELOT (2004) Amlodipine 10 mg vs enalapril vs placebo 1,991 patients with CAD, normal BP Amlodipine reduced adverse CV events 31% vs placebo. Enalapril non-significant. IVUS substudy (NORMALISE): amlodipine slowed atherosclerosis progression. Provided evidence for CCBs beyond BP lowering in established CAD.
VALUE (2004) Valsartan vs amlodipine 15,245 high-risk hypertensive patients Similar cardiac outcomes with valsartan and amlodipine overall. Amlodipine achieved faster BP control in early follow-up. Greater BP difference in early months associated with more events in valsartan group — highlighted the importance of early BP control, not just drug choice.
Staessen meta-analysis (2001) CCBs vs other antihypertensives 9 trials, 62,605 patients CCBs equivalent to ACEi, diuretics, and beta-blockers for overall CV outcomes. CCBs superior for stroke prevention; diuretics/beta-blockers superior for HF prevention. Confirmed CCBs as tier-one antihypertensives with specific strengths.

The DHP Choice in 2024: Amlodipine remains the dominant DHP — its very long half-life (~35–50 hours) eliminates the reflex tachycardia and BP variability that plagued short-acting nifedipine, and its once-daily dosing maximises adherence. Lercanidipine offers a useful alternative in patients troubled by amlodipine-associated peripheral oedema — its higher lipophilicity and slower channel kinetics produce similar BP lowering with less oedema. Combining amlodipine with an ACEi or ARB (e.g., perindopril-amlodipine, olmesartan-amlodipine) is increasingly favoured as combination therapy reduces oedema (ACEi/ARBs facilitate venous return) and improves adherence.

Adverse Effects

  • Peripheral oedema (DHPs) — most common — dependent ankle/leg oedema from pre-capillary arteriolar dilation without matched venodilation; not due to fluid retention (no weight gain, no diuretic response). More pronounced with amlodipine than lercanidipine; reduced when combined with ACEi/ARBs (venodilation restores balance). Dose-dependent; problematic in 10–20% at standard doses
  • Reflex tachycardia (short-acting DHPs) — rapid-onset vasodilation triggers baroreflex; nifedipine immediate-release associated with reflex tachycardia and increased adverse outcomes — only use extended-release formulations for chronic HTN/angina
  • Flushing, headache (DHPs) — vasodilatory symptoms; more common at initiation; usually resolve within weeks
  • Constipation (verapamil) — most common non-cardiac verapamil side effect; L-type channels in intestinal smooth muscle blocked → reduced peristalsis. Common in up to 25–40% of patients. Diltiazem causes less constipation than verapamil
  • Gingival hyperplasia — class-wide effect (~3–6% with nifedipine; reported with amlodipine and verapamil); mechanism involves gingival fibroblast proliferation via TGF-β in the presence of reduced Ca²⁺; more common with poor oral hygiene; usually resolves after drug discontinuation
  • Grapefruit interaction (all CCBs) — grapefruit contains furanocoumarins that irreversibly inhibit CYP3A4 in the intestinal wall → markedly increased bioavailability of most CCBs (especially felodipine, nifedipine, amlodipine less so). Can cause hypotension; counsel patients to avoid grapefruit and grapefruit juice
  • Bradycardia / high-degree heart block (non-DHPs) — particularly with verapamil; risk greatly increased with concomitant beta-blockers (absolute contraindication to IV combination), digoxin, or amiodarone

Contraindications

  • Non-DHP + beta-blocker combination: Additive negative chronotropy and inotropy → severe bradycardia, high-degree AV block, haemodynamic collapse. Absolute contraindication for IV administration; use with great caution orally if at all
  • Non-DHPs in HFrEF (EF <40%): Negative inotropy worsens cardiac output in already-compensated HF; associated with increased hospitalisation and mortality. Only amlodipine and felodipine are safe in HFrEF
  • Non-DHPs in WPW syndrome: AV nodal slowing forces conduction through the accessory pathway → extremely rapid ventricular response in AF/flutter → ventricular fibrillation risk
  • Non-DHPs in cardiogenic shock / severe hypotension: Further reduction in contractility and vasodilation worsens haemodynamics
