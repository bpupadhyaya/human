Overview

Hemoglobin (Hb) is the paradigm allosteric protein. Packed at ~330 g/L inside erythrocytes, it carries 98.5% of blood oxygen — the remainder dissolved in plasma. Its four subunits communicate through quaternary conformational change: the T (tense, deoxy, low-affinity) state ↔ R (relaxed, oxy, high-affinity) state transition generates the sigmoidal O₂ dissociation curve that is exquisitely adapted to tissue oxygen delivery.

Three key regulatory inputs right-shift the ODC (reduce O₂ affinity), ensuring O₂ release in metabolically active tissues: (1) rising CO₂/H⁺ (Bohr effect), (2) 2,3-bisphosphoglycerate (2,3-BPG) binding, and (3) rising temperature. The opposite inputs in the pulmonary capillary facilitate O₂ loading. Fetal hemoglobin (HbF, α₂γ₂) has lower 2,3-BPG affinity, conferring higher O₂ affinity to extract oxygen across the placenta.

Mechanism — T↔R Cooperativity & Bohr Effect

Cooperative O₂ binding arises from the concerted (MWC) quaternary switch. The Bohr effect couples cellular CO₂/pH to O₂ release via protonation of His146β (and to a lesser extent His122α), which stabilises the T-state by forming a salt bridge to Asp94β. 2,3-BPG, produced by erythrocyte Rapoport-Luebering shunt, allosterically stabilises the T-state by bridging the two β subunits.

  1. Deoxy-Hb (T-state): all four heme irons are out-of-plane (domed toward proximal His); O₂ binding pulls Fe²⁺ into plane, moving the proximal His and the F-helix, triggering the T→R switch at the α₁β₂ interface.
  2. R-state: C-terminal β residues freed; salt bridges broken; central cavity contracts, expelling 2,3-BPG; O₂ affinity increases ~100-fold.
  3. Bohr protons (H⁺) from tissue CO₂ protonate His146β-COO⁻ → salt bridge stabilises T-state → right-shifts ODC → facilitates O₂ unloading at tissues.
  4. In the lung, CO₂ is exhaled; alkaline pH deprotonates His146β; R-state favoured; O₂ binds to all four subunits at PO₂ ~100 mmHg → 97% saturation.
  5. HbCO (carboxyHb): CO binds Fe²⁺ with 240× higher affinity than O₂; locks Hb in R-state with remaining O₂ sites but ODC left-shifted → no O₂ delivery — CO poisoning.

Pharmacology & Clinical Use

Hydroxyurea (HU) — ribonucleotide reductase inhibitor; reactivates γ-globin expression via HbF induction (mechanism: nitric oxide-mediated soluble guanylate cyclase activation → HbF gene derepression); 3–5× ↑HbF → HbS polymer inhibition → reduces sickling crises. Standard of care for sickle cell disease.

Voxelotor (Oxbryta) — allosteric Hb modifier; covalently modifies Val1α to maintain R-state, raising O₂ affinity; reduces HbS polymerisation. Trade-off: reduced O₂ unloading at tissues — used for haemolytic anaemia component of SCD.

L-glutamine — reduces oxidative stress in SCD erythrocytes; modestly reduces vaso-occlusive crises.

Gene therapy — betibeglogene (Zynteglo): lentiviral addition of functional β-globin in haematopoietic stem cells; approved for β-thalassaemia. Exa-cel (Casgevy): CRISPR-Cas9 disruption of BCL11A enhancer → HbF reactivation; first approved CRISPR therapy for SCD and β-thalassaemia.

Transfusion — stored blood depletes 2,3-BPG within 1–2 weeks (ODC left-shift); clinical significance debated, but fresh blood preferred for massive transfusion. Leukodepleted packed RBCs for haemolytic and aplastic anaemia.

Clinical pearl: HbA1c reflects glycation of β-chain N-terminal Val1β by glucose over RBC lifespan (~120 days). HbA1c underestimates glycaemia in haemolytic anaemia, HbS, HbC (reduced RBC lifespan); overestimates in iron deficiency anaemia (↑young RBCs with long lifespan). Use fructosamine in haemolytic disorders.

Connections

Hemoglobin synthesis depends on erythropoietin (EPO/JAK2/STAT5 → reticulocytosis) and iron (transferrin → TFRC → ferritin storage; hepcidin regulates). 2,3-BPG is a glycolytic intermediate (connects to glucose metabolism). HbS polymerisation involves NO scavenging → vasoconstriction (connects to eNOS/NO pathway). HbA1c is the gold standard glycaemic index (connects to insulin/glucose). CO production from HO-1 catabolism of heme is a signalling molecule.
