NF-κB

The master switch of inflammation — a family of five transcription factors (p65/p50 dimer dominant) that integrates upstream danger signals into a rapid, powerful transcriptional program driving cytokines, chemokines, cell adhesion molecules, anti-apoptotic proteins, and immune effector enzymes.

Discovered in 1986 by Sen and Baltimore as a nuclear factor binding the κ light-chain immunoglobulin enhancer in activated B cells. NF-κB is activated by LPS, TNF-α, IL-1β, viral nucleic acids, oxidative stress, and DNA damage, converting upstream IKKβ-mediated IκBα phosphorylation and proteasomal degradation into explosive nuclear translocation of p65/p50 within 15–30 minutes. Dysregulated NF-κB is the molecular nexus of RA, IBD, septic shock, atherosclerosis, and cancer.

Atlas One · Molecular · Transcription Factor / Inflammatory Master Regulator

NF-κB (Nuclear Factor kappa B)

Class: Rel/NF-κB transcription factor family  ·  Inflammatory master switch  |  Canonical dimer: p65 (RelA) / p50 (NF-κB1)  |  UniProt (p65): Q04206  |  Gene: RELA (chr 11q13.1)

NF-κB is the master transcription factor of innate immune activation, integrating signals from pattern-recognition receptors (TLRs, NLRs), cytokine receptors (TNFR1, IL-1R), antigen receptors (BCR/TCR), genotoxic stress, and oxidative stress into a transcriptional response activating hundreds of genes within minutes. In unstimulated cells, the canonical p65/p50 heterodimer is sequestered in the cytoplasm by IκBα. Activation proceeds through IKKβ phosphorylating IκBα at Ser32/Ser36, triggering βTrCP-mediated K48-linked polyubiquitylation and 26S proteasomal degradation, exposing the p65/p50 nuclear localisation signal for rapid nuclear translocation and target gene transcription. NF-κB is simultaneously essential for pathogen defence and a driver of pathological inflammation when constitutively active — a duality that has made it one of the most intensively studied but therapeutically challenging targets in medicine.

Overview

NF-κB was discovered in 1986 by Ranjan Sen and David Baltimore at MIT as a nuclear protein that bound to a decameric DNA sequence in the immunoglobulin κ light-chain enhancer specifically in activated B lymphocytes. This initial description as a B-cell-specific factor proved misleading: within a few years, NF-κB was found to be an inducible, ubiquitous regulator present in virtually every mammalian cell type, with a central role in innate immunity that transcended lymphocyte biology. The subsequent three decades have revealed NF-κB as a family of five proteins forming multiple dimers, regulated by at least three IκB inhibitory proteins and three IκB kinase (IKK) complex subunits, activated by at least a dozen distinct upstream pathways, and driving transcription of over 300 target genes.

The clinical significance of NF-κB spans essentially every inflammatory and many malignant diseases. In acute pathology: it is the central mediator of septic shock (LPS→TLR4→NF-κB→cytokine storm) and cytokine release syndrome in CAR-T therapy. In chronic inflammatory disease: constitutive NF-κB in synovial fibroblasts drives RA pannus; in gut epithelium and lamina propria macrophages drives IBD; in endothelial cells exposed to oxidised LDL drives atherosclerosis. In cancer: constitutive NF-κB activity in multiple myeloma, ABC-DLBCL, and many solid tumours promotes tumour cell survival via BCL-2 family induction, resisting chemotherapy-induced apoptosis.

Despite its centrality, direct NF-κB inhibition as a therapeutic strategy has failed repeatedly in clinical trials — principally because NF-κB is also an essential anti-apoptotic survival factor in normal cells, and complete inhibition causes immunosuppression and oncogenesis from impaired apoptotic surveillance. Current therapeutic strategies instead target NF-κB indirectly: anti-TNF biologics remove the positive-feedback TNF→NF-κB loop; corticosteroids (via GR transrepression) suppress NF-κB target genes without blocking IKK; bortezomib (proteasome inhibitor) prevents IκBα degradation in multiple myeloma.

IKK Complex (canonical pathway hub)

IKKα (IKK1): catalytic; primarily non-canonical activation; also required for some canonical signalling. IKKβ (IKK2): principal catalytic subunit of canonical NF-κB activation; phosphorylates IκBα at Ser32 and Ser36; activation loop phosphorylation at Ser177/Ser181 activates IKKβ. NEMO (IKKγ): non-catalytic regulatory scaffold; essential for canonical pathway; polyubiquitin-binding domain (via Uban domain) links upstream ubiquitin-based signals from TRAF6/LUBAC to IKK activation; NEMO mutations cause incontinentia pigmenti (X-linked).

  • IκBα negative feedback and oscillation: IκBα is a direct NF-κB target gene; newly synthesised IκBα enters the nucleus, strips p65/p50 from DNA, and escorts it back to the cytoplasm. This generates ~90-minute oscillations of NF-κB nuclear residence. Single-cell live imaging (Ashall et al., 2009) showed that the number and amplitude of NF-κB pulses — not simply total nuclear level — encodes signal identity and determines differential target gene expression, a form of frequency-domain information encoding.
  • Non-canonical (alternative) pathway: Activated by TNF superfamily receptors (BAFF-R, CD40, LTβR, RANK) through NIK (NF-κB-inducing kinase) accumulation → IKKα homodimer activation → p100 partial proteasomal processing → p52 → RelB/p52 nuclear translocation. Critical for lymphoid organ development and B-cell survival. Constitutive NIK activity (from TRAF3 loss-of-function mutations) drives non-canonical NF-κB in multiple myeloma.
  • Post-translational modifications of p65: PKAc phosphorylates Ser276 (enhances CBP/p300 binding); IKKβ phosphorylates Ser536 (activation); CBP/p300 acetylates Lys310 (required for full transcriptional activity); SIRT1/HDAC3 deacetylates p65 → nuclear export; GSK3β phosphorylates Ser468 → repression. These modifications integrate multiple signalling inputs at the level of p65 itself, enabling context-dependent fine-tuning of NF-κB transcriptional output beyond binary on/off activation.
  • NF-κB as survival factor: The anti-apoptotic target genes (BCL-2, BCL-XL, XIAP, c-FLIP) explain why complete NF-κB inhibition is dangerous — cells become hypersensitive to TNF-induced apoptosis, which can cause massive tissue destruction. This survival function also explains why constitutive NF-κB in cancer is oncogenic: tumour cells acquire resistance to apoptosis-inducing chemotherapy and radiation.

The NF-κB survival paradox and why it is hard to drug directly: NF-κB is simultaneously the master driver of pathological inflammation AND an essential anti-apoptotic survival factor in normal cells. Complete IKKβ inhibition eliminates cytokine production but also removes survival signals from epithelial cells, hepatocytes, and lymphocytes — causing apoptosis-driven gut barrier failure, liver injury, and lymphopenia in multiple animal studies. This dual function explains why no direct NF-κB inhibitor has succeeded in clinical inflammatory trials. Successful drugs instead act on upstream inputs (anti-TNF, anti-IL-1β) or downstream outputs (GR transrepression via corticosteroids), selectively attenuating NF-κB activity in inflamed tissues without globally blocking this essential survival pathway.
