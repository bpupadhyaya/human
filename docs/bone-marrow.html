Bone Marrow

Soft, highly vascularised connective tissue filling the medullary cavities of flat bones and the epiphyses of long bones. Generates ~500 billion blood cells/day via haematopoiesis from multipotent HSCs in endosteal and perivascular niches. Primary lymphoid organ: all blood cell lineages arise here. Two functional compartments: active red marrow (axial skeleton in adults) and quiescent yellow (adipose) marrow that reconverts under haematopoietic stress.

Overview

Bone marrow is the soft, highly vascularised connective tissue occupying the medullary cavities of bones. It is the central haematopoietic factory of the adult human body, generating an estimated 500 billion blood cells per day — approximately 3.5 million cells per second — to replace senescent erythrocytes, neutrophils, platelets, and other short-lived blood elements. All major blood cell lineages arise from a small, self-renewing pool of multipotent haematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) maintained in specialised marrow niches.

Beyond haematopoiesis, bone marrow is a primary lymphoid organ. B-cell lymphopoiesis and T-cell precursor generation both initiate here; T-cell maturation completes in the thymus. It is also a major site of immune memory: long-lived plasma cells (LLPCs) produced during germinal-centre reactions home back to bone marrow survival niches expressing CXCL12, IL-6, APRIL, and BAFF — where they secrete protective antibodies for years to decades, forming the durable humoral immunity that underpins effective vaccination.

Two functional types exist. Red marrow is haematopoietically active, appearing red from haemoglobin-laden maturing erythrocytes at different stages. Yellow marrow (marrow adipose tissue, MAT) is haematopoietically inactive, dominated by unilocular adipocytes. At birth nearly all marrow is red. Through childhood and adolescence, yellow marrow progressively occupies the appendicular skeleton, leaving red marrow confined to the axial skeleton (vertebrae, ribs, sternum, pelvis, skull) and proximal epiphyses of the femur and humerus by adulthood. Yellow marrow retains its capacity to reconvert to red marrow under haematopoietic stress — including severe haemolytic anaemia, chronic blood loss, and systemic infection.

Anatomy & Structure

Gross distribution (adults). Active red marrow concentrates in vertebral bodies, sternum, ribs, flat bones of the pelvis and skull, and the proximal epiphyses of the femur and humerus. Total marrow volume is approximately 1.5–2 kg (~4% of body weight); red marrow accounts for roughly half. The marrow is encased within the endosteum (inner bone surface lined by osteoblasts and osteoclasts) and perfused by a nutrient artery branching into arterioles and sinusoids.

Resident cell types beyond haematopoietic progenitors include: osteoblasts (endosteal surface, niche maintenance, THPO/SCF/CXCL12 production), osteoclasts (bone resorption), CAR/reticular stromal cells (primary CXCL12 and SCF source), sinusoidal endothelial cells, pericytes, macrophages (erythroblastic island central cells, niche macrophages), T cells, NK cells, and long-lived plasma cells — all cohabiting in an orchestrated three-dimensional architecture.

Erythropoiesis regulation

EPO from renal cortical peritubular fibroblasts (under hypoxia via HIF-2α) drives CFU-E expansion. Reticulocytes squeeze through sinusoid fenestrations into blood; mature in 1–2 days. EPO deficiency in CKD → normocytic normochromic anaemia; treated with recombinant EPO or HIF-prolyl hydroxylase inhibitors.

B-lymphopoiesis

IL-7 from stromal cells is the survival signal at pro-B stage. VDJ recombination of immunoglobulin heavy chain (RAG1/2) at pre-B; VJ recombination of light chain at immature B. Negative selection removes autoreactive BCRs before export. CXCL12, SCF, FLT3L support each stage.

LLPC niche & immune memory

After peripheral germinal-centre reactions, affinity-matured plasma cells express CXCR4 and home to bone marrow survival niches. CXCL12, IL-6, APRIL (from megakaryocytes), BAFF, and eosinophil-derived IL-5 maintain LLPC survival for decades — the basis of durable post-vaccination serum antibody titres.

Mobilisation

G-CSF disrupts CXCR4/CXCL12 and VLA-4/VCAM-1 retention interactions → HSC egress into blood — the basis of peripheral blood HSC harvest for transplantation. AMD3100 (plerixafor) directly antagonises CXCR4 for rapid mobilisation. Used clinically when G-CSF alone is insufficient.

Pathology

Acute Leukaemia (AML / ALL)

Malignant transformation of a myeloid (AML) or lymphoid (ALL) progenitor generates a clonal blast population that displaces normal haematopoiesis → pancytopenia (anaemia, infection susceptibility, bleeding). AML key mutations: t(8;21), inv(16), t(15;17) (APL — ATRA-responsive), FLT3-ITD, NPM1. ALL: t(9;22) (BCR-ABL1, Philadelphia⁺ — imatinib-responsive), t(12;21) (ETV6-RUNX1, most common childhood ALL, favourable prognosis). Treatment: induction chemotherapy → consolidation → HSCT for high-risk or relapsed/refractory disease; CAR-T (anti-CD19) for relapsed/refractory B-ALL.

Multiple Myeloma

Clonal marrow plasma cells produce a monoclonal immunoglobulin (M-protein). Marrow infiltration → anaemia, lytic bone lesions (osteoclast activation by DKK1/RANKL), hypercalcaemia; M-protein → renal cast nephropathy, hyperviscosity, amyloidosis (AL). CRAB criteria: hyperCalcaemia, Renal failure, Anaemia, Bone lesions. Treatment: bortezomib (proteasome inhibitor) + lenalidomide (IMiD) + dexamethasone → autologous HSCT; anti-CD38 (daratumumab) now frontline; BCMA-directed CAR-T and bispecifics in relapsed/refractory disease.

Myelofibrosis

JAK2 V617F (~60%), CALR, or MPL driver mutations → clonal myeloproliferation → reactive fibrosis (TGF-β from dysplastic megakaryocytes → reticulin/collagen deposition) → "dry tap" on marrow aspiration, leukoerythroblastic blood film (teardrop cells, nucleated RBCs, immature myeloid cells). Massive splenomegaly from extramedullary haematopoiesis. Symptoms: constitutional, anaemia, splenomegaly-related pain. Treatment: ruxolitinib (JAK1/2 inhibitor) for symptom control; allogeneic HSCT is the only potentially curative option.

Aplastic Anaemia

Destruction or autoimmune suppression of HSCs → hypocellular marrow → pancytopenia. Causes: idiopathic autoimmune (CD8⁺ T cells targeting HSCs, ~70%), drug/toxin (chloramphenicol, benzene), viral (EBV, CMV, parvovirus B19 in pure red cell aplasia), PNH clone, Fanconi anaemia. Treatment: immunosuppression (anti-thymocyte globulin + cyclosporin ± eltrombopag) when HSCT not feasible; matched sibling allogeneic HSCT for severe aplastic anaemia.

Inherited Bone Marrow Failure Syndromes

Fanconi anaemia (FANC gene mutations; DNA interstrand crosslink repair defect → progressive BMF + cancer predisposition + congenital anomalies). Dyskeratosis congenita (DKC1, TERC, TERT — telomere maintenance defects → BMF + oral leukoplakia + nail dystrophy + pulmonary fibrosis). Diamond–Blackfan anaemia (ribosomal protein mutations RPS19, RPL5 etc. → selective red cell aplasia; steroid-responsive in ~80%). Shwachman–Diamond syndrome (SBDS gene → neutropenia + exocrine pancreatic insufficiency).

Metastatic Marrow Infiltration

Carcinoma invasion (breast, prostate, lung, neuroblastoma) displaces haematopoietic tissue → leukoerythroblastic blood picture (teardrops, nucleated RBCs, immature myeloid cells). Marrow trephine biopsy is the most sensitive staging tool. Clinical features: bone pain, pathological fractures, hypercalcaemia, cytopenias. RANKL inhibitor (denosumab) and bisphosphonates (zoledronic acid) reduce skeletal-related events.
