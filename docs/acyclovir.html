Acyclovir

Acyclovir (aciclovir; 9-[(2-hydroxyethoxy)methyl]guanine) is the prototype acyclic nucleoside antiviral, discovered by Gertrude Elion and colleagues at Burroughs Wellcome in the 1970s. Its extraordinary selectivity for herpesvirus-infected cells — approximately 3000-fold relative to uninfected cells — arises from a two-stage activation process uniquely dependent on viral thymidine kinase. It remains the first-line treatment for HSV and VZV infections and the structural foundation for an entire family of antiherpetic prodrugs.

Drug class: Acyclic nucleoside analogue MW: 225.2 g/mol FDA approval: 1982 WHO Essential Medicine: yes

Mechanism of Action

Acyclovir is a guanosine analogue with an acyclic side chain in place of the standard deoxyribose sugar ring. This "open ring" lacks the 3′-hydroxyl group found in natural nucleosides, which is the basis for chain termination. The drug itself is inactive — it requires sequential phosphorylation to its triphosphate form (ACV-TP) to inhibit viral DNA polymerase.

Step 1: Viral Thymidine Kinase Monophosphorylation

In uninfected cells, acyclovir is a very poor substrate for cellular thymidine kinase (TK) and enters cells largely un-phosphorylated. In HSV-1/2 or VZV-infected cells, the viral thymidine kinase (TK; UL23 in HSV) has a much broader and more permissive substrate specificity than its cellular counterpart. Viral TK accepts acyclovir ~200-fold more efficiently than cellular TK, converting it to acyclovir monophosphate (ACV-MP). This first phosphorylation step is the primary selectivity filter: essentially all ACV-MP is generated in virus-infected cells.

Step 2: Cellular Kinases → Diphosphate and Triphosphate

ACV-MP is then phosphorylated by cellular guanylate kinase to ACV-diphosphate (ACV-DP), and then by several non-specific cellular nucleoside diphosphate kinases to acyclovir triphosphate (ACV-TP). These cellular enzymes are less discriminating and efficiently process ACV-MP, but since ACV-MP is only generated in infected cells, the triphosphate likewise accumulates selectively in infected cells. ACV-TP intracellular concentrations in HSV-infected cells reach ~40–100 μM, compared to <0.1 μM in uninfected cells.

Step 3: Competitive Inhibition and Chain Termination of Viral DNA Polymerase

ACV-TP acts as a competitive inhibitor of viral DNA polymerase (UL30, the catalytic subunit, complexed with its processivity factor UL42 for HSV). ACV-TP competes with the natural substrate dGTP for incorporation into the growing viral DNA strand. When ACV-TP is incorporated, the missing 3′-OH means no further nucleotide can be added — obligate chain termination occurs. Additionally, the viral polymerase becomes irreversibly bound to the DNA template–primer complex at the site of ACV-TP incorporation (a "dead-end complex"), inactivating the polymerase itself. This suicide inhibition amplifies the antiviral effect.

Selectivity quantified: ACV-TP has ~10–100-fold lower affinity for human cellular DNA polymerases (pol α, β, δ, γ) than for HSV DNA pol UL30. Combined with the ~200-fold selectivity of viral TK for ACV over cellular TK, the overall selectivity factor for acyclovir against HSV in infected vs. uninfected cells is approximately 3000-fold. This extraordinary selectivity index made acyclovir the first truly selective antiviral, earning Elion a Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1988.

The poor oral bioavailability of acyclovir (15–20%) reflects saturable intestinal absorption via the human intestinal peptide transporter (hPepT1); increasing the dose above 800 mg per dose yields diminishing returns. This pharmacokinetic limitation drove the development of prodrugs with improved bioavailability.

Clinical Uses

HSV-1 and HSV-2

Acute episodic treatment: Oral acyclovir or valacyclovir reduce duration and severity of recurrent genital herpes by 1–2 days when started within 24–48 hours of prodrome. Benefit in recurrent labialis is modest (<1 day reduction) unless initiated at prodrome. Valacyclovir 1 g twice daily × 5 days (or twice daily in immunocompetent recurrent genital HSV) is preferred over acyclovir 400 mg 5×/day due to simpler dosing.

Suppressive therapy: Valacyclovir 500 mg once daily reduces recurrence frequency by 70–80% and reduces HSV-2 sexual transmission by ~48% in serodiscordant couples (Corey et al., NEJM 2004, the AIMST trial; and the VALTREX suppression studies). This is the primary strategy for patients with frequent (≥6/year) recurrences.

HSV encephalitis: IV acyclovir 10 mg/kg every 8 hours for 14–21 days is the standard of care. Reduces mortality from >70% (untreated) to ~20–30%, and significantly reduces neurological sequelae. Prompt initiation (before CSF PCR confirmation) is essential. Pivotal evidence from the NIAD-supported trials (Whitley et al., NEJM 1986).

Neonatal HSV: IV acyclovir 20 mg/kg every 8 hours for 14–21 days (CNS disease) or 21 days (disseminated). High-dose IV acyclovir is the only treatment shown to reduce mortality and neurological morbidity in neonatal HSV disease; high dose is required because neonates have higher viral burden and more disseminated disease.

VZV

Varicella in adults: Oral acyclovir 800 mg 5×/day for 5 days (or valacyclovir 1 g 3×/day); initiated within 24–48 hours of rash onset. Reduces duration of new lesion formation and total fever by ~1–2 days in immunocompetent adults. Recommended in adolescents/adults (higher risk of complications), pregnant women in second/third trimester, and immunocompromised patients.

Herpes zoster (shingles): Valacyclovir 1 g 3×/day × 7 days (or famciclovir 500 mg 3×/day) initiated within 72 hours of rash onset reduces duration of rash, acute pain, and risk of postherpetic neuralgia (PHN). Efficacy is highest when treatment is started within the first 24–48 hours.

CMV Prophylaxis and Treatment

Acyclovir has limited activity against CMV (CMV lacks an efficient TK; activation depends on the UL97 kinase, which phosphorylates ganciclovir ~10–100-fold more efficiently than acyclovir). High-dose oral acyclovir (800 mg 4×/day) and valacyclovir reduce CMV disease incidence post-solid organ transplant, likely via anti-EBV and indirect immunomodulatory effects, but ganciclovir/valganciclovir are standard for CMV prophylaxis and treatment in immunocompromised hosts. Valganciclovir 900 mg once daily (with dose adjustment for renal function) is the standard prophylaxis regimen post-kidney, liver, heart, and lung transplantation.

Resistance Mechanisms

Acyclovir resistance is clinically significant primarily in immunocompromised patients (HIV/AIDS, post-transplant) where prolonged therapy selects for resistant mutants. In immunocompetent individuals, resistance is rare (<0.5% of clinical HSV isolates). Two major resistance mechanisms exist:

1. Thymidine Kinase Mutations (UL23) — Most Common (>95% of resistant isolates)

  • TK-null mutants: Frameshift or nonsense mutations that abolish TK expression entirely; no phosphorylation of acyclovir; most common resistance mechanism in clinical isolates; these viruses are less neurovirulent (TK is required for efficient reactivation from latency) but can cause progressive mucocutaneous disease in immunocompromised hosts
  • TK low-producer mutants: Reduced TK expression from promoter or early codon mutations; partial resistance; may respond to higher acyclovir doses
  • TK altered-specificity mutants: Point mutations in the substrate-binding pocket (e.g., around the nucleoside-binding region) that selectively reduce ACV phosphorylation while retaining thymidine phosphorylation for viral DNA replication

2. DNA Polymerase Mutations (UL30) — Less Common (~5%)

Point mutations in the UL30 catalytic subunit reduce ACV-TP binding affinity or incorporation. These are more likely to confer cross-resistance to multiple nucleoside analogues including penciclovir, in contrast to TK mutations which are specific to drugs requiring TK activation. DNA pol mutations tend to cluster in highly conserved polymerase motifs (Regions I, II, III, V, VI of the polymerase domain).

Salvage Therapies for Resistant HSV/VZV

  • Foscarnet (IV): First-line for acyclovir-resistant HSV and VZV; directly inhibits viral DNA polymerase at the pyrophosphate binding site without requiring phosphorylation; active against TK-null and most TK-altered mutants; primary toxicity is nephrotoxicity and electrolyte disturbances (hypocalcaemia, hypomagnesaemia, hypophosphataemia)
  • Cidofovir (IV): Second-line; nucleotide analogue (already monophosphorylated); does not require TK; active against many ACV-resistant HSV strains; nephrotoxic — must co-administer probenecid and IV saline prehydration
  • Brincidofovir (CMX001): Oral lipid conjugate of cidofovir with reduced nephrotoxicity; activity against HSV, CMV, and adenovirus
Cross-resistance: TK-null HSV mutants resistant to acyclovir are also resistant to valacyclovir, famciclovir/penciclovir, and ganciclovir, since all require viral TK for the initial phosphorylation step. Foscarnet and cidofovir remain active because they bypass TK. This complete cross-resistance among TK-dependent antivirals makes susceptibility testing and early identification of resistance clinically important in immunocompromised patients with non-healing HSV lesions.
