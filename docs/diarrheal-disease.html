Diarrheal Disease

The second leading cause of death in children under five globally — yet almost entirely preventable and treatable with ORS, zinc, clean water, and vaccines.

Diarrheal disease encompasses any clinical syndrome of three or more loose stools per day, caused by a diverse array of enteric pathogens (viruses, bacteria, parasites) as well as non-infectious causes. Despite enormous reductions in child mortality since the 1970s driven by oral rehydration therapy (ORT), diarrheal disease still kills ~525,000 children under five per year, with 80% of deaths in sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia. Understanding each pathogen's unique mechanism — from enterotoxin-driven secretion to attaching-effacing lesions to direct invasion — is essential for rational management and vaccine development.

Diarrheal Disease — Epidemiology, Pathogens & Management

Diarrheal disease is a syndromic diagnosis encompassing distinct molecular and cellular mechanisms: secretory diarrhea driven by enterotoxin-mediated cAMP/cGMP elevation; osmotic diarrhea from unabsorbed solutes; inflammatory/invasive diarrhea with mucosal destruction; and motility-mediated diarrhea. Each pathogen exploits a specific mechanism that dictates both clinical presentation and optimal management. Rotavirus alone causes ~25% of childhood diarrheal deaths, yet rotavirus vaccines have achieved 50–90% efficacy in preventing hospitalisation. Vibrio cholerae cholera toxin (CT) remains the canonical model of secretory diarrhea: CT ADP-ribosylates the Gsα subunit of adenylate cyclase, driving massive Cl⁻/HCO₃⁻ secretion via CFTR and producing rice-water stool at up to 1 litre/hour. ORS remains the intervention with the greatest impact per cost in the history of medicine.

Pathophysiology — Four Mechanisms

Secretory diarrhea

Driven by pathological elevation of intracellular second messengers that activate chloride secretion channels (primarily CFTR) independently of osmotic driving forces. cAMP is the key mediator for cholera toxin (CT) and ETEC LT: both ADP-ribosylate Gsα → constitutively active adenylate cyclase → cAMP ↑↑ → PKA → CFTR phosphorylation → Cl⁻ efflux into lumen (with compensatory Na⁺ and water secretion). ETEC ST enterotoxin and guanylin act via guanylate cyclase C (GCC) → cGMP ↑ → PKG → CFTR. VIP-secreting tumors (VIPoma) and carcinoid tumors cause secretory diarrhea by the same downstream pathways. Key clinical features: large-volume, watery, non-bloody; persists with fasting (unlike osmotic); isotonic stool; no fever; osmotic gap in stool is normal (<50 mOsm/kg).

Osmotic diarrhea

Results from the presence of poorly absorbed, osmotically active solutes in the intestinal lumen, which retain water and prevent reabsorption. Examples: lactose in lactase deficiency (undigested disaccharide → bacterial fermentation → short-chain acids + gas + osmotic load); magnesium-containing antacids/laxatives (Mg²⁺ is poorly absorbed); lactulose used therapeutically in hepatic encephalopathy; sorbitol and fructose malabsorption. Key diagnostic feature: diarrhea stops with fasting. Stool osmotic gap is elevated: measured osmolarity − 2×(stool Na⁺ + K⁺) >125 mOsm/kg indicates osmotic etiology.

Inflammatory / invasive diarrhea

Mucosal invasion and destruction by enteroinvasive pathogens — classically Shigella (the prototypical dysentery organism), EIEC, Campylobacter jejuni, non-typhoidal Salmonella, Entamoeba histolytica, and CDAD (Clostridioides difficile TcdA/TcdB). Pathogens invade the colonic mucosa (or ileum), inducing an intense inflammatory response with neutrophil and macrophage infiltration. Results in: small-volume bloody/mucoid stool (dysentery), fever, lower abdominal cramping, tenesmus, and urgency. Stool microscopy shows white blood cells (WBCs) and red blood cells. Histopathology: mucosal ulceration, crypt abscesses, lamina propria neutrophilia. Treatment with appropriate antibiotics (azithromycin for Campylobacter/Salmonella; ciprofloxacin or ceftriaxone for Shigella in adults; metronidazole/vancomycin for C. difficile) is important; antimotility agents contraindicated in bloody/febrile diarrhea due to risk of toxic megacolon and delayed pathogen clearance.

Motility-related diarrhea

Abnormal accelerated intestinal transit reduces contact time between luminal contents and the absorptive mucosal surface, resulting in loose stools despite normal absorptive capacity. Mechanisms include: post-infectious irritable bowel syndrome (PI-IBS) — persistent altered motility after an acute enteric infection (rotavirus, Campylobacter, ETEC), associated with mast cell activation and serotonin release; thyrotoxicosis (elevated T3/T4 increases smooth muscle excitability and GI transit); diabetic autonomic neuropathy (vagal denervation disrupts peristaltic coordination); short bowel syndrome after ileal resection; and VIP/serotonin-mediated disorders. IBS diarrhea subtype (IBS-D): altered gut-brain axis, visceral hypersensitivity, low-grade immune activation, and dysbiosis contribute.

Management

Oral Rehydration Solution (ORS)

The WHO/UNICEF low-osmolality ORS formula (2002): Na⁺ 75 mmol/L, Cl⁻ 65 mmol/L, glucose 75 mmol/L (1:1 molar ratio with Na⁺ — exploits coupled Na⁺-glucose cotransporter SGLT1 in enterocytes, which is preserved even in secretory diarrhea because SGLT1 is cAMP-independent), K⁺ 20 mmol/L, citrate 10 mmol/L, total osmolarity 245 mOsm/L. The reduction from 311 mOsm/L (original formula) to 245 mOsm/L reduced stool output by 33% and vomiting by 30% in children. ORS has reduced child diarrheal mortality by an estimated 50-fold since introduction in the 1970s. For cholera (very high purge rates), some guidelines retain higher-sodium ORS; IV Ringer's lactate for severe dehydration (sunken eyes, poor skin turgor, lethargy, inability to drink).

Zinc supplementation

WHO/UNICEF recommend zinc 20 mg/day (10 mg/day in infants <6 months) for 10–14 days in all children with acute diarrhea. Evidence base: Bhutta et al. meta-analysis (2000, Pediatrics) and subsequent pooled analyses show zinc reduces diarrhea duration by ~25% and frequency by ~25%, and reduces the risk of a subsequent diarrheal episode by 33% over the following 2–3 months. Mechanism: zinc restores intestinal mucosal barrier function (tight junctions, brush-border enzymes), modulates immune response, and reverses zinc depletion from diarrheal losses (zinc is secreted in large quantities in stool during acute diarrhea). Zinc deficiency affects >2 billion people globally and is independently associated with increased severity and duration of diarrhea.

Antibiotic use — targeted indications only

Antibiotics are indicated for: cholera (single-dose azithromycin 1g adult/20 mg/kg child, or doxycycline 300 mg single dose, reduces duration and transmission; reduces stool output by ~50%); Shigella dysentery (azithromycin first-line due to widespread fluoroquinolone resistance); severe or prolonged Salmonella (NTS in immunocompromised, infants, or bacteraemic disease — azithromycin or ceftriaxone); febrile traveller's diarrhea with systemic symptoms. Antibiotics are NOT indicated for: viral diarrhea (rotavirus, norovirus), ETEC (self-limited), or uncomplicated non-bloody watery diarrhea in immunocompetent adults. Empiric antibiotic use in uncomplicated childhood diarrhea is associated with microbiome disruption, Clostridioides difficile risk, and antibiotic resistance without benefit.

Antimotility agents

Loperamide (a peripheral µ-opioid receptor agonist) reduces intestinal motility and secretion, decreasing stool volume and frequency. Appropriate use: uncomplicated non-dysenteric, non-febrile traveller's diarrhea in adults; reduces trip-interrupting diarrhea episodes. Absolute contraindications: bloody diarrhea, fever (may indicate invasive bacterial or colitis requiring antibiotic clearance), Shigella or STEC (E. coli O157:H7) infection (risk of toxic megacolon, prolonged bacteraemia, and haemolytic uraemic syndrome — HUS), children under 2 years (risk of ileus and CNS effects), and colitis. Evidence for bismuth subsalicylate (BSS): ~62% efficacy for prevention and ~50% treatment of traveller's diarrhea; MOA includes antisecretory and anti-inflammatory properties.

WASH and Global Prevention

WASH frameworkWater, Sanitation, and Hygiene (WASH) — 94% of diarrheal disease is attributable to unsafe water, inadequate sanitation, and poor hygiene (WHO). WASH encompasses: safe drinking water supply (piped, treated, protected); improved sanitation (toilets connected to sewers or safely managed pits, eliminating open defecation); and handwashing with soap at critical times (after defecation, before food handling). WASH is also one of the most cost-effective health interventions — every $1 invested returns $5–$11 in economic benefits (reduced healthcare costs, lost productivity).
SDG 6 targetsSustainable Development Goal 6 (Clean Water and Sanitation): achieve universal access to safe and affordable drinking water (SDG 6.1) and adequate and equitable sanitation and hygiene (SDG 6.2) by 2030. As of 2023, ~2.2 billion people lack safely managed drinking water services; ~3.5 billion lack safely managed sanitation. Progress is off track for 2030 deadline in Sub-Saharan Africa and parts of South Asia.
Vaccine impactRotavirus vaccines (Rotarix/RotaTeq) have prevented an estimated 4 million hospitalisations and 180,000–200,000 deaths per year globally since 2006 introduction and WHO recommendation. Oral cholera vaccines (OCV: Shanchol, Euvichol-Plus) are WHO pre-qualified for outbreak response; 2-dose regimens provide 65% protection for 2+ years. Typhoid conjugate vaccine (TCV) protects against enteric fever (Salmonella typhi), increasingly relevant as an invasive complication of enteric disease in endemic regions. No licensed vaccine exists for ETEC, Campylobacter, Cryptosporidium, or norovirus as of 2026 (multiple in trials).
BreastfeedingExclusive breastfeeding for 6 months provides passive immune protection against enteric pathogens via secretory IgA and bioactive compounds (lactoferrin, oligosaccharides, lysozyme) in breast milk. Breastfed infants have 50–70% lower risk of diarrheal disease-related hospitalisation compared to formula-fed infants in LMICs. WHO recommends exclusive breastfeeding to 6 months, continued alongside complementary foods to 2 years.
