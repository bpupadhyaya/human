Varivax

Varivax (Merck) is the live attenuated varicella vaccine using the Oka strain of varicella-zoster virus, licensed in the United States in 1995. Its introduction transformed varicella from a near-universal childhood illness (∼4 million cases/year in the US) into a largely vaccine-preventable disease, with a two-dose schedule achieving ≥98% protection against clinical varicella and near-complete elimination of severe disease.

Scale: L02 Molecular Type: Live attenuated Developer: Merck US licensure: March 17, 1995 Schedule: 2 doses (12–15 months + 4–6 years)

Overview

Varivax is a live attenuated varicella-zoster virus vaccine manufactured by Merck & Co., Inc. It was licensed by the FDA on March 17, 1995 — becoming the first varicella vaccine licensed in the United States — making the US one of the first countries to implement universal varicella vaccination. The vaccine is administered by subcutaneous injection.

Following initial licensure with a single-dose schedule (12–18 months of age), surveillance data from the early post-licensure period revealed a rate of breakthrough varicella (typically mild, 10–50 vesicles) in single-dose vaccinees of approximately 1–3% per year. This prompted ACIP to revise the schedule in 2006 to a two-dose series: dose 1 at 12–15 months and dose 2 at 4–6 years. The two-dose schedule reduces breakthrough varicella by approximately 3.3-fold compared to the one-dose schedule and achieves ≥98% protection against any clinical varicella.

Varivax is also available as a component of ProQuad (MMRV), a combined measles-mumps-rubella-varicella vaccine. Varivax may be given simultaneously with other childhood vaccines but should not be administered within 28 days of another live vaccine (other than MMRV) unless given on the same day.

Prior to the introduction of universal vaccination, varicella caused approximately 4 million cases, 11,000 hospitalizations, and 100 deaths annually in the United States, predominantly in children. Post-vaccination era surveillance shows >90% reduction in varicella incidence, hospitalization, and mortality in vaccinated cohorts.

Oka Strain: Attenuation and Genetic Characteristics

The Oka strain is the attenuated VZV strain used in all licensed varicella and zoster vaccines worldwide (Varivax, ProQuad, Varilrix [GSK], Zostavax). It was developed in the early 1970s by Dr. Michiaki Takahashi at Osaka University, Japan, through sequential passage of wild-type VZV isolated from a child named "Oka" with natural varicella.

Attenuation Passage History

Wild-type VZV was progressively attenuated by serial passage in human embryonic lung (HEL) cells, then guinea pig embryo (GPE) cells, and finally in WI-38 human diploid cells (Takahashi et al., Lancet 1974). The serial passage process selected for variants with altered cell tropism, reduced replication efficiency at body temperature, and diminished neurovirulence while preserving immunogenicity.

  • Passage history: ~11 passages in HEL cells → 12 passages in GPE cells → 11–14 passages in WI-38 cells (producer cell line for Varivax is MRC-5)
  • Genetic divergence from wild-type: The Oka vaccine strain differs from wild-type VZV at approximately 42 single nucleotide positions across the genome; these mutations are distributed across both unique short (US) and unique long (UL) regions and repeat elements
  • Critical attenuation mutations: The molecular basis of Oka attenuation includes mutations in ORF62 (the major transactivator IE62, IE63 transcriptional regulator), ORF10 (virion tegument protein), and membrane glycoproteins; no single mutation is responsible — attenuation is polygenic
  • Reduced neurovirulence: Guinea pig model studies demonstrate substantially reduced ganglionic invasion compared to wild-type VZV; Oka strain replicates poorly at 39°C (human body core temperature), limiting systemic viremia

Despite attenuation, the Oka strain retains full immunogenicity against wild-type VZV challenges and can still establish latency in dorsal root ganglia, as discussed in the vaccine-strain zoster section below.

Genetic identification: Vaccine Oka (vOka) can be distinguished from wild-type VZV by single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) at multiple positions, most reliably at nucleotide 54857 (ORF62/ORF71), which carries a characteristic vOka allele. This allows clinical differentiation of vaccine-strain versus wild-type VZV zoster by sequencing — an important epidemiological and clinical distinction.

Immunogenicity

Varivax induces both humoral and cell-mediated immunity against VZV, replicating the key immunological features of natural infection at a lower risk than clinical varicella.

Humoral Response

Seroconversion rates (anti-VZV IgG) following the two-dose schedule exceed 99% in healthy children. A gpELISA (glycoprotein enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay) antibody titer of ≥5 IU/mL is considered the serological correlate of protection against varicella. Antibody titers following the two-dose schedule are substantially higher than after single-dose vaccination, with a more durable response. Long-term follow-up studies confirm persistence of anti-VZV antibodies for ≥20 years in two-dose vaccinees, although levels are typically lower than those seen following wild-type varicella infection.

The seroconversion rate is somewhat lower in adolescents and adults (approximately 78–82% after dose 1, >99% after dose 2), reflecting possible incomplete responses in those with pre-existing partial immunity from subclinical exposure and the less permissive immune environment in older individuals. Adults who remain seronegative after two doses have a higher lifetime risk of breakthrough varicella.

Cell-Mediated Immunity

VZV-specific CD4+ and CD8+ T-cell proliferative responses are induced following vaccination. Cell-mediated immunity (CMI) is the primary effector mechanism for VZV clearance and control of reactivation. Vaccinees develop VZV-specific lymphoproliferation detectable for ≥10 years post-vaccination in immunocompetent individuals, though at lower levels than natural infection survivors.

Efficacy and Post-Licensure Performance

One-Dose Schedule (1995–2006)

The initial single-dose schedule demonstrated approximately 85% efficacy against any clinical varicella and >95% efficacy against severe varicella (>50 vesicular lesions, fever, or hospitalization) in controlled trials and post-licensure surveillance. Breakthrough varicella in single-dose vaccinees was typically mild (median ~50 lesions vs. ~300 in unvaccinated children) and less contagious than natural varicella.

Two-Dose Schedule (2006–present)

Post-licensure observational data and prospective studies firmly established the superior protection of the two-dose schedule:

  • Kuter et al. (2004): Among children vaccinated at 12–23 months, the two-dose schedule provided 3.3-fold greater protection against breakthrough varicella compared to single-dose vaccination; cumulative breakthrough varicella rates were 2.7% (two-dose) vs. 8.9% (one-dose) over 10 years of follow-up
  • Marin et al. (2016): 10-year follow-up from a Varivax cohort study found two-dose effectiveness of 98.3% against any varicella and 99.9% against severe varicella; vaccine-era children 14–16 years old showed >90% reduction in varicella incidence compared to the pre-vaccine era
  • Nearly 100% protection against severe varicella: Across multiple post-licensure datasets, no vaccinated two-dose recipients have died from varicella, and hospitalization for varicella is exceedingly rare in two-dose vaccinees in the absence of immunocompromise

Effectiveness Against Varicella-Related Complications

The vaccine eliminates the severe complications of primary varicella that drove pre-vaccine mortality: bacterial superinfection of pox lesions (particularly Group A Streptococcus and Staphylococcus aureus), varicella pneumonia, varicella encephalitis, and cerebellar ataxia. Population-level surveillance shows >90% reductions in varicella-associated hospitalization rates in birth cohorts receiving two-dose vaccination.

Vaccine-Strain Zoster (vOka Reactivation)

Despite attenuation, the Oka vaccine strain retains the capacity to establish latency in dorsal root ganglia (DRG) neurons following vaccination — a consequence of the biology of VZV cell-to-cell spread and neurotropism that is inherent to the viral replication cycle during primary infection. A small proportion of vaccine recipients subsequently experience vaccine-strain zoster (vOka zoster):

  • Incidence: The rate of vaccine-strain zoster is substantially lower than wild-type VZV zoster; estimates range from approximately 2–5 cases per 100,000 vaccine doses, compared to a wild-type zoster lifetime risk of ~30% in the unvaccinated; vOka zoster incidence in children is approximately 2–4-fold lower than wild-type VZV zoster in historical unvaccinated cohorts
  • Clinical characteristics: vOka zoster is typically milder than wild-type VZV zoster, with fewer vesicular lesions per dermatome, shorter duration, and a lower risk of postherpetic neuralgia; systemic dissemination is extremely rare in immunocompetent individuals
  • Identification: vOka zoster is confirmed by PCR with sequencing of the characteristic SNP at nucleotide 54857 (or other defining polymorphisms); routine clinical differentiation is not performed but is essential for epidemiological surveillance
  • Immunocompromised vaccinees: Children with leukemia or other immunosuppressive conditions who receive Varivax (with appropriate precautions) may rarely develop vOka zoster, typically within 1–2 years of vaccination; cases have been documented in children with acute lymphoblastic leukemia in remission
Risk-benefit calculation: The residual risk of vOka zoster is substantially smaller in both magnitude and severity than the risk of zoster from wild-type VZV infection (which occurs in ~30% of individuals over a lifetime and commonly causes significant morbidity). Vaccination eliminates wild-type VZV acquisition in most vaccinees, and any subsequent vOka zoster is almost uniformly mild. The risk-benefit balance strongly favors vaccination.

Two-Vaccine Strategy: Varivax + Shingrix

VZV causes two distinct clinical diseases: primary varicella (chickenpox) following first infection, and herpes zoster (shingles) following reactivation of latent virus. Varivax and Shingrix address these two disease phases in separate populations:

Adults vaccinated with Varivax in childhood who reach age 50 are subsequently eligible and recommended to receive Shingrix to prevent herpes zoster reactivation from latent vOka (or any wild-type VZV acquired from breakthrough exposure). These vaccines are fully complementary across the life course.
