Lung Slice (PCLS)

Precision-cut lung slices (PCLS) are 150–300 µm-thick ex vivo sections that preserve viable airway, vascular, and parenchymal architecture for 24–72 hours. They are the gold-standard tissue-level model for airway pharmacology, bronchoconstriction assays, pulmonary infection, and fibrosis research.

Overview — The PCLS Technique

Precision-cut lung slices (PCLS) were introduced as a preparation for studying intact lung tissue outside the body. The method exploits the structural accessibility of excised lung — either rodent or human donor tissue — while preserving the three-dimensional cellular relationships that are lost in monolayer cell culture.

Standard protocol: The excised lung (or isolated lobe) is inflated via the trachea or bronchus with warm low-melting-point agarose (1.5–2% in PBS), which solidifies at room temperature and stabilizes alveolar architecture during cutting. A vibratome (oscillating blade microtome) cuts serial slices at 150–300 µm thickness. Slices are collected in culture medium (DMEM/F-12 with 1% antibiotics, typically without serum to avoid myofibroblast activation) and rocked or placed on transwell inserts for oxygenation.

Viability window: Fresh PCLS remain metabolically active for 24–72 hours at 37°C under 5% CO₂. Ciliary beating in bronchiolar epithelium, spontaneous airway smooth muscle tone, and live cell markers (ATP content, MTT reduction, TEER for barrier integrity) confirm viability. Extended culture protocols using air-liquid interface conditions can maintain viability for up to 7–14 days with gradual cellular dedifferentiation. Human donor PCLS (from lung transplant explants or research-consented surgical resections) replicate human pharmacology more accurately than rodent material — a critical consideration given species differences in airway pharmacology (e.g., β2-adrenergic receptor expression on ASM, inflammatory cell populations).

Readouts: Airway caliber (cross-sectional area) quantified by phase-contrast video microscopy; cytokine/chemokine secretion (ELISA, multiplex); cell viability/apoptosis (LDH release, caspase); gene expression (RT-qPCR, RNA-seq); immunofluorescence confocal microscopy of intact architecture; viral replication (plaque assay, qPCR); impedance-based cell index.

Lung Architecture at the Tissue Scale

Conducting zone (generations 1–16)

The conducting zone conducts air from trachea (generation 0) through bronchi, bronchioles, and terminal bronchioles (generation 16) to the gas-exchange units. It has no alveoli and performs no gas exchange; its functions are air humidification, warming, filtration, and mucociliary defense. Total cross-sectional area increases dramatically from trachea (~2.5 cm²) to terminal bronchioles (~180 cm²), reducing airflow velocity and allowing particles to settle or be cleared.

Epithelial changes along the conducting zone:

  • Trachea and main bronchi: Pseudostratified ciliated columnar epithelium (tall ciliated cells, goblet cells, basal cells); supported by cartilaginous C-rings; submucosal glands (mucous acini + serous acini) contribute mucus and antimicrobial proteins (lysozyme, lactoferrin, secretory IgA)
  • Lobar and segmental bronchi: Transition to simple columnar; cartilage plates (incomplete); goblet cells prominent; submucosal glands present
  • Bronchioles (generations 9–16): No cartilage; no submucosal glands; simple columnar → cuboidal; goblet cells decrease; club cells (formerly Clara cells) increase to ~80% of cells in terminal bronchioles; club cells are non-ciliated secretory cells that secrete CC16 (anti-inflammatory), detoxify inhaled xenobiotics via CYP450 enzymes (CYP1B1, CYP2B6), and serve as bronchiolar progenitors
  • Terminal bronchioles (generation 16): Smallest purely conducting airways (~0.5 mm diameter in adult human); simple cuboidal; predominantly club cells with scattered ciliated cells

Respiratory zone (generations 17–23)

The respiratory zone begins where alveoli first appear on bronchiolar walls (respiratory bronchioles, generation 17–19) and extends through alveolar ducts (generations 20–22) and alveolar sacs (generation 23 — blind-ending clusters). The acinus — the fundamental functional gas-exchange unit — encompasses all structures distal to a single terminal bronchiole (~5 alveolar duct generations, ~2,000 alveoli per acinus in humans). Approximately 150,000 acini per lung. Total alveolar number: ~300 million (range 200–600M by stereology); mean alveolar diameter ~250 µm at functional residual capacity. Total gas-exchange surface area ~70 m².

Airway Smooth Muscle (ASM) — Contraction and Relaxation

Airway smooth muscle forms a helical-to-circumferential layer encircling airways from the lobar bronchi to respiratory bronchioles; no ASM in alveolar ducts. ASM constitutes the primary mechanical determinant of airway caliber and is central to the pathophysiology of asthma and COPD.

ASM markers: α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA), smooth muscle myosin heavy chain (SM-MHC/MYH11), calponin, smoothelin; distinguish ASM from vascular smooth muscle and myofibroblasts.

Contraction

Multiple pro-constrictive agonists converge on intracellular Ca²⁺ elevation and myosin light chain kinase (MLCK) activation:

  • Methacholine / acetylcholine: M3 muscarinic receptor (Gq) → phospholipase Cβ → IP₃ → IP₃R on sarcoplasmic reticulum → Ca²⁺ release; also DAG → protein kinase C → MLCK sensitization
  • Histamine: H1 receptor (Gq) → same IP₃/Ca²⁺ pathway; mast cell-derived; important in allergen challenge
  • Leukotrienes (LTC4, LTD4, LTE4): CysLT1/CysLT2 receptors → Gq; potent bronchoconstrictors derived from eosinophil/mast cell arachidonic acid via 5-lipoxygenase
  • Ca²⁺-calmodulin activation of MLCK → phosphorylation of myosin regulatory light chain (rMLC20) → cross-bridge cycling between myosin II and actin → shortening; Rho kinase (ROCK1/2) inhibits myosin light chain phosphatase → Ca²⁺ sensitization (contraction maintained at submaximal Ca²⁺)

Relaxation

  • β2-adrenergic receptor (β2-AR, ADRB2) pathway: β2-agonists (salbutamol/albuterol, salmeterol, formoterol) → Gs → adenylyl cyclase → cAMP ↑ → protein kinase A (PKA) → MLCK phosphorylation (reduced Ca²⁺ sensitivity) + BKCa channel activation (membrane hyperpolarization → reduced VOCC opening → Ca²⁺ influx ↓); bronchodilation within minutes
  • Nitric oxide / sGC pathway: NO from airway epithelium + constitutive NOS in ASM → soluble guanylate cyclase → cGMP ↑ → protein kinase G (PKG) → MLCK inhibition + K⁺ channel activation → relaxation
  • PDE4 inhibitors (roflumilast): block cAMP catabolism by phosphodiesterase-4 → sustained cAMP → PKA activation → bronchodilation + anti-inflammatory effects (reduces neutrophil and eosinophil activation)

ASM remodeling in chronic asthma: Repeated bronchospasm and chronic Th2/Th17 inflammatory stimuli (IL-13, IL-17, TGF-β1) drive ASM hypertrophy (cell enlargement) and hyperplasia (proliferation) → irreversible airway narrowing contributing to fixed airflow obstruction. Thickened ASM layer visible on endobronchial biopsy (>3 cell layers vs. 1–2 in normal). Bronchial thermoplasty (BT) — radiofrequency (RF) energy delivered bronchoscopically to ablate ASM — reduces severe asthma exacerbations (AIR2 trial, Castro et al., AJRCCM 2010).

Mucociliary Clearance and Airway Surface Liquid

Mucociliary clearance (MCC) is the primary physical defense mechanism of the conducting airways, propelling inhaled particles, pathogens, and cellular debris from the lower respiratory tract toward the larynx for swallowing or expectoration.

Airway surface liquid (ASL) organization

The ASL is a two-layer system overlying ciliated airway epithelium:

  • Periciliary layer (PCL): ~7 µm depth; low viscosity (similar to water); contains membrane-tethered mucins (MUC1, MUC4, MUC16) forming a hydrated gel-brush that lubricates cilia and supports effective ciliary beating; tightly regulated depth matching ciliary height
  • Mucus layer: 2–5 µm; viscoelastic gel composed of secreted, gel-forming mucins (MUC5AC from goblet cells; MUC5B from submucosal glands); entraps particles >0.5 µm; propelled orally by ciliary beating

Ciliary mechanics: Airway cilia (9+2 axoneme structure; outer dynein arm-driven) beat at 12–15 Hz with a two-phase stroke: effective (power) stroke sweeps mucus toward the mouth; recovery stroke returns cilia through the low-viscosity PCL. Coordination of beat direction across thousands of cells (metachronal coordination) via hydrodynamic coupling amplifies transport. Particles 1–5 µm deposited in bronchioles are cleared within hours; particles <1 µm may reach alveoli (cleared by macrophages).

CFTR and ion transport in ASL maintenance

CFTR (cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator; ABCC7 gene) is a cAMP-activated Cl⁻ channel on the apical membrane of ciliated and goblet cells. CFTR secretes Cl⁻ into the ASL, driving osmotic water flow to maintain PCL hydration. ENaC (epithelial Na⁺ channel, SCNN1 subunits) on the same apical surface absorbs Na⁺ (and water osmotically), regulated in part by CFTR (CFTR tonically inhibits ENaC).

CFTR: activated by PKA phosphorylation (cAMP) → Cl⁻ secretion ↑ → PCL hydration ENaC: constitutively active Na⁺ absorption → regulated by CFTR inhibition Net: fluid balance determines PCL depth → optimal cilia beat → effective MCC
In CF (ΔF508, G551D, or other CFTR mutations): Cl⁻ secretion ↓ + ENaC overactive → PCL dehydration → mucus layer compresses onto cilia → cilia become immobilized → chronic airway infection (Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Staphylococcus aureus, Burkholderia cepacia).

CFTR modulators represent the transformative pharmacological advance in CF treatment: Ivacaftor (VX-770): CFTR potentiator — keeps CFTR gate open; for gating mutations (G551D, ~4% of CF); restores Cl⁻ channel function in rare but functional CFTR protein. Lumacaftor/ivacaftor (Orkambi) and tezacaftor/ivacaftor (Symdeko): corrector + potentiator combinations for ΔF508 homozygotes; lumacaftor/tezacaftor correct protein folding (ΔF508 mis-folded CFTR is degraded by ERAD); modest FEV1 improvement. Elexacaftor/tezacaftor/ivacaftor (KAFTRIO/Trikafta): triple combination; elexacaftor is a next-generation corrector binding a different site on ΔF508-CFTR from tezacaftor; combination restores ~50% of normal CFTR function; ~14% improvement in FEV1 predicted; dramatic reduction in exacerbations and sweat chloride; eligible for ~90% of CF patients with ≥1 ΔF508 allele (Heijerman et al., Lancet 2019).

Lung Pathology at the Tissue Level

Asthma

Asthma is a chronic inflammatory airway disease characterized by variable airflow obstruction and bronchial hyperresponsiveness. The histological hallmarks visible in airway biopsies and PCLS include:

  • Goblet cell metaplasia and mucus hypersecretion: IL-13 (Th2 cytokine) drives transdifferentiation of club cells and ciliated cells into goblet cells (via STAT6 → SPDEF transcription factor); increased MUC5AC secretion → mucus plugging; mucus plugs in small airways are a major cause of fatal asthma and air trapping
  • Subepithelial fibrosis: Thickening of the lamina reticularis (basement membrane zone) by collagen III and V deposition; driven by TGF-β1 from epithelial and immune cells; correlates with severity of airway obstruction; visible on PCLS as dense pericellular matrix under the epithelial layer
  • ASM hypertrophy and hyperplasia: IL-13, PDGF, EGF, and TGF-β drive ASM mass increase; thickened ASM layer reduces maximal bronchodilator response and contributes to fixed obstruction
  • Eosinophilic inflammation: Submucosal and intraepithelial eosinophils (IL-5 + eotaxin-3/CCL26 dependent); eosinophil-derived MBP, ECP damage epithelium; type 2 severe eosinophilic asthma treated with anti-IL-5 (mepolizumab, benralizumab), anti-IL-4Rα (dupilumab, blocks IL-4 + IL-13)
  • Mast cell infiltration: Intraepithelial and ASM-resident mast cells (ASMAs); IgE-mediated degranulation (FcεRI) releases histamine, LTC4/D4, prostaglandin D2 → immediate bronchoconstriction

COPD

COPD encompasses two partly overlapping pathological processes:

  • Emphysema: Permanent, abnormal enlargement of airspaces distal to terminal bronchioles, with destruction of alveolar walls. Mechanism: protease-antiprotease imbalance — tobacco smoke recruits neutrophils and macrophages → neutrophil elastase + MMP-9/12 degrade elastin and collagen in alveolar septa; α1-antitrypsin (AAT) normally inhibits neutrophil elastase — genetic AAT deficiency (Z or S allele; PiZZ phenotype) accelerates emphysema, especially in smokers. Centriacinar (centrilobular) emphysema — upper-lobe predominant, smoking-related; panacinar emphysema — lower-lobe, AAT deficiency. PCLS from emphysematous lungs show enlarged irregular airspaces, reduced alveolar number per field, and torn septal remnants.
  • Chronic bronchitis: Clinically defined as productive cough for ≥3 months/year for ≥2 years. Histologically: mucous gland hypertrophy (Reid index = gland:wall thickness ratio >0.4; normal <0.4); goblet cell metaplasia in small airways; mucus hypersecretion; neutrophilic airway inflammation (IL-8, TNF-α, LTB4-mediated); ciliary dysfunction from smoke toxins; small airway inflammation and fibrosis (bronchiolitis)

Idiopathic Pulmonary Fibrosis (IPF)

IPF is characterized by the usual interstitial pneumonia (UIP) pattern — the most common and most severe of the idiopathic interstitial pneumonias. Key tissue-level features:

  • Honeycombing: Clusters of cystic airspaces (~3–10 mm diameter) lined by bronchiolar epithelium (bronchiolization of alveoli) within densely fibrotic tissue; predominantly basal and subpleural; the hallmark of established UIP
  • Traction bronchiectasis: Irregular dilatation of bronchi and bronchioles pulled open by surrounding fibrosis; visible on HRCT and PCLS from IPF tissue
  • Fibroblastic foci: Discrete collections of proliferating fibroblasts/myofibroblasts within loose myxoid matrix, overlying a damaged alveolar epithelium; considered the active leading edge of fibrosis; their density correlates with disease progression and mortality
  • Temporal and spatial heterogeneity: Areas of normal-appearing parenchyma adjacent to areas of complete fibrotic obliteration — the sine qua non of UIP pattern distinguishing it from NSIP (non-specific interstitial pneumonia, more homogeneous). PCLS from IPF shows this regional variation within a single slice.

Anti-fibrotic treatments: Nintedanib (INPULSIS trial; tyrosine kinase inhibitor of PDGFR, VEGFR, FGFR — reduces FVC decline ~50%) and pirfenidone (ASCEND/CAPACITY trials; reduces TGF-β/TNF-α signaling and collagen synthesis — reduces FVC decline ~50%). Both reduce disease progression but do not halt or reverse it; median survival from diagnosis without treatment ~3–5 years.
