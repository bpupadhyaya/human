Dengue Virus

The world's most prevalent arthropod-borne virus — four antigenically distinct serotypes whose cross-reactive immune memory turns a second infection into a potentially fatal hemorrhagic emergency.

Dengue virus (DENV) is a +ssRNA flavivirus transmitted by Aedes aegypti and Ae. albopictus. An estimated 390 million infections occur annually across 128 countries. Its unique four-serotype biology means prior immunity can paradoxically amplify viral load via antibody-dependent enhancement (ADE) — the molecular engine of dengue hemorrhagic fever and dengue shock syndrome. There is no approved antiviral; management is supportive fluid therapy in the critical phase.

Dengue Virus (DENV1–4)

Enveloped positive-sense ssRNA flavivirus with a ~10.7 kb genome encoding a single polyprotein (~3,391 aa) cleaved into 3 structural proteins (C, prM/M, E) and 7 non-structural proteins (NS1–NS5). The 90 E-protein dimers arranged in a herringbone pattern on the icosahedral virion mediate receptor binding via DC-SIGN, AXL, and mannose receptors on dendritic cells. NS5 is the RNA-dependent RNA polymerase and a potent IFN antagonist — it directly degrades STAT2, blunting interferon signaling. NS1, secreted at up to 50 µg/mL into blood during acute infection, disrupts the endothelial glycocalyx and activates complement, driving the vascular leak that defines dengue hemorrhagic fever. Antibody-dependent enhancement (ADE) in secondary heterotypic infection dramatically amplifies viral replication in monocytes/macrophages via FcγR-mediated entry.

Infection Mechanism

1 · Mosquito inoculation and dendritic cell entry

Aedes aegypti deposits DENV-containing saliva into the dermis during a blood meal. Skin-resident immature dendritic cells (Langerhans cells) are the primary initial targets, infected via DC-SIGN (CD209), AXL receptor tyrosine kinase, and the mannose receptor. Infected DCs replicate the virus, migrate to draining lymph nodes, and seed the bloodstream — establishing primary viremia typically by days 4–5 post-bite.

2 · Endocytosis, E-protein rearrangement, and RNA release

After receptor-mediated endocytosis, endosomal acidification (pH <6.5) triggers E-protein conformational change from antiparallel dimers to homotrimers, inserting the hydrophobic fusion loop into the endosomal membrane and driving lipid bilayer merger. The nucleocapsid is released into the cytoplasm and the +ssRNA genome is directly translated as a single large polyprotein cleaved co- and post-translationally by host signal peptidase and viral NS2B/NS3 protease.

3 · Replication and virion assembly on ER membranes

NS5 (RdRp) and NS3 (helicase) form the replication complex on ER-derived membrane invaginations. Genomic +ssRNA is copied via a (−)ssRNA intermediate. New virions assemble at the ER membrane as immature prM/E particles; furin protease cleavage of prM in the trans-Golgi produces mature infectious virions secreted by exocytosis.

4 · Antibody-dependent enhancement (ADE) in secondary infection

In a second infection with a different serotype, cross-reactive IgG from the primary infection opsonizes new-serotype virions but cannot neutralise them at sub-neutralising concentrations. FcγRI/II receptors on monocytes and macrophages recognise these IgG-coated virions and mediate uptake without neutralisation — resulting in 100–1,000-fold higher intracellular viral loads. Cross-reactive memory T cells also recognise heterotypic peptides with lower affinity, producing abundant IFN-γ and TNF-α but killing infected cells inefficiently, amplifying the systemic inflammatory response. ADE is the dominant mechanism of dengue hemorrhagic fever.

5 · NS5-mediated STAT2 degradation blocks IFN signalling

NS5 directly binds STAT2 and recruits the UPS (ubiquitin-proteasome system) to target it for degradation, blocking JAK-STAT signalling downstream of both IFN-α/β and IFN-γ receptors. NS2B/NS3 protease additionally disrupts STING-mediated cGAS sensing in some cellular contexts. The result is a broad suppression of ISG induction and a wide-open window of viral amplification during the first 4–6 days of illness.

6 · NS1 hexamer disrupts the endothelial glycocalyx

Secreted NS1 hexamer accumulates in plasma at 1–50 µg/mL during acute dengue. NS1 directly activates TLR4 on endothelial cells and triggers complement (generating C3a and C5a anaphylatoxins), disrupting the endothelial glycocalyx and massively increasing vascular permeability independent of direct endothelial cell infection. Activated T cells and monocytes further release IL-8, MCP-1, and TNF-α. NS1-driven leak is the proximate cause of dengue hemorrhagic fever.

Treatment & Prevention

AntiviralsNone approved. Celgosivir (iminosugar, inhibits α-glucosidase I/II needed for E-protein folding) reached Phase II but did not meet endpoints. NS5 RdRp inhibitors and NS2B/NS3 protease inhibitors remain in early clinical development as of 2026.
Supportive careCornerstone of DHF/DSS management. Aggressive IV crystalloid (0.9% NaCl or Ringer’s lactate) during critical phase (days 3–7, especially at fever defervescence when NS1-driven leak peaks). Monitor haematocrit every 4–6 hours and urine output hourly. Avoid NSAIDs (platelet dysfunction + GI bleeding). Platelet transfusion rarely indicated unless <10,000/µL or active bleeding.
Dengvaxia (CYD-TDV)Sanofi Pasteur live attenuated chimeric vaccine (dengue structural proteins on yellow fever 17D backbone); covers all 4 serotypes. FDA-approved 2019 for children 9–16 years with confirmed prior dengue infection only — seronegative recipients face increased DHF risk on breakthrough infection due to vaccine-primed sub-neutralising antibodies mimicking secondary ADE. Requires pre-vaccination serology (ELISA or rapid test for anti-dengue IgG).
TAK-003 (Qdenga)Takeda live attenuated tetravalent vaccine (DENV2 backbone expressing prM/E of all 4 serotypes); EMA-approved 2022 for ages 4+; does not require pre-vaccination serostatus testing; Phase III TIDES trial: 84% efficacy against symptomatic dengue, 61% against hospitalisation over 4.5-year follow-up.
Vector controlEliminate Aedes breeding sites (standing water). Wolbachia-infected mosquito release programs reduce vector competence by 40–70% and achieved >75% reduction in dengue incidence in Yogyakarta, Indonesia (AWED trial, 2021) and Medellin, Colombia. Sterile insect technique (SIT) and gene-drive approaches in development.
