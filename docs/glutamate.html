Glutamate (Glu)

The brain's primary excitatory drive — glutamate mediates fast synaptic transmission at ~80% of CNS synapses and, via NMDA coincidence detection, provides the molecular basis of long-term potentiation and memory formation.

MW 147.13 g/mol. Non-essential amino acid; CNS pool synthesised from glutamine via mitochondrial glutaminase — an obligate astrocyte-neuron partnership. Three ionotropic receptor families (AMPA, NMDA, kainate) plus 8 metabotropic GPCRs (mGluR1–8). NMDA receptor Mg²⁺ block provides voltage-gated coincidence detection. Excitotoxic over-activation drives ischemic neuronal death and ALS.

Glutamate (Glu)

Class: Dicarboxylic amino acid  ·  Principal excitatory neurotransmitter  |  Synthesis: Glutaminase (presynaptic mitochondria)  |  Reuptake: EAAT1–5  |  Ionotropic receptors: AMPA, NMDA, Kainate  |  Metabotropic: mGluR1–8

Glutamate (L-glutamic acid; Glu) is the most abundant neurotransmitter in the mammalian brain, mediating fast excitatory synaptic transmission at approximately 80% of all CNS synapses. It spans the full range from the molecular basis of intelligence — through the NMDA receptor's role as a coincidence detector underlying long-term potentiation (LTP) and memory — to the mechanism of ischemic brain death via excitotoxicity. Three families of ionotropic glutamate receptors (AMPA: fast EPSPs; NMDA: Ca²⁺-permeable coincidence detector; kainate: presynaptic modulation) and eight metabotropic mGluR GPCRs (mGluR1–8) mediate its diverse signalling. Astrocytes provide the crucial glutamine-glutamate shuttle that recycles released glutamate and maintains the CNS pool separate from metabolic glutamate.

Glu L-glutamate glutamic acid excitatory amino acid (EAA)

Overview & Structure

Glutamate is a dicarboxylic α-amino acid (formula C₅H₉NO₄; MW 147.13 g/mol) carrying a net negative charge at physiological pH (γ-carboxylate pKa ~4.1, fully deprotonated at pH 7.4). It cannot cross the blood-brain barrier efficiently — the CNS pool is synthesised locally from glutamine supplied by perisynaptic astrocytes, forming the glutamine-glutamate cycle.

Intraneuronally, glutamate is compartmentalised: cytosolic glutamate is ~10 mM (used for metabolism), but only vesicular glutamate (packaged by VGLUT1/2/3) is available for synaptic release. This compartmentalisation ensures that metabolic and signalling pools remain distinct.

Mechanism of Action — AMPA, NMDA, and LTP

  FAST EXCITATORY TRANSMISSION  (AMPA receptor — millisecond timescale)
  ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  Glutamate release → AMPA (GluA1-4 tetramers) binds
        │  Na⁺ influx + K⁺ efflux → fast EPSC
        ▼
  Postsynaptic membrane depolarises → summates → reaches threshold → AP

  NMDA COINCIDENCE DETECTION  (memory gate — voltage + ligand dual requirement)
  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  At resting potential (−70 mV):
        NMDA channel pore blocked by Mg²⁺ (1 mM extracellular)
        Even with glutamate + glycine co-agonist bound → CHANNEL CLOSED

  Simultaneous requirements for NMDA opening:
  1. Glutamate (presynaptic activity) binds GluN2 subunit
  2. Glycine / D-serine (co-agonist) binds GluN1 subunit
  3. Membrane depolarisation (~−30 mV) → expels Mg²⁺ block (postsynaptic activity)
        │
        ▼
  Ca²⁺ floods through NMDA channel
        │
        ▼
  CaMKII (Ca²⁺/calmodulin kinase II) activated
        ├─ Phosphorylates GluA1 Ser831 → ↑AMPA channel conductance
        ├─ Drives AMPA receptor insertion into postsynaptic density (PSD)
        └─ Recruits AMPA from recycling endosomes → ↑synaptic AMPA → LTP
        │
        ▼
  LTP: synapse is stronger for future activation — MEMORY ENCODED
  ("Neurons that fire together, wire together" — Hebb's rule, NMDA mechanism)

  EXCITOTOXICITY  (NMDA over-activation in ischemia)
  ────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  Ischemia → O₂/glucose deprivation → reversal of EAATs → massive Glu release
        │  Sustained NMDA activation → Ca²⁺ overload
        ▼
  Calpains, phospholipases, nNOS (NO•) → mitochondrial permeability transition
        │
        ▼  Neuronal death in infarct core and penumbra

NMDA-hypofunction paradox: Too much NMDA activation (excitotoxicity in ischemia) and too little NMDA activation (NMDA hypofunction in schizophrenia) both cause disease. The difference: excitotoxicity involves sustained NMDA current in neurons with high baseline activity (stroke), while NMDA hypofunction preferentially silences fast-spiking PV interneurons (which require high tonic NMDA drive) → loss of inhibitory control → disinhibited pyramidal cell activity that paradoxically increases dopamine release. Ketamine at anaesthetic doses → excitotoxic concern; ketamine at sub-anaesthetic doses → rapidly reverses depression (AMPA-mediated synaptic potentiation).
