Respiratory system

Airways, lungs, chest wall, and diaphragm forming the gas-exchange apparatus: ~6–8 L/min ventilation delivered to 300–500 million alveoli across ~70 m² of surface. Coupled to the cardiovascular system via the pulmonary circuit. Controlled by medullary respiratory centres (pre-Bötzinger complex) and chemoreceptors responding to PaCO₂, PaO₂, and arterial pH. Regulates acid-base balance and participates in voice production and immune defense.

Overview

The respiratory system is the body's gas-exchange apparatus — the integrated set of structures that moves O₂ from ambient air into the bloodstream and removes CO₂ in the opposite direction, maintaining the arterial blood gas tensions (PaO₂ ~100 mmHg, PaCO₂ ~40 mmHg, pH ~7.40) essential for normal cellular function. It comprises five integrated components:

Upper airway

Nose, mouth, pharynx, larynx: filter, warm, and humidify inspired air before it reaches the gas-exchange surface.

Conducting airways

Trachea → bronchi → bronchioles (generations 0–16): transport air; no gas exchange; anatomical dead space ~150 mL.

Lungs and alveoli

The gas-exchange parenchyma: ~300–500 million alveoli, ~70 m² surface area. Covered in the Lung entry.

Chest wall and diaphragm

The mechanical pump: inspiratory muscles generate negative intrapleural pressure to drive air inward. Diaphragm: ~70% of resting inspiratory effort (phrenic nerve C3–C5).

The respiratory system cannot function in isolation — it is tightly coupled to the cardiovascular system via the pulmonary circuit, and its rhythm is driven by medullary and pontine respiratory centres modulated by chemoreceptors detecting PaO₂, PaCO₂, and arterial pH.

Mechanics of Breathing — Pressure and Volume

Breathing is driven by pressure gradients generated by the respiratory muscles. At rest, inspiration is active (diaphragm and external intercostals contract → chest volume expands → intrapleural pressure falls from −5 to −8 cmH₂O → alveolar pressure falls below atmospheric → air flows in). Expiration at rest is passive — elastic recoil of lung and chest wall restores FRC.

  Inspiration (active)                Expiration (passive at rest)
  ─────────────────────────           ──────────────────────────────
  Diaphragm contracts                 Diaphragm relaxes
  ↓                                   ↓
  Thoracic volume ↑                   Thoracic volume ↓
  ↓                                   ↓
  Intrapleural pressure ↓             Lung elastic recoil
  (−5 → −8 cmH₂O)                    drives air out
  ↓                                   ↓
  Alveolar pressure ↓                 Alveolar pressure ↑
  (~−1 cmH₂O)                         (~+1 cmH₂O)
  ↓                                   ↓
  Air flows in                        Air flows out

The work of breathing has two components: overcoming elastic recoil (compliance work) and overcoming airway resistance (resistance work). In COPD (↑airway resistance, ↑air trapping, ↓elastic recoil) and pulmonary fibrosis (↓compliance, ↑elastic work), the work of breathing rises substantially, contributing to dyspnea and respiratory muscle fatigue.

Diaphragm failure (phrenic nerve injury in high cervical SCI at C3–C5, or demyelination in Guillain-Barré syndrome) requires mechanical ventilation. Inspiratory muscle weakness is assessed by maximal inspiratory pressure (MIP) — values <−30 cmH₂O suggest impending respiratory failure.

Respiratory Control — Medullary Centres and Chemoreceptors

The respiratory rhythm originates in brainstem centres and is continuously modulated by chemical feedback:

Pre-Bötzinger complex (VRG)

Ventral respiratory group in the medulla — the respiratory rhythm generator. Produces the ~12–20/min oscillatory inspiratory drive via pacemaker neurons. Output to phrenic and intercostal motor neurons.

Pneumotaxic centre (pontine)

Modulates breath timing; limits inspiratory duration; coordinates the switch between inspiration and expiration. Lesion → apneustic breathing (prolonged inspiratory effort).

Central chemoreceptors (medullary)

Respond to PCO₂/pH in CSF. Most powerful ventilatory driver — a 1 mmHg rise in PaCO₂ increases minute ventilation by ~2–3 L/min. CO₂ crosses blood-brain barrier; CO₂ + H₂O → H₂CO₃ → H⁺ + HCO₃⁻ → detected as pH change.

Peripheral chemoreceptors (carotid/aortic body)

Respond to PaO₂ (<60 mmHg triggers significant activation), PaCO₂, and pH. Primary O₂ sensors. Important in altitude acclimatization and chronic hypercapnia (COPD: dependence on hypoxic drive — controversial but clinically relevant).

The ventilatory control loop maintains PACO₂ at ~40 mmHg across a 20–30-fold change in metabolic rate (rest → maximal exercise) — a remarkable regulatory achievement accomplished primarily by increasing both respiratory rate and tidal volume.

Ventilation and Gas Exchange

At rest, the respiratory system delivers ~5–6 L/min of alveolar ventilation to maintain normal blood gas tensions:

ParameterRestMaximal exercise
Respiratory rate (RR)12–16 breaths/min40–60 breaths/min
Tidal volume (VT)~500 mL~3,000 mL
Minute ventilation (V̇E)~6–8 L/min100–200 L/min
Alveolar ventilation (V̇A)~5.25 L/min~80+ L/min
O₂ uptake (VO₂)~250 mL/min3,000–6,000 mL/min (trained)

Alveolar ventilation drives alveolar PCO₂:

PACO₂ = (VCO₂ / V̇A) × 0.863
VCO₂ = CO₂ production (mL/min), V̇A = alveolar ventilation (L/min). Hypoventilation → ↑PACO₂ → respiratory acidosis

The alveolar gas equation determines the maximum achievable PaO₂:

PAO₂ = FiO₂ × (Patm − PH₂O) − PACO₂ / RQ
FiO₂ = 0.21 (room air); PH₂O = 47 mmHg at 37°C; RQ = respiratory quotient (~0.8). A-a gradient = PAO₂ − PaO₂; normal <15 mmHg in young adults

Acid-Base Regulation

The respiratory system is the fastest acid-base regulator — it can change PaCO₂ within seconds to minutes (renal compensation takes hours to days). The Henderson-Hasselbalch relationship:

pH = 6.1 + log([HCO₃⁻] / (0.0306 × PaCO₂))
Normal: pH 7.35–7.45; PaCO₂ 35–45 mmHg; HCO₃⁻ 22–26 mEq/L

Pathology

COPD

Smoke-induced airway inflammation (neutrophils, macrophages) + emphysema (alveolar wall destruction → ↓surface area → ↓DLCO → airflow obstruction from ↓elastic recoil + dynamic collapse) + mucus hypersecretion (chronic bronchitis component). Spirometry: FEV₁/FVC <0.70; ↑RV/TLC (air trapping). 3.2 million deaths/year globally. Management: bronchodilators (LABA/LAMA), ICS in high-risk, smoking cessation, pulmonary rehab, supplemental O₂ (if PaO₂ <55 mmHg), lung volume reduction or transplant in selected severe cases.

Asthma

Eosinophilic airway inflammation (Th2 cytokines: IL-4, IL-5, IL-13) + mast cell activation → bronchospasm + mucus plugging → reversible airflow obstruction (FEV₁ improves >12% with bronchodilator). Triggers: aeroallergens, exercise, cold, infections, aspirin/NSAIDs, occupational sensitizers. Treatment: ICS + SABA (reliever); add LABA, LAMA, LTRA in step-up; biologics (anti-IgE, anti-IL-5/5R, anti-IL-4Rα, anti-TSLP) for severe eosinophilic or allergic phenotypes.

Pneumonia

Pathogen-induced alveolar consolidation → V/Q mismatch → hypoxemia. Community-acquired: S. pneumoniae (most common), H. influenzae, atypicals (Mycoplasma, Legionella), viruses (SARS-CoV-2, influenza). Hospital-acquired and ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP): Gram-negatives (Pseudomonas, Klebsiella), S. aureus (MRSA). PSI/PORT or CURB-65 score guides outpatient vs. inpatient vs. ICU decision.

ARDS

Diffuse alveolar damage from any systemic or pulmonary insult (sepsis, pneumonia, aspiration, trauma, pancreatitis): cytokine storm → capillary leak → alveolar flooding → bilateral infiltrates → severe refractory hypoxemia (PaO₂/FiO₂ <300). Berlin definition: bilateral infiltrates; non-hydrostatic; onset within 7 days. Mortality: mild 27%, moderate 32%, severe 45%. Lung-protective ventilation (6 mL/kg IBW, Pplat <30 cmH₂O) is the only intervention with robust mortality benefit; prone positioning reduces mortality in severe ARDS.

Lung Cancer

Leading cause of cancer death (~1.8 million deaths/year). Staging drives management: early (IA-IIA) → surgical resection (lobectomy or segmentectomy); locally advanced (IIIA/B) → concurrent chemoradiation ± durvalumab; metastatic (IV) → systemic therapy guided by molecular profiling (EGFR, ALK, ROS1, BRAF, KRAS G12C, MET exon 14, NTRK, PD-L1). Low-dose CT screening in high-risk smokers reduces lung cancer mortality 20% (NLST).

Obstructive Sleep Apnea (OSA)

Repetitive upper airway collapse during sleep → cyclical hypoxemia, hypercapnia, arousal → fragmented sleep → daytime somnolence (Epworth Sleepiness Scale), morning headaches. Cardiovascular sequelae: systemic hypertension (most common, ~50% of OSA patients), AF, heart failure (HFpEF), pulmonary hypertension. Diagnosis: polysomnography (AHI >5/h). Treatment: CPAP (first-line); mandibular advancement devices; positional therapy; weight loss; upper airway surgery in selected cases.

Pulmonary Fibrosis

Progressive fibrotic replacement of alveolar parenchyma → restriction (↓TLC, ↓FVC, normal or ↑FEV₁/FVC) + ↓DLCO + progressive hypoxemia. IPF (UIP pattern): median survival 3–5 years; anti-fibrotics (nintedanib, pirfenidone) slow FVC decline; lung transplant for eligible patients. Secondary causes: connective tissue disease (RA, SSc, myositis-ILD), drug toxicity (amiodarone, bleomycin, methotrexate), hypersensitivity pneumonitis, radiation.
