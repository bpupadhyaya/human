Overview

The human eye is an optical and sensory organ organized into three concentric coats: the outer fibrous tunic (sclera posteriorly, cornea anteriorly), the middle vascular uveal tract (choroid, ciliary body, iris), and the inner neurosensory retina. Transparent media — the cornea, aqueous humor, crystalline lens, and vitreous body — form a refractive system that brings parallel rays of light to focus on the fovea centralis at the center of the macula. The cornea contributes approximately +43 diopters (about two-thirds of total refractive power) while the relaxed crystalline lens adds +19 diopters; ciliary muscle contraction (accommodation) can increase lens power by up to +15 diopters in a young adult.

The retina is a displaced piece of brain — embryologically an outgrowth of the diencephalon — organized into ten histological layers that process light signals through a sequence of photoreceptors, interneurons, and retinal ganglion cells (RGCs) whose axons form the optic nerve. A continuous supply of aqueous humor, secreted by the ciliary epithelium and drained via the trabecular meshwork and Schlemm's canal, maintains intraocular pressure (IOP) at 10–21 mmHg, sustaining the globe's shape and optical geometry.

Structural Anatomy — Three Coats

Outer Coat: Sclera and Cornea

The sclera forms the posterior five-sixths of the fibrous coat — an opaque, avascular shell of interwoven type I/III collagen bundles that provides mechanical rigidity and attachment for the six extraocular muscles. At the posterior pole the sclera is perforated by the optic nerve (lamina cribrosa) and by posterior ciliary arteries and nerves. The cornea comprises the anterior one-sixth and has five layers:

  • Epithelium (5–7 cell layers, ~50 µm): stratified non-keratinized squamous; regenerates rapidly from limbal stem cells; provides smooth optical surface via tear film
  • Bowman layer (~12 µm): acellular condensed collagen; not regenerated after injury
  • Stroma (~450 µm, ~90% of corneal thickness): orthogonally arranged collagen type I/V lamellae with uniform fibril spacing (~31 nm center-to-center) maintained by small leucine-rich proteoglycans (keratocan, lumican); this precise lattice produces destructive interference of scattered light — the molecular basis of corneal transparency
  • Descemet's membrane (~10–15 µm): thick basement membrane secreted by endothelium throughout life; contains collagen type IV/VIII
  • Endothelium (monolayer, ~5 µm): hexagonal cells with Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase + carbonic anhydrase pumps that actively export ions from stroma into anterior chamber, maintaining corneal dehydration (~78% water) and therefore transparency; non-regenerative — density declines from ~3,500/mm² at birth to ~2,000–2,500/mm² in adults; Fuchs endothelial dystrophy results from endothelial failure

Middle Coat: Uveal Tract

The uvea consists of three continuous structures. The choroid is a highly vascular layer between sclera and RPE containing the choriocapillaris — a lobular capillary network that supplies the outer retina and photoreceptors (which lack their own vasculature). Choroidal blood flow is among the highest per unit mass in the body (~2,000 mL/min/100 g). The ciliary body has two functions: the ciliary muscle (smooth, innervated by CNIII parasympathetics via the ciliary ganglion) controls accommodation by altering zonular tension on the lens; the ciliary epithelium (double-layered, non-pigmented inner + pigmented outer) secretes aqueous humor. The iris is a pigmented diaphragm with a central pupillary aperture; the pupillary sphincter (CNIII) and dilator (sympathetic, superior cervical ganglion) muscles regulate light entering the eye.

Inner Coat: Retina

The neurosensory retina has ten histological layers enumerated from the inner (vitreal) surface outward:

  1. Internal limiting membrane (ILM): basement membrane of Müller cell end-feet; interface with vitreous
  2. Nerve fiber layer (NFL): axons of retinal ganglion cells traveling toward the optic disc
  3. Ganglion cell layer (GCL): RGC nuclei — ~1.2 million cells; 30+ subtypes encode motion, color, contrast
  4. Inner plexiform layer (IPL): synapses between bipolar, amacrine, and ganglion cells
  5. Inner nuclear layer (INL): nuclei of bipolar cells (ON/OFF), amacrine cells (~30 subtypes), horizontal cells
  6. Outer plexiform layer (OPL): synapses between photoreceptors, bipolar cells, and horizontal cells
  7. Outer nuclear layer (ONL): photoreceptor nuclei (rods and cones)
  8. External limiting membrane (ELM): adherens junctions between Müller cells and photoreceptor inner segments
  9. Photoreceptor outer segments (OS): rod OS (50–100 discs stacked, rhodopsin embedded) and cone OS (shorter, conical)
  10. Retinal pigment epithelium (RPE): cuboidal monolayer; phagocytoses shed photoreceptor OS (~30 discs/rod/day); regenerates 11-cis-retinal; constitutes outer blood-retinal barrier; expresses VEGF and PEDF

The macula (3.5 mm diameter, centered 3 mm temporal to the optic disc) is specialized for high-acuity color vision; it is enriched with xanthophyll carotenoids (lutein/zeaxanthin) that filter high-energy blue light. The fovea centralis (0.35 mm) is cone-only — all other layers displaced centrifugally (foveal pit), maximizing direct photon-to-cone access. Cone density at the foveal center: ~199,000/mm².

Photoreceptor distribution: approximately 120 million rods concentrated in the mid-periphery (peak density ~20° from fovea) for scotopic (dim-light) vision, and approximately 6 million cones in three subtypes with peak spectral sensitivities at ~420 nm (S, blue-sensitive), ~530 nm (M, green-sensitive), and ~560 nm (L, red-sensitive). The L:M:S cone ratio is approximately 10:5:1, though highly variable between individuals.

Crystalline Lens and Accommodation

The crystalline lens is a biconvex, avascular, transparent structure (~9 mm diameter × 4 mm thick in resting state) suspended behind the iris by zonular fibers (ciliary zonule) radiating from the ciliary body. Its transparency relies on three families of structural proteins — α-crystallins (chaperones that prevent protein aggregation), β-crystallins, and γ-crystallins — arranged in a gradient of increasing refractive index from cortex to nucleus. With age, crystallin proteins undergo post-translational modifications (deamidation, glycation, crosslinking by advanced glycation end-products and UV-generated radicals) that increase opacity — the biochemical basis of senile cataract.

Accommodation mechanism: Ciliary muscle contraction (circular and meridional fibers) reduces zonular tension → lens rounds up under its elastic capsule → curvature and refractive power increase → near-focus. With age, the lens nucleus hardens (presbyopia) and reduces the amplitude of accommodation from ~15 D at age 8 to <1 D by age 55.

The vitreous humor fills the posterior segment (~4 mL volume). Composition: ~99% water, collagen type II fibrils forming a loose scaffold, hyaluronic acid chains (sodium hyaluronate) providing viscoelasticity, and sparse hyalocytes. It transmits light, provides structural support to the retina, and acts as a reservoir for growth factors including PDGF-BB. With age, the vitreous undergoes syneresis (liquid pockets form) and eventual posterior vitreous detachment (PVD).

Phototransduction — Rhodopsin Cascade

Phototransduction in rod photoreceptors converts single photons into a graded hyperpolarizing receptor potential with extraordinary amplification (~10⁵-fold). In darkness, cGMP-gated cation channels (CNG channels) are held open by high cytoplasmic cGMP, admitting an inward Na⁺/Ca²⁺ "dark current" — the rod is tonically depolarized (~−40 mV) and continuously releasing glutamate at its synaptic terminal.

Light activation sequence:

  1. Photon absorption isomerizes 11-cis-retinal → all-trans-retinal within the opsin apoprotein, generating activated rhodopsin (R*)
  2. R* catalytically activates transducin (Gαt) — GDP → GTP exchange; each R* activates ~500 transducin molecules
  3. Gαt-GTP activates cGMP phosphodiesterase 6 (PDE6), which hydrolyzes cGMP → 5'-GMP
  4. cGMP concentration falls → CNG channels close → Na⁺/Ca²⁺ influx ceases → rod hyperpolarizes to ~−70 mV
  5. Hyperpolarization decreases glutamate release at the rod spherule → ON-bipolar cells (which express metabotropic mGluR6) depolarize (sign-inverted); OFF-bipolars (AMPA/kainate) hyperpolarize

Recovery and adaptation: Rhodopsin kinase (GRK1) phosphorylates R* at multiple C-terminal serine/threonine residues → arrestin binds, quenching activation. PDE6 GTPase activity (via RGS9-1/Gβ5) terminates Gαt signalling. Guanylate cyclase (RetGC-1/2), activated by guanylate cyclase-activating proteins (GCAPs) in response to falling Ca²⁺, resynthesizes cGMP to restore dark current. The entire single-photon response resolves in ~300 ms in humans.

Visual cycle (retinoid cycle): After photoactivation, all-trans-retinal dissociates from opsin and is reduced to all-trans-retinol (by NADPH-dependent retinol dehydrogenase), then transported to the RPE where LRAT (lecithin:retinol acyltransferase) esterifies it, and RPE65 (retinal pigment epithelium 65 kDa protein, an isomerohydrolase) converts all-trans-retinyl ester → 11-cis-retinol → 11-cis-retinal, which diffuses back to photoreceptors. Mutations in RPE65 cause Leber congenital amaurosis (LCA2); voretigene neparvovec (AAV2 gene therapy) restores vision.

Aqueous Humor and Intraocular Pressure

Aqueous humor is a clear ultrafiltrate-like fluid produced at ~2–3 µL/min by the double-layered ciliary epithelium. Ion transport driving secretion: Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase on the non-pigmented epithelium transports Na⁺ into the posterior chamber, creating an osmotic gradient; carbonic anhydrase II/IV in ciliary epithelium generates HCO₃⁻, contributing to net fluid flow. Aqueous flows from the posterior chamber → through the pupil → anterior chamber → trabecular meshwork outflow (~85%) or uveoscleral (unconventional) outflow (~15%).

IOP = F / C + Pe
F = aqueous production rate; C = outflow facility (ease of drainage); Pe = episcleral venous pressure (~8 mmHg). Normal IOP 10–21 mmHg. Raised IOP (>21 mmHg) is the primary modifiable risk factor for glaucoma.

Trabecular outflow: Aqueous passes through the trabecular meshwork (corneoscleral and juxtacanalicular tissue) → Schlemm's canal → collector channels → episcleral veins. The juxtacanalicular tissue provides most of the resistance; its extracellular matrix (fibronectin, laminin, versican) is remodeled by matrix metalloproteinases. Prostaglandin analog eye drops (latanoprost, bimatoprost) reduce IOP primarily by increasing uveoscleral outflow via FP receptor activation → MMP upregulation → ciliary muscle relaxation.

Blood Supply and Blood-Retinal Barrier

The eye has a dual blood supply reflecting the two functional zones of the retina. The inner retina (RGC through INL) is supplied by the central retinal artery, a branch of the ophthalmic artery (from the internal carotid), which enters the optic nerve and branches into superior/inferior nasal/temporal arterioles visible on fundoscopy. The outer retina and photoreceptors are avascular and rely on diffusion from the underlying choriocapillaris (a lobular fenestrated capillary network within the choroid); this is the highest perfusion rate per unit tissue in the body and is not autoregulated.

The blood-retinal barrier (BRB) exists in two anatomically distinct locations:

  • Inner BRB: tight junctions (claudin-5, occludin, ZO-1) between retinal vascular endothelial cells, supported by pericyte coverage and Müller glia endfeet; analogous to the blood-brain barrier. Breakdown occurs in diabetic retinopathy (VEGF-mediated phosphorylation of tight junction proteins) and vein occlusions.
  • Outer BRB: tight junctions between RPE cells; restricts bidirectional movement between the choriocapillaris and the neural retina; maintains the subretinal microenvironment. Disrupted in AMD (RPE dysfunction → drusen accumulation → BRB breakdown → CNV).
