Plasmodium falciparum

Humanity's deadliest parasite — 249 million cases and 608,000 deaths in 2022. PfEMP1 cytoadherence in brain microvasculature defines the lethal difference between uncomplicated fever and cerebral malaria.

Plasmodium falciparum is an apicomplexan with a complex two-host life cycle: asymptomatic hepatic schizogony (5–7 days) followed by synchronised 48-hour erythrocytic cycles that drive fever. After 12–16 hours intra-erythrocytically, infected red blood cells (iRBCs) express PfEMP1 — encoded by ~60 var genes — on their surface, mediating sequestration in brain, heart, and placental microvasculature. Artemisinin-based combination therapy (ACT) remains highly effective, though partial resistance is spreading from Southeast Asia.

Plasmodium falciparum

Apicomplexan protozoan with a two-host life cycle: definitive host (Anopheles mosquito, sexual reproduction) and human intermediate host (asexual amplification). Sporozoites travel to liver within 30 minutes of inoculation; hepatic schizogony produces 10,000–30,000 merozoites per infected hepatocyte in 5–7 days. Blood-stage merozoites invade erythrocytes via MSP1, AMA1/RON2, and EBA175; synchronised schizont rupture every 48 hours drives fever spikes via GPI toxins and TNF-α. After 12–16 hours, infected RBCs express PfEMP1 (∼350 kDa; ~60 var genes, one expressed at a time) on the RBC surface — mediating cytoadherence to ICAM-1, CD36, and PECAM-1 on vascular endothelium. Cerebral sequestration causes microvascular obstruction, endothelial activation, BBB disruption, coma, and 15–20% mortality despite treatment. Partial artemisinin resistance (kelch13 mutations) emerging in Southeast Asia and spreading to Africa.

Infection Mechanism & Pathogenesis

1 · Hepatic phase (5–7 days, asymptomatic)

Anopheles female inoculates sporozoites; they travel to liver via portal circulation within 30 minutes. Sporozoites invade Kupffer cells (liver macrophages), traverse through them, then invade hepatocytes using CD81 and SR-B1 receptors. Inside a parasitophorous vacuole, hepatic schizogony produces 10,000–30,000 merozoites per infected hepatocyte. Merosomes (merozoite-filled hepatocyte blebs) bud into the bloodstream and are transported to lung capillaries, releasing merozoites. The entire hepatic phase is clinically silent — no symptoms, no fever — making pre-erythrocytic vaccine targets attractive.

2 · Erythrocytic cycles and fever (48 h, symptomatic)

Merozoites invade RBCs via multiple redundant invasion ligands: MSP1 (merozoite surface protein 1), AMA1/RON2 complex (tight junction formation), EBA175 (binds glycophorin A on RBC surface). Inside the RBC, the parasite degrades hemoglobin in its food vacuole, converting toxic heme to crystalline hemozoin. Synchronised schizont rupture every 48 hours releases GPI toxins and hemozoin that activate TLR2/TLR4 and NLRP3 on macrophages → massive TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β release → high fever spikes (tertian pattern), rigors, headache, myalgia.

3 · Cytoadherence and sequestration (cerebral malaria)

After 12–16 hours of intra-erythrocytic development, iRBCs export PfEMP1 to the RBC surface via the PEXEL-mediated export pathway and the MAHRP1/KAHRP knob structure. PfEMP1 binds ICAM-1, CD36, PECAM-1, and endothelial protein C receptor (EPCR) on cerebrovascular endothelium — anchoring iRBCs to vessel walls and removing them from splenic clearance. In cerebral malaria: sequestration in brain microvasculature → mechanical obstruction + endothelial activation (ICAM-1 upregulation, NF-κB) + blood-brain barrier disruption → cerebral oedema, raised ICP, coma, seizures. Case fatality 15–20% even with IV artesunate.

4 · var gene antigenic variation

~60 var genes per haploid genome; only one is transcribed at a time (monoallelic expression maintained by epigenetic silencing: H3K9me3 marks at silent loci; active locus associates with perinuclear transcription zone). Immune pressure from host anti-PfEMP1 antibodies selects for iRBCs expressing a different var allele; switching frequency ~10⁻²/cell/generation. This mechanism ensures that acquired immunity to PfEMP1 variants takes years of cumulative exposure to develop — explaining why children bear the highest mortality burden in endemic areas.

Treatment & Prophylaxis

Artemisinin-Based Combination Therapy (ACT) — Standard of Care

Artesunate + amodiaquine (Africa), artesunate + lumefantrine (AL, Coartem — global), artesunate + mefloquine (SE Asia), dihydroartemisinin-piperaquine (DHA-PPQ). Artemisinin acts via ROS generation from heme-activated endoperoxide bridge — kills ring-stage and young trophozoites within hours. Partner drug kills remaining asexual parasites and provides post-treatment prophylaxis. ACT is >95% effective against susceptible strains.

IV Artesunate — Severe Malaria

IV artesunate (2.4 mg/kg at 0, 12, 24 h, then daily) is WHO recommended for severe malaria; superior to IV quinine in reducing mortality in RCTs (AQUAMAT trial); transition to oral ACT when patient can tolerate; follow with single primaquine dose to clear gametocytes in non-G6PD-deficient patients.

Chemoprophylaxis

Atovaquone-proguanil (Malarone): daily; start 1–2 days before travel; well tolerated. Doxycycline: daily; start 1–2 days before; avoid in pregnancy/children <8 years. Mefloquine: weekly; start 2–3 weeks before; neuropsychiatric side effects. Primaquine: prevents P. vivax/P. ovale relapse (must test G6PD first).

RTS,S/AS01 Vaccine (Mosquirix) and R21/Matrix-M

RTS,S/AS01: WHO-approved 2021 for children in high-transmission areas; targets circumsporozoite protein (CSP); ~50% efficacy against clinical malaria over 4-dose schedule; approved for routine childhood immunisation in Africa. R21/Matrix-M (Serum Institute/Oxford): ~75% efficacy at 1 year in Phase 3 trial; WHO approval 2023; higher efficacy, simpler supply chain; being rolled out across Africa.
