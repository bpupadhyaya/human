Axonal Transport

Neurons are uniquely challenged cells: their axons extend up to 1 metre from the soma, yet nearly all proteins and organelles are synthesised in the cell body and must be delivered distally — or retrogradely returned — by active molecular motor systems running on microtubule rails.

Kinesin-1 motors drive anterograde fast transport at ~0.8 µm/s; cytoplasmic dynein drives retrograde transport; slow transport delivers cytoskeletal proteins over days. Failure of axonal transport is an early and causative event in ALS, Alzheimer’s disease, Huntington’s disease, and multiple forms of Charcot–Marie–Tooth neuropathy.

Axonal Transport

Axonal transport is the microtubule-based long-distance delivery system of neurons. Kinesin-1 (KIF5 family) carries mitochondria, synaptic vesicle precursors, APP, and neurotrophins anterograde at up to 400 mm/day; cytoplasmic dynein-1 with dynactin returns endosomes, autophagosomes, and signalling endosomes retrogradely. Miro/TRAK adaptor complexes regulate mitochondrial arrest at active synapses via Ca²⁺ sensing. Disruption of any motor, adaptor, or cargo-tethering complex underlies neurodegeneration in ALS, Alzheimer's, Huntington's, and hereditary neuropathies.

Overview

Neurons are highly polarised cells with two structurally and functionally distinct compartments: the somatodendritic domain and the axon. The soma synthesises the vast majority of neuronal proteins and organelles, yet the axon terminal — which may be up to 1 metre away in spinal motor neurons or dorsal root ganglion projection neurons — requires continuous delivery of synaptic vesicle precursors, mitochondria, transmitter biosynthetic enzymes, and membrane proteins. Conversely, signals generated at the terminal (survival factors, stress sensors, damaged organelles) must be returned to the soma for transcriptional responses or proteolytic degradation.

This bidirectional logistics is accomplished by molecular motors walking along polarised microtubules. In axons, microtubules are uniformly oriented with their plus-ends pointing distally, providing directional information: plus-end-directed kinesins drive anterograde transport; minus-end-directed dynein drives retrograde transport. The entire axon is therefore navigable in both directions simultaneously, with different cargoes moving in opposite directions on the same microtubule rails, separated by cargo-specific motor and adaptor protein complexes.

Clinical relevance: Axonal transport defects precede motor neuron death in ALS models by weeks, and tau-mediated transport disruption is detectable before plaque formation in Alzheimer’s disease. Axonal transport is thus not merely a housekeeping process but a critical determinant of neuronal survival.

The distinction between fast and slow transport reflects both motor type and cargo association state: fast transport involves membrane-bound organelles with stably attached motors, while slow transport involves cytosolic and cytoskeletal proteins moving in brief, infrequent bouts of fast movement interspersed with prolonged pauses — the net movement is slow but the instantaneous motor velocity is the same.

Retrograde Signalling Endosomes (“Signalling Endosome Hypothesis”)

How does a motor neuron soma, metres away from its terminals, receive survival signals and stress information from the periphery? The answer is the signalling endosome: after target-derived neurotrophins (NGF, BDNF) bind their cognate Trk receptors at the axon terminal, the receptor–ligand complex is internalised into early endosomes. Rather than being immediately degraded, these endosomes are loaded onto dynein-dynactin by adaptor proteins (HOOK3, JIP3/JIP4) and transported retrogradely at 1–3 µm/s toward the soma.

The journey from a distal axon terminal to the soma of a DRG or motor neuron can take 12–24 hours. Throughout this transit the endosome continues signalling: TrkB remains phosphorylated within the endosomal membrane, and downstream effectors (MAPK/ERK, PI3K/Akt) remain active on the endosomal surface — producing a “sustained signal” that propagates from periphery to nucleus. On arrival at the soma, activated ERK and CREB-kinase complexes enter the nucleus and drive expression of survival genes (Bcl-2, BDNF itself, Arc). Failure of this system — as in axonal transport deficit models — causes neuronal atrophy and death even when target-derived factors are plentiful, explaining why long-axon neurons in the spinal cord, DRG, and retina are disproportionately vulnerable in neurodegenerative disease.

Pathology — Transport Defects in Neurodegeneration

ALS (Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis)

Axonal transport defects are among the earliest pathological findings in ALS motor neurons, preceding cell death by weeks in mouse models. SOD1 mutants: misfolded SOD1 impairs dynein retrograde transport; accumulates in axons. TDP-43 (TARDBP; mutated or mis-localised cytoplasmatically in ~97% of sporadic ALS): TDP-43 normally regulates mRNA stability including neurofilament mRNA and kinesin/dynein subunit mRNAs; cytoplasmic aggregates containing p62/ubiquitin sequester ribonucleoprotein complexes and block both mRNA granule transport and axonal protein synthesis. FUS mutations: similar RNA granule aggregation and anterograde transport defect. DCTN1 (p150glued) mutations: dominant motor neuron disease; Glued (dynactin) haploinsufficiency → dynein processivity defect → retrograde transport failure.

Alzheimer’s Disease

APP (amyloid precursor protein) is a kinesin-1 cargo; its cleavage by β-secretase (BACE1) and γ-secretase in transit vesicles generates Aβ peptides that are released into the synaptic cleft — suggesting that the axon terminal is a primary site of amyloidogenic processing. Tau hyperphosphorylation (by CDK5, GSK-3β, DYRK1A) causes tau to detach from microtubules and form oligomeric/fibrillar aggregates; simultaneously, detached tau competes with motor proteins for microtubule binding and cross-links adjacent microtubules, impairing kinesin processivity. In mouse models, axonal transport defects precede plaque and tangle pathology; reduction of kinesin-1 accelerates AD-like pathology, while restoring transport by inhibiting tau kinases is neuroprotective.

Huntington’s Disease

Mutant huntingtin (mHtt; polyglutamine expansion >36 repeats) physically tangles both kinesin and dynein motors, impairing their association with microtubules and reducing organelle run lengths. BDNF is the most vulnerable cargo: mHtt disrupts the association of BDNF-containing vesicles with kinesin and the mHtt/HAP1/p150glued complex required for BDNF anterograde transport in striatal neurons, leading to BDNF deprivation of the striatum — a primary mechanism of striatal neurodegeneration. Restoring BDNF transport by expressing constitutively active Htt-associated protein 1 (HAP1) rescues striatal neurons in HD mouse models.

Charcot–Marie–Tooth Neuropathy (Axonal Forms)

KIF1Bβ mutations (CMT2A2): loss-of-function in Kinesin-3 (KIF1B) impairs anterograde transport of synaptic vesicle precursors and mitochondria in peripheral axons. NEFL mutations (CMT2E): misassembled neurofilaments block axonal transport. DCTN1 (p150glued) mutations (HMN7B): dynactin point mutations (Gly59Ser) reduce microtubule-binding affinity of p150glued → dynein activation failure. GARS (CMT2D): glycyl-tRNA synthetase mutations disrupt aminoacyl-tRNA transport in axons. SMA (SMN1 deletions): SMN complex is required for assembly of snRNPs and also for localised axonal mRNA transport (SMN co-localises with mRNA granules containing β-actin and GAP-43 mRNAs in growth cones).

Spinal Muscular Atrophy (SMA)

Homozygous deletion/mutation of SMN1; ubiquitous SMN protein required for snRNP biogenesis, but axonal phenotype is prominent: SMN associates with mRNA granules in axons containing β-actin, GAP-43 (growth-associated protein 43), and cpg15 mRNAs via hnRNP-R interaction; reduced SMN impairs axonal mRNA transport and local translation at the NMJ, causing presynaptic terminal retraction before lower motor neuron cell death. Treatment: nusinersen (ASO targeting SMN2 exon 7 skipping), risdiplam (small-molecule SMN2 splicing modifier), onasemnogene abeparvovec (AAV9-SMN1 gene therapy).
