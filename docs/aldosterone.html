Aldosterone

The principal mineralocorticoid of the human adrenal cortex — a C21 steroid produced exclusively in the zona glomerulosa under RAAS and potassium control. Aldosterone acts via the mineralocorticoid receptor (MR, NR3C2) to regulate sodium retention, potassium excretion, and blood pressure homeostasis, while also driving maladaptive cardiovascular fibrosis when chronically elevated.

Scale: L02 Molecular MW: 360.44 g/mol Class: C21 mineralocorticoid steroid Receptor: NR3C2 (MR) Half-life: ~20 min (plasma)

Structure and Biosynthesis

Aldosterone is a C21 steroid with a unique C11–C18 aldehyde bridge (hemiacetal form) that distinguishes it from cortisol and confers >3000-fold greater mineralocorticoid potency. It is synthesized exclusively in the zona glomerulosa of the adrenal cortex — the outermost cortical layer, which lacks 17α-hydroxylase (CYP17A1) and therefore cannot produce cortisol or androgens.

CYP11B2 (aldosterone synthase) is the key enzyme. It is expressed only in the zona glomerulosa and catalyses all three terminal steps unique to aldosterone synthesis. CYP11B1 (in the zona fasciculata) shares 93% sequence identity but cannot perform the 18-oxidation step, explaining the strict zonal segregation of aldosterone versus cortisol biosynthesis.

Angiotensin II and extracellular potassium are the two dominant physiological stimuli, each acting through calcium-mediated CYP11B2 induction. Elevated K+ directly depolarizes zona glomerulosa cells as a safeguard against hyperkalemia, making aldosterone a critical component of renal potassium homeostasis.

Mechanism of Action

Genomic Signaling via MR (NR3C2)

Aldosterone is lipophilic and diffuses freely across the cell membrane. In target cells (predominantly collecting duct principal cells, distal tubule, colon, sweat glands), it binds the mineralocorticoid receptor (MR, NR3C2), a ligand-activated nuclear transcription factor of the steroid hormone receptor superfamily.

  1. Cytoplasmic complex: In the absence of ligand, MR is held inactive in the cytoplasm by a chaperone complex (Hsp90, Hsp70, FKBP51). The complex prevents spurious nuclear entry.
  2. Aldosterone binding: Aldosterone binds MR with ~10-fold higher affinity than cortisol. Note that cortisol circulates at ~1000-fold higher concentration, so renal MR specificity is conferred by 11β-HSD2 (hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase type 2), which converts cortisol → inactive cortisone in aldosterone target cells, protecting MR from glucocorticoid occupancy.
  3. Nuclear translocation: MR-aldosterone complex translocates to the nucleus within 30–60 minutes.
  4. Transcription of target genes: MR homodimerizes and binds hormone response elements (HREs), driving transcription of:
    • SGK1 (serum- and glucocorticoid-inducible kinase 1): phosphorylates and stabilizes ENaC (epithelial sodium channel) at the apical membrane; also phosphorylates NEDD4-2 to prevent ENaC ubiquitination and internalization
    • α-ENaC subunit: increased transcription augments apical Na+ channel number
    • Na+/K+-ATPase α1 and β1 subunits: basolateral pump increased to sustain the electrochemical gradient
  5. Net ion transport: Na+ is reabsorbed across the apical ENaC, generating a lumen-negative transepithelial potential that drives passive K+ secretion (via ROMK) and H+ secretion (via H+-ATPase on intercalated cells). This is the primary mechanism of RAAS-driven hypertension and hypokalemic alkalosis in hyperaldosteronism.

11β-HSD2 as the mineralocorticoid gatekeeper: Mutations in HSD11B2 (encoding 11β-HSD2) cause apparent mineralocorticoid excess (AME) syndrome — cortisol floods unprotected MR receptors, causing severe hypertension and hypokalemia in children despite low aldosterone levels. Licorice root contains glycyrrhizinic acid, a potent 11β-HSD2 inhibitor, which can cause licorice-induced pseudohyperaldosteronism.

Physiological Regulation

The aldosterone–RAAS system operates as a closed-loop blood pressure and volume controller:

  • Volume/pressure feedback: Na+ retention → ECF volume expansion → increased renal perfusion pressure → JG cell stretch → suppression of renin → reduced Ang II → reduced aldosterone secretion. This negative feedback limits volume overload.
  • ANP (atrial natriuretic peptide): Released by atrial cardiomyocytes in response to volume overload; directly suppresses aldosterone secretion from the zona glomerulosa (via cGMP/PKG pathway) and inhibits renin release — acting as the physiological counterbalance to the RAAS.
  • Circadian rhythm: Aldosterone has a diurnal pattern parallel to ACTH/cortisol — peak in early morning (6–8 am), nadir near midnight. Upright posture and volume depletion (standing from bed in the morning) are physiological stimuli for the morning peak via RAAS activation.
  • Potassium balance: A rise in plasma K+ of as little as 0.1–0.2 mmol/L directly stimulates aldosterone secretion, providing a rapid, renin-independent mechanism for K+ homeostasis.
  • Escape phenomenon: With prolonged aldosterone excess, Na+ retention is self-limiting due to the pressure natriuresis response (increased renal perfusion pressure forces Na+ loss by pressure-dependent mechanisms in the proximal nephron). This “aldosterone escape” prevents unlimited edema but does not normalize blood pressure, K+, or cardiovascular risk.

Cardiovascular Effects Beyond Sodium and Potassium

MR is expressed not only in kidney but also in cardiomyocytes, cardiac fibroblasts, vascular smooth muscle, endothelial cells, and macrophages. Chronic aldosterone excess drives maladaptive changes in these non-epithelial tissues:

  • Cardiac fibrosis: MR activation in cardiac fibroblasts induces TGF-β1, stimulating collagen I and III synthesis → interstitial and perivascular fibrosis → diastolic dysfunction. This occurs independently of blood pressure and K+ effects.
  • Endothelial dysfunction: MR in endothelial cells promotes NADPH oxidase-derived reactive oxygen species (ROS), reduces eNOS expression and bioavailable nitric oxide, and enhances endothelin-1 production → impaired vasodilation and increased vascular resistance.
  • Macrophage-driven inflammation: MR-activated macrophages upregulate pro-inflammatory cytokines (MCP-1, TNF-α, IL-6), contributing to vascular inflammation and atherogenesis independently of hemodynamic effects.
  • Arrhythmia risk: Hypokalemia and hypomagnesemia from aldosterone excess prolong the cardiac action potential, increasing the risk of ventricular arrhythmias and atrial fibrillation.

MR Antagonists in Heart Failure: RALES and EMPHASIS-HF

RALES (1999): Spironolactone 25–50 mg/day added to standard HFrEF therapy (ACE inhibitor ± digoxin ± diuretic) reduced all-cause mortality by 30% (RR 0.70; 95% CI 0.60–0.82; p<0.001) in patients with NYHA class III–IV HF and EF ≤35%. The mechanism is thought to be prevention of aldosterone-driven myocardial fibrosis, not diuresis alone. EMPHASIS-HF (2011): Eplerenone 25–50 mg/day in milder HFrEF (NYHA II, EF ≤30% or EF ≤35% with recent hospitalization) reduced the composite of cardiovascular death or HF hospitalization by 37% (HR 0.63; 95% CI 0.54–0.74). Together, these landmark trials established MR antagonism as a pillar of HFrEF therapy.

Liddle syndrome Gain-of-function mutations in ENaC β or γ subunit PY motifs → impaired NEDD4-2-mediated ENaC internalization → constitutively increased apical Na+ channel number → Na+ retention despite suppressed RAAS and suppressed aldosterone. Severe early-onset hypertension, hypokalemia, metabolic alkalosis; plasma aldosterone and renin are both low (distinguishes from hyperaldosteronism). Autosomal dominant. Amiloride or triamterene (ENaC blockers) — highly effective. MR antagonists (spironolactone, eplerenone) are ineffective because the defect is downstream of MR. Low-sodium diet.
