Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome

A life-threatening inflammatory lung syndrome defined by acute hypoxemic respiratory failure — bilateral radiographic infiltrates, impaired oxygenation (P/F ratio <300 mmHg), and non-cardiogenic pathophysiology — with a histological substrate of diffuse alveolar damage and a 35–46% mortality in severe cases.

Criteria: Berlin Definition 2012 Scale: L05 Clinical / Pathology System: Respiratory Type: Syndrome Reviewed: 2026-06-05

Berlin 2012 Definition and Severity Classification

The Berlin Definition (2012) — developed by a European Society of Intensive Care Medicine task force and endorsed by ARDS Definition Task Force — replaced the older American-European Consensus Conference (AECC) criteria. It specifies four mandatory criteria, all of which must be met simultaneously:

  • Timing: Acute onset within 1 week of a known clinical insult or new/worsening respiratory symptoms
  • Chest imaging: Bilateral opacities on CXR or CT not fully explained by effusions, lobar/lung collapse, or nodules
  • Oxygenation failure: PaO₂/FiO₂ (P/F ratio) <300 mmHg with PEEP or CPAP ≥5 cmH₂O
  • Origin of edema: Not fully explained by cardiac failure or fluid overload; if no risk factor for ARDS is present, echocardiographic assessment is required to exclude hydrostatic pulmonary edema

Key distinction from AECC (1994): The Berlin Definition removed the term "acute lung injury" (ALI) as a precursor category, eliminated the ambiguous "absence of left atrial hypertension" criterion (replaced by clinical judgment and echo), specified a minimum PEEP requirement to standardize oxygenation assessment, and added severity stratification validated against clinical outcomes. Sensitivity 89%, specificity 63% for DAD at autopsy.

ARDS incidence is approximately 10–86 cases per 100,000 person-years depending on country and diagnostic criteria. In ICUs, ARDS affects ~10% of all admitted patients and ~23% of mechanically ventilated patients (LUNG SAFE study, Bellani et al. 2016, n=29,144 in 50 countries).

Pathophysiology: Diffuse Alveolar Damage (DAD)

The histological hallmark of ARDS is diffuse alveolar damage (DAD), a stereotyped lung injury response that evolves through three overlapping phases regardless of the underlying trigger:

Exudative Phase (Days 0–7)

Injury to both the alveolar epithelium (primarily type I pneumocytes) and the pulmonary capillary endothelium leads to alveolar-capillary barrier disruption. This allows protein-rich fluid to flood the alveolar space — non-cardiogenic pulmonary edema. The key features are:

  • Type I pneumocyte necrosis: AT1 cells, which cover ~95% of alveolar surface area, are highly susceptible to oxidative, mechanical, and cytotoxic injury. Their death leaves denuded basement membranes.
  • Hyaline membrane formation: Protein-rich exudate (fibrin, plasma proteins, cellular debris) condenses along denuded alveolar walls into eosinophilic hyaline membranes — the pathognomonic histological feature seen at autopsy or open lung biopsy.
  • Neutrophil influx: Massive recruitment of activated neutrophils into the alveolar space, driven by IL-8/CXCL8, C5a, and leukotriene B4. Neutrophil degranulation releases elastase, MPO, and ROS that amplify barrier injury.
  • Alveolar macrophage activation: Resident AMs sense DAMPs/PAMPs via TLR4 → NF-κB → IL-1β/TNF-α/IL-6/IL-8 cascade, amplifying inflammation and neutrophil chemotaxis.
  • Interstitial edema and capillary congestion: Lymphatic drainage is overwhelmed; interstitial then alveolar flooding causes severe V/Q mismatch and intrapulmonary shunt → hypoxemia.

Proliferative Phase (Days 7–21)

If the initial injury resolves, type II pneumocyte (AT2) proliferation begins to resurface denuded alveolar walls. AT2 cells are the alveolar progenitor; they proliferate and transdifferentiate into AT1 cells under Wnt/β-catenin, EGF receptor, and Notch signaling. Simultaneously, fibroblasts are recruited and activated by TGF-β1 released from AT2 cells, macrophages, and injured endothelium. Early fibroproliferative changes (collagen I/III deposition) begin in this phase. Clinically, patients may improve (resolution) or progress to irreversible fibrosis.

Fibrotic Phase (>21 Days)

In the subset of patients who fail to resolve, progressive collagen deposition and architectural distortion lead to fibrotic ARDS. Fibroblastic foci (active myofibroblasts embedded in young fibrous tissue) are the dominant histological feature, analogous to the UIP pattern of IPF. This stage is associated with prolonged ventilator dependence, pulmonary hypertension, and high mortality. The mechanism involves sustained TGF-β1 → SMAD2/3 → Col1A1/Col3A1 fibrogenic program; mechanical stretch of stiff lung tissue → YAP/TAZ mechanosignaling further amplifies fibroblast activation.

Triggers: Direct vs. Indirect Injury

ARDS triggers are classified as direct (pulmonary) — primary injury to the lung parenchyma — or indirect (extrapulmonary) — systemic inflammatory states that injure the lung via the circulation. The distinction has pathophysiological and potentially therapeutic implications (direct ARDS tends toward alveolar consolidation with less recruitability; indirect ARDS tends toward ground-glass opacity with greater recruitability).

Molecular Mechanisms

Innate Immune Activation and Cytokine Storm

ARDS initiation centers on sensing of pathogen-associated (PAMPs) and damage-associated (DAMPs) molecular patterns by alveolar macrophages and recruited monocytes:

  • Key DAMPs: HMGB1 (released from necrotic nuclei), double-stranded RNA (dsRNA from viral replication or mitochondrial stress), mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA — activates TLR9), ATP (purinergic P2X7 signaling), and heat shock proteins
  • Key PAMPs: LPS (TLR4), flagellin (TLR5), peptidoglycan (TLR2), bacterial DNA (TLR9), viral dsRNA (TLR3), ssRNA (TLR7/8)
  • TLR4/NF-κB in alveolar macrophages: TLR4-MD2-CD14 → MyD88/TRIF → IKK complex → NF-κB nuclear translocation → IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-6, IL-8 transcription → massive neutrophil recruitment

Neutrophil-Mediated Injury

IL-8/CXCL8 binds CXCR2 on circulating neutrophils → diapedesis into alveolar space. Activated neutrophils release:

  • Neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs): Extruded chromatin decorated with neutrophil elastase (NE), MPO, and citrullinated histones; NETs trap pathogens but also damage AT1 cells and endothelium; elevated cell-free DNA in ARDS BAL fluid correlates with poor prognosis
  • Reactive oxygen species (ROS): NADPH oxidase (NOX2) burst → superoxide → peroxynitrite → lipid peroxidation → membrane damage; inactivates surfactant proteins SP-A and SP-B
  • Proteases: Neutrophil elastase (NE) degrades collagen IV, elastin, and claudin-18 (tight junction) → barrier permeability; MMP-8 and MMP-9 cleave extracellular matrix, releasing cryptic TGF-β from LTBP-1 stores

Endothelial Permeability

VE-cadherin (CDH5) at inter-endothelial adherens junctions is a critical permeability regulator. TNF-α/IL-1β → VEGF upregulation → VEGFR2 → Src kinase → VE-cadherin phosphorylation at Y658/Y685 → internalization via Rab-GTPase vesicles → junctional gap formation → vascular leak. NE also directly cleaves VE-cadherin extracellular domain. Permeability amplified by thrombin via PAR-1 → Rho/ROCK → actomyosin contraction.

NLRP3 Inflammasome and Pyroptosis

In severe ARDS, NLRP3 inflammasome assembly in alveolar macrophages is triggered by mtDNA, ATP, and pore-forming toxins → caspase-1 activation → IL-1β/IL-18 maturation + Gasdermin D (GSDMD) cleavage → pyroptotic cell death in type I pneumocytes → further DAMP release. IL-1β in BAL fluid is elevated 100-fold above plasma in ARDS and correlates with severity and mortality.

Coagulation and Complement Amplification

Alveolar fibrin deposition contributes to hyaline membranes. PAI-1 (plasminogen activator inhibitor-1) is massively overexpressed in ARDS alveolar epithelium and endothelium, inhibiting fibrinolysis (tPA/uPA) → persistent fibrin; elevated BAL/plasma PAI-1 is an independent ARDS mortality predictor. Complement C5a binds C5aR1 on neutrophils and macrophages → priming → amplification loop. Tissue factor (TF) expressed on injured AT1 cells and macrophages → extrinsic coagulation → thrombin → fibrin deposition + PAR-1 activation on endothelium → further permeability.

Lung-Protective Ventilation

Mechanical ventilation is life-sustaining in ARDS but can itself cause ventilator-induced lung injury (VILI) through four mechanisms: volutrauma (overdistension), barotrauma (pressure injury), atelectrauma (repeated recruitment/derecruitment of unstable alveoli), and biotrauma (stretch-induced cytokine release). The ARDS lung is mechanically heterogeneous — "baby lung" concept (Gattinoni): only 20–30% of lung remains aerated; normal tidal volumes injure this small compartment at high local strain.

Pharmacology

Despite decades of clinical trials, no pharmacological agent has proven survival benefit in unselected ARDS. The most promising interventions target inflammation, fibroproliferation, or disease-specific drivers (COVID-19).

Connections

  • occurs-inLung Alveolus — diffuse alveolar damage is the primary tissue substrate of ARDS; AT1 necrosis, hyaline membrane formation, and alveolar flooding are the hallmark pathological events
  • visualized-atLung Slice — ARDS patterns on CT/HRCT include diffuse bilateral ground-glass opacity ± consolidation with dependent gradient; lung slice anatomy contextualizes lobar vs. diffuse patterns
  • caused-byCOVID-19 Disease — SARS-CoV-2 pneumonia is the most common single cause of ARDS in the post-2020 era; COVID-19 ARDS has distinctive IL-6 dominance and benefit from dexamethasone+baricitinib
  • amplified-byCytokine Storm — severe ARDS involves dysregulated IL-1β/IL-6/TNF-α amplification reminiscent of cytokine storm; the overlap is greatest in COVID-19 and sepsis-associated ARDS
  • mediated-byNLRP3 Inflammasome — NLRP3 activation in alveolar macrophages produces IL-1β and drives pyroptosis in AT1 pneumocytes; NLRP3 is a key amplifier of the exudative phase of DAD
  • impairsPulmonary Surfactant — ARDS causes surfactant inactivation by inflammatory proteins and oxidative modification of DPPC/SP-B; reduced surfactant function worsens atelectasis and oxygenation failure
  • can-progress-toPulmonary Fibrosis — non-resolving ARDS transitions to fibroproliferative and ultimately fibrotic phase; TGF-β1/myofibroblast activation in late ARDS is mechanistically identical to early IPF pathogenesis
  • driven-byTGF-β — TGF-β1 plays dual roles in ARDS: acute immunosuppression of alveolar macrophages (protective early) and later fibroproliferative activation of fibroblasts (harmful in late/fibrotic ARDS)
