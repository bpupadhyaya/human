Calcium

The preeminent intracellular second messenger and the most abundant mineral in the body — a 10,000-fold Ca²⁺ gradient across the SR membrane powers every heartbeat via Ca²⁺-induced Ca²⁺ release.

Resting cytosolic [Ca²⁺] is ~100 nM; during cardiac systole it rises to ~1 µM triggering troponin C and actin-myosin cross-bridge cycling. Over 99% of body calcium (~1 kg) resides in bone and teeth as hydroxyapatite. Deficiency causes tetany, QT prolongation, and osteoporosis; excess causes nephrolithiasis, bradycardia, and vascular calcification.

Atlas One · Atomic · Alkaline Earth Metal / Second Messenger

Calcium

Symbol: Ca  ·  Z: 20  ·  Mass: 40.078 u  ·  Config: [Ar] 4s²  ·  Ion: Ca²⁺ (ionic radius 99 pm)

Calcium is simultaneously the most abundant mineral in the body and the most versatile intracellular signal. The strict separation of a vast structural pool (bone hydroxyapatite) from a vanishingly small cytosolic signalling pool (~100 nM at rest) creates thermodynamic gradients — 10,000-fold across the SR membrane, ~100,000-fold across the plasma membrane — that make Ca²⁺ signalling both powerful and fast. Every cardiac contraction, every neurotransmitter release, every smooth muscle contraction, and every exocrine secretion is triggered by a transient rise in [Ca²⁺]i. The cell exploits EF-hand proteins (calmodulin, troponin C) as fast Ca²⁺ sensors and SERCA, NCX, and PMCA as return mechanisms to ensure precise signal termination.

Overview & Atomic Properties

Calcium (symbol Ca, Z=20) is an alkaline earth metal in Group 2 of the periodic table. It forms the divalent cation Ca²⁺ (ionic radius 99 pm) in all biological contexts, losing both 4s electrons. A 70 kg adult contains approximately 1 kg of calcium: >99% as hydroxyapatite [Ca₁₀(PO₄)₆(OH)₂] in bone and teeth, ~0.9% in intracellular stores and protein-bound fractions, and only ~0.1% in plasma (total calcium 2.2–2.6 mmol/L; ionised fraction 1.15–1.35 mmol/L). Serum albumin binds ~40% of circulating calcium — acidosis increases ionised Ca²⁺ (H⁺ displaces Ca²⁺ from albumin), alkalosis decreases it.

Why Ca²⁺ is irreplaceable as a second messenger: (1) High charge density enables tight, specific EF-hand coordination (7 oxygen ligands, Kd ~0.1–10 µM); (2) steep gradients across the SR and plasma membrane allow large-amplitude signals from small ion fluxes; (3) millisecond on/off kinetics match action potential timescales; (4) multiple return mechanisms (SERCA, NCX, PMCA) enable precise signal termination; (5) chemical incompatibility with phosphate at physiological concentrations necessitates strict nanomolar maintenance — a constraint that biology converted into a signalling asset.

    PKA phosphorylates TnI     → ↓ TnC Ca²⁺ affinity → faster relaxation
    Net result: Faster, stronger systole + complete diastolic relaxation

Non-cardiac Ca²⁺ signalling: In most cells, Ca²⁺ signals are generated by IP₃ receptor-mediated ER Ca²⁺ release (downstream of Gq/PLCβ) and store-operated Ca²⁺ entry (SOCE) via Orai1/STIM1. Ca²⁺-bound calmodulin (CaM) activates CaMKII, eNOS, calcineurin/NFAT, and MLCK — linking Ca²⁺ to gene expression, NO production, and smooth muscle tone. Exocytosis (neurotransmitter release, insulin secretion, mast cell degranulation) all require Ca²⁺ binding to synaptotagmin or other SNARE-regulatory proteins.

Absorption & Metabolism

Intestinal absorption (~30% of dietary calcium, rising to ~60% in pregnancy/growth): Two parallel pathways: (1) Transcellular (active, saturable, regulated by vitamin D): apical entry through TRPV6 (transient receptor potential vanilloid 6), cytoplasmic transport by calbindin-D9k, basolateral export via PMCA1b and NCX1. Calcitriol (1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D₃) upregulates TRPV6 and calbindin transcription — the dominant hormonal mechanism increasing absorption. (2) Paracellular (passive, driven by lumenal Ca²⁺ gradient): dominant at high dietary calcium intake.

Hormonal triad: Serum Ca²⁺ is maintained within ±5% by (1) PTH — secreted by parathyroid chief cells when Ca²⁺ falls; stimulates renal 1α-hydroxylase (↑ calcitriol), osteoclast-mediated bone resorption (via RANKL/OPG), and DCT Ca²⁺ reabsorption (TRPV5); (2) Calcitriol — increases GI absorption and bone remodelling; (3) Calcitonin — thyroid C-cells; reduces osteoclast activity acutely; relatively minor in adults.

Renal handling: ~98% of filtered Ca²⁺ is reabsorbed. ~60% paracellular in PCT; ~25% via claudin-14/16/19 tight junctions in thick ascending LOH; ~8% PTH-regulated active reabsorption in DCT (TRPV5 + calbindin-D28k); ~5% in collecting duct. Loop diuretics increase urinary Ca²⁺ excretion (calciuria); thiazides reduce it.

Clinical Use

IV calcium gluconate / CaCl₂: Emergency treatment for symptomatic hypocalcaemia (tetany, laryngospasm), hyperkalaemia (cardioprotection — Ca²⁺ raises the cardiomyocyte threshold potential, stabilising the membrane against the depolarising effect of excess K⁺), and calcium channel blocker toxicity. Calcium chloride (10%) delivers ~3× more elemental Ca²⁺ than gluconate per ampoule; preferred in cardiac arrest.

Calcium supplements: Widely used for osteoporosis prevention. Calcium carbonate (40% elemental Ca) taken with food; calcium citrate (21% elemental Ca) absorbed with or without food, preferred in achlorhydria. Evidence for primary cardiovascular disease prevention is mixed — dietary calcium is preferred over high-dose supplements.

Calcium channel blockers (CCBs): Dihydropyridines (amlodipine, nifedipine) — selective vascular Cav1.2 block → vasodilation → antihypertensive; non-dihydropyridines (diltiazem, verapamil) — cardiac + vascular Cav1.2 block → rate control in AF, anti-anginal. Contraindicated in heart failure with reduced EF (negative inotropy) and with beta-blockers for rate control (bradycardia risk).
