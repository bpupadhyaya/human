Gut Microbiome

The gut microbiome — also termed intestinal microbiota — comprises the bacteria, archaea, fungi, viruses (phage), and eukaryotes inhabiting the gastrointestinal tract, with the densest colonisation in the colon (10¹¹ cells per gram of luminal content). The aggregate genome of this community (the metagenome) encodes ~3.3 million unique genes — 150× the ~22,000 in the human nuclear genome — expanding host metabolic capacity to ferment dietary fibre, synthesise vitamins (B12, K2, folate), and detoxify xenobiotics. Key metabolic outputs include short-chain fatty acids (butyrate, propionate, acetate) that serve as colonocyte fuel, HDAC inhibitors, and GPR signalling ligands. The microbiome educates and calibrates mucosal immunity — inducing secretory IgA (10–15 g/day), Foxp3+ regulatory T cells (Tregs), and TH17 cells — while the ENS ("second brain," ~500 million neurons) and the gut-brain axis via vagal afferents and tryptophan/serotonin metabolism connect gut microbial activity to brain function, behaviour, and mood. Dysbiosis — altered microbial composition — underlies or contributes to IBD, Clostridioides difficile infection, metabolic syndrome, and autism spectrum disorder.

Overview

The human gut microbiome is a complex, dynamic ecosystem co-evolved with its host over millions of years. At birth, the gut is rapidly colonised — vaginally delivered infants acquire maternal vaginal and fecal flora (Lactobacillus-rich), while caesarean-born infants acquire skin and environmental flora — establishing microbial communities whose composition influences immune development, allergy risk, and metabolic programming for life.

The colon harbours the overwhelming majority of gut bacteria: up to 10¹¹ CFU per gram of colonic content, compared with 10¹–10³ per gram in the stomach and proximal small intestine. Strict anaerobes dominate (Firmicutes, Bacteroidetes, Actinobacteria) because the colonic lumen is profoundly hypoxic. The small intestine supports a distinct, less dense community (Lactobacillus, Streptococcus) adapted to oxygen microaerophily and the presence of bile acids and digestive enzymes.

Key commensal species with established host-interaction roles:

Akkermansia muciniphila

Strict anaerobe, Verrucomicrobia; colonises the mucus layer, degrading mucin as its carbon source while stimulating mucus production. Inversely correlated with obesity, type 2 diabetes, and metabolic syndrome. Outer membrane protein Amuc_1100 activates TLR2, improving tight junction expression and reducing intestinal permeability. Clinical trials of pasteurised A. muciniphila supplementation show improvements in insulin sensitivity and cardiometabolic markers in overweight individuals.

Faecalibacterium prausnitzii

Most abundant single species in healthy adults (~5–15% of Firmicutes); strict anaerobe of Clostridium cluster IV. Major butyrate producer with potent anti-inflammatory activity — secretes microbial anti-inflammatory molecule (MAIM/salicylate derivative) that inhibits NF-κB. Markedly reduced in Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis; its abundance inversely predicts post-surgical Crohn's relapse. Used as a biomarker and candidate next-generation probiotic.

Bifidobacterium spp.

Dominant in breastfed infants (up to 90% in breast-milk-fed neonates); declining in adults to ~1–5%. Ferment HMOs and produce acetate and lactate (not butyrate directly, but cross-feeds butyrate producers). Bifidobacterium longum and B. infantis promote Treg induction; B. bifidum degrades mucin glycans. Widely used in commercial probiotics; evidence for efficacy in antibiotic-associated diarrhoea and infant colic.

Lactobacillus spp.

Facultative anaerobes; relatively minor in the colon but dominant in the proximal small intestine and vagina. Produce lactic acid, reducing luminal pH, inhibiting Enterobacteriaceae growth. Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG (LGG) is the most clinically studied probiotic: reduces antibiotic-associated diarrhoea by ~50%, shortens acute infectious diarrhoea in children, and has documented efficacy in the prevention of travellers' diarrhoea and C. difficile recurrence.

Short-Chain Fatty Acids — Metabolism and Signalling

Microbial fermentation of indigestible dietary fibre (non-starch polysaccharides, resistant starch, inulin/FOS) in the colon yields short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) — principally butyrate (~15–20%), propionate (~20–25%), and acetate (~60–65%) in molar ratios of approximately 6:3:3 (butyrate:propionate:acetate). Total colonic SCFA production ~300–1,000 mmol/day. These molecules serve as the primary energy source for colonocytes, regulate gene expression epigenetically, and act as intercellular signalling ligands.

Butyrate's role as a class I/IIa HDAC inhibitor (IC₅₀ ~1–5 mM, consistent with luminal concentrations) is mechanistically significant: by preventing deacetylation of histones at gene promoters, it promotes expression of Foxp3 in naive T cells (Treg induction), inhibits NF-κB-driven inflammatory gene programmes, and induces apoptosis in cancer cell lines — forming a plausible mechanistic basis for epidemiological data linking dietary fibre to reduced colorectal cancer risk.

Gut-Brain Axis

The gut and brain maintain bidirectional communication through the gut-brain axis — a network of neural, endocrine, and immune pathways that allow the enteric nervous system and gut microbiome to influence behaviour, mood, stress responses, and cognitive function, and allow the brain to regulate gut motility, secretion, and immunity.

Vagal Afferents

~80% of vagal fibres are afferent (gut-to-brain), conveying signals from enteroendocrine cells and ENS neurons to the nucleus tractus solitarius (NTS) in the brainstem, thence to the hypothalamus, limbic system, and cortex. SCFAs activate GPR41 on enteroendocrine cells → PYY/GLP-1 → vagal afferents → satiety signalling. Vagotomy abolishes germ-free to colonised mouse behavioural effects in some paradigms, implicating direct neural relay.

Tryptophan and Serotonin Axis

The gut contains ~90–95% of the body's total serotonin (5-HT), stored in and secreted by enterochromaffin (EC) cells of the intestinal epithelium. Gut microbiota regulate host 5-HT biosynthesis: spore-forming Clostridia produce metabolites (including secondary bile acids and short-chain fatty acids) that stimulate EC cell TPH1 (tryptophan hydroxylase-1) expression, boosting mucosal 5-HT. Gut 5-HT activates intrinsic ENS 5-HT3/4 receptors (peristaltic reflex, secretory reflexes) and extrinsic 5-HT3 on vagal afferents. Serotonin reuptake by SERT on enterocytes terminates signalling; SERT inhibitors (SSRIs) affect gut motility. Tryptophan also enters the kynurenine pathway (IDO1) — dysbiosis-driven IDO1 upregulation shifts tryptophan away from serotonin toward kynurenine, linked to neuroinflammation and depression.

Enteric Nervous System (ENS)

The ENS — sometimes called the "second brain" — contains ~500 million neurons in two ganglionated plexuses (Meissner's/submucosal and Auerbach's/myenteric), comparable to the spinal cord. It operates autonomously from the central nervous system, coordinating peristalsis, secretion, and blood flow. Gut bacteria directly interact with ENS neurons: bacterial metabolites activate TLR2/4 on enteric glia; butyrate promotes ENS neurogenesis in developing gut; Campylobacter infection triggers enteric neuropathy via molecular mimicry with gangliosides.

HPA Axis and Stress

Germ-free animals display exaggerated hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis responses to restraint stress, with elevated corticosterone — normalised by early colonisation with specific bacteria (Bifidobacterium infantis in rat models). Lactobacillus rhamnosus (JB-1) reduces anxiety-like behaviour in mice via vagus-dependent GABA receptor expression changes. Stress reciprocally alters gut motility and barrier function — the neurogenic intestinal permeability response — potentially modifying microbial composition and setting up feed-forward dysbiosis.

Immune Interactions — Education, Tolerance, and Activation

The gut-associated lymphoid tissue (GALT) — encompassing Peyer's patches, isolated lymphoid follicles, mesenteric lymph nodes, and the diffuse lamina propria lymphoid tissue — is the largest immune organ in the body. The microbiome shapes virtually every arm of intestinal and systemic immunity.

Dysbiosis — Altered Microbiome Composition and Disease

Dysbiosis refers to microbiome compositional or functional alterations that perturb host-microbiome homeostasis. Key patterns:

Clostridioides difficile Infection (CDI) — Post-Antibiotic Dysbiosis

Broad-spectrum antibiotics (fluoroquinolones, clindamycin, cephalosporins) decimate colonic Bacteroidetes and Firmicutes, reducing colonisation resistance → C. difficile (spore-forming gram-positive anaerobe) germinates and overgrows → produces toxins A (TcdA) and B (TcdB), which glucosylate Rho GTPases → actin cytoskeleton collapse → colonocyte death → pseudomembranous colitis. Severity ranges from mild diarrhoea to toxic megacolon. In the USA, ~500,000 cases/year, ~29,000 deaths. Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) restores microbial diversity and achieves ~90% resolution of recurrent CDI (vs. ~30% for vancomycin alone) — one of the most striking demonstrations of microbiome causality in human disease. Standard-of-care for recurrent CDI (≥2 episodes); oral vancomycin or fidaxomicin for primary episodes.

Inflammatory Bowel Disease (IBD) — Reduced Diversity

Both Crohn's disease (CD) and ulcerative colitis (UC) are characterised by reduced microbial diversity (α-diversity), specific taxa alterations: Faecalibacterium prausnitzii ↓, Roseburia ↓, Akkermansia muciniphila ↓; Proteobacteria (E. coli, particularly adherent-invasive E. coli [AIEC]) ↑, Ruminococcus gnavus ↑. It is debated whether dysbiosis is cause or consequence, but germ-free animals do not develop experimental colitis (TNBS model) without microbial colonisation, supporting causality. Genetic susceptibility (NOD2/CARD15 → impaired innate bacterial sensing; ATG16L1 → defective autophagy of intracellular bacteria) interacts with dysbiosis to break mucosal tolerance.

Metabolic Syndrome — Firmicutes↑ / Bacteroidetes↓ Ratio

Landmark studies (Turnbaugh et al., 2006; Ley et al., 2006) showed obese mice and humans have an elevated Firmicutes:Bacteroidetes ratio versus lean controls, with the obese microbiome having enhanced capacity to extract energy from diet (measured by faecal caloric content and short-chain fatty acid production). Transfer of obese microbiome to germ-free recipients increased adiposity compared to lean microbiome transfer. Mechanistically: excess propionate → de novo hepatic lipogenesis; reduced butyrate → impaired GLP-1 secretion → reduced insulin sensitivity; increased Proteobacteria LPS → metabolic endotoxaemia (elevated plasma LPS → low-grade TLR4-driven inflammation → insulin resistance). Dietary interventions, caloric restriction, and gastric bypass surgery all shift the Firmicutes:Bacteroidetes ratio toward leaner compositions.

Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) — Prevotella↓, Altered Tryptophan Metabolism

Multiple studies report altered gut microbiome composition in ASD cohorts: Prevotella ↓ (implicated in diverse carbohydrate fermentation and social behaviour in mouse models), Clostridium cluster XI/XVII species ↑ (4-ethylphenylsulfate and p-cresol production — associated with anxiety behaviour in rodents), Bifidobacterium ↓. The mechanistic link is speculative but may involve: tryptophan → kynurenine shift (IDO1 upregulation by dysbiosis → reduced serotonin + increased neuroactive kynurenic acid); GABA imbalance; altered vagal signalling. Randomised trials of microbiome-directed therapy in ASD are ongoing; a 2019 open-label study (Kang et al.) reported sustained gut and behavioural improvements 2 years after FMT.
