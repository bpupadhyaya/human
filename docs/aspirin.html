Aspirin

Class: Antiplatelet  ·  NSAID  ·  Analgesic  |  Route: Oral  |  Status: Standard of care for secondary CV prevention; no longer routinely recommended for primary prevention

Aspirin (acetylsalicylic acid, ASA) is one of the most prescribed drugs in human history — an irreversible COX-1/COX-2 inhibitor whose pharmacological action is exquisitely dose-dependent. At low doses (75–325 mg), it permanently acetylates platelet COX-1, abolishing thromboxane A₂ (TXA₂) synthesis for the platelet's 10-day lifespan — the basis of its antiplatelet effect. At higher doses, it achieves systemic COX inhibition for anti-inflammatory and analgesic effects. ISIS-2 (1988) established it as a cornerstone of acute MI treatment (23% mortality reduction), equal in magnitude to thrombolysis. But three 2018 RCTs in primary prevention showed no benefit and increased bleeding — fundamentally changing how aspirin is used in asymptomatic patients.

Overview

Aspirin (acetylsalicylic acid, ASA) is the acetyl ester of salicylic acid — a simple chemical modification of the ancient therapeutic principle found in willow bark (salicin). Its synthesis by Felix Hoffmann at Bayer in 1897 produced a drug that would become one of the most consequential in history. The key discovery came 74 years later, when John Vane demonstrated in 1971 that aspirin-like drugs act by inhibiting prostaglandin synthesis — a finding that earned him the Nobel Prize in 1982.

The pharmacological action of aspirin depends critically on dose, reflecting the differential sensitivity and regenerative capacity of COX enzymes in different tissues:

Low Dose — 75–325 mg

Irreversibly inactivates platelet COX-1 → blocks TXA₂ for platelet lifetime (~10 days). Antiplatelet effect. Standard for secondary CV prevention and acute ACS.

Intermediate — 0.5–2 g/day

Systemic COX inhibition. Analgesic and antipyretic. Inhibits PGE₂-mediated fever sensitisation; reduces nociceptive prostaglandins.

High Dose — 2–6 g/day

Full anti-inflammatory COX-1/COX-2 blockade. Used historically for rheumatic fever. Salicylate accumulation → tinnitus at toxicity threshold.

Why platelets are especially vulnerable to aspirin: Platelets are anucleate — they cannot synthesise new proteins. Once platelet COX-1 is acetylated by aspirin, that platelet permanently lacks TXA₂ synthesis capacity for its entire ~10-day lifespan. New platelets (~10% replaced per day) gradually restore aggregate TXA₂ capacity 5–10 days after stopping aspirin — hence the recommendation to hold aspirin 5–7 days before elective surgery.

Platelet TXA₂ Amplification Loop

Platelet COX-1 converts arachidonic acid → PGG₂ → PGH₂ → TXA₂ (thromboxane synthase). TXA₂ then:

  1. Binds TP receptors on adjacent platelets → Gq signalling → ↑[Ca²⁺] → platelet activation and shape change
  2. Causes vasoconstriction (TP receptors on vascular smooth muscle)
  3. Amplifies platelet activation synergistically with ADP, thrombin, and collagen exposure at the site of plaque rupture
  4. Aspirin blocks step 1 irreversibly → the amplification loop is permanently suppressed for that platelet

This is why even very low doses of aspirin are sufficient for antiplatelet effect — complete COX-1 inhibition in platelets is achieved at doses below 100 mg, and unlike most drugs, higher doses do not add antiplatelet potency but do increase toxicity.

Anti-inflammatory Mechanism

At higher doses, aspirin inhibits COX-2 at sites of inflammation → ↓PGE₂, PGI₂, PGD₂ → ↓vasodilation, ↓oedema, ↓sensitisation of pain receptors → classic NSAID anti-inflammatory effect. Salicylate (the hydrolysis product) also has aspirin-independent anti-inflammatory effects via NF-κB inhibition. Additionally, aspirin-triggered lipoxins (ATLs) are generated by acetylated COX-2 acting on arachidonic acid — these have pro-resolution, anti-inflammatory activity distinct from blockade of prostaglandin synthesis.

Clinical Use

Acute MI (STEMI / NSTEMI)

Standard acute dosing: 300–325 mg chewed immediately as a loading dose — chewing achieves peak plasma levels ~30 minutes (vs ~60 minutes swallowed), ensuring rapid complete platelet COX-1 inhibition. Then 75–100 mg daily. The ISIS-2 trial established aspirin as equivalent in benefit to thrombolysis (streptokinase) in suspected acute MI — and the two treatments were additive when combined, establishing the modern standard.

Dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT): After ACS or coronary stent implantation, aspirin + P2Y₁₂ inhibitor (ticagrelor, prasugrel, or clopidogrel) is standard for 12 months — aspirin is the permanent backbone (indefinitely in secondary prevention), and the P2Y₁₂ inhibitor is discontinued after the DAPT period.

Secondary Cardiovascular Prevention

Patients with established ASCVD (prior MI, prior stroke/TIA, peripheral arterial disease, stable coronary artery disease) benefit clearly from low-dose aspirin. The Antithrombotic Trialists' Collaboration (2002) meta-analysis of 287 trials and 135,000 patients demonstrated a 22% proportional reduction in serious vascular events — absolute benefit ~1–2% per year in high-risk patients (number needed to treat ~40–50 per year to prevent one event).

Primary Prevention — Changed Landscape (2018)

Three large RCTs reported in 2018 dramatically changed practice:

  • ASPREE (McNeil et al, 2018): 19,114 healthy elderly patients (≥70 years) randomised to aspirin 100 mg/day vs placebo — no reduction in disability-free survival; increased risk of major hemorrhage (GI, intracranial), and a signal toward increased all-cause mortality
  • ARRIVE (Gaziano et al, 2018): 12,546 patients at moderate CV risk — no significant reduction in MACE; GI bleeding increased
  • ASCEND (Bowman et al, 2018): 15,480 patients with diabetes — aspirin 100 mg/day reduced serious vascular events, but was offset by an equal increase in major bleeding; no net clinical benefit

Current ACC/AHA 2019 guidelines: aspirin not routinely recommended for primary prevention in most adults. May be considered in selected individuals aged 40–70 with high 10-year ASCVD risk AND low bleeding risk, after shared decision-making. Not recommended in adults ≥70 years for primary prevention.

The Secondary/Primary Prevention Divide: Aspirin's benefit-to-harm ratio depends entirely on the baseline absolute cardiovascular risk. In secondary prevention (established ASCVD, risk ~3–5% per year), the absolute risk reduction from antiplatelet therapy (~1–2% per year) clearly outweighs the absolute bleeding risk (~0.5% per year). In primary prevention (baseline risk 1–2% per year), the benefits and harms are nearly equal — explaining why three large modern RCTs found no net benefit. This is one of the clearest demonstrations in pharmacology of how baseline risk determines whether a treatment helps or harms.

Side Effects

  • GI bleeding / gastric ulceration — most clinically important side effect of long-term aspirin. Mechanism: COX-1 inhibition in gastric mucosa reduces PGE₂-mediated bicarbonate secretion, mucus production, and mucosal blood flow. Risk reduced by enteric coating (delays absorption but does not eliminate systemic COX-1 inhibition) and proton pump inhibitors (PPIs, recommended in patients at high GI risk on long-term aspirin).
  • Intracranial haemorrhage — rare but serious; absolute risk ~0.1–0.2% per year with long-term low-dose aspirin. The most feared bleeding complication; risk increases with age, hypertension, and concurrent anticoagulation.
  • Aspirin hypersensitivity / Samter's triad — aspirin-exacerbated respiratory disease (AERD) in patients with asthma, nasal polyps, and aspirin hypersensitivity. Mechanism: COX-1 blockade shifts arachidonate metabolism toward the 5-lipoxygenase pathway → ↑leukotrienes → bronchoconstriction, rhinorrhoea.
  • Reye's syndrome — encephalopathy and liver failure in children and adolescents with viral infections (influenza, varicella) given aspirin. Mechanism unclear but well-established epidemiologically. Contraindicated in children <16 years except in specific conditions (Kawasaki disease, juvenile arthritis) under specialist supervision.
  • Salicylate toxicity (overdose) — tinnitus, hearing loss (high-frequency), sweating, hyperpnoea, metabolic acidosis. In severe poisoning: mixed respiratory alkalosis/metabolic acidosis, hyperthermia, seizures. Management: activated charcoal, alkalinisation of urine, haemodialysis in severe cases.
