Hydrogen

The most abundant element in the universe and in the human body by atom count — present in every water molecule, every C–H bond, every hydrogen bond in DNA and proteins, and powering ATP synthesis as H⁺ across the inner mitochondrial membrane.

~60% of all atoms in a 70 kg adult are hydrogen. As a bare proton (H⁺), hydrogen is the transferred species in all acid-base chemistry, the substrate for the mitochondrial proton-motive force, and the regulatory handle on enzyme activity via pH. As hydride (H⁻ in NADH), it is the reducing equivalent currency of catabolism.

Atlas One · Atomic · Nonmetal / Proton / Hydrogen Bond Donor

Hydrogen

Symbol: H  ·  Z: 1  ·  Mass: 1.008 u  ·  Config: 1s¹  ·  Isotopes: ¹H (99.985%), ²H/D (0.015%), ³H/T (trace)

Hydrogen is the simplest element — one proton, one electron — yet it is functionally indispensable at every biological scale. Covalently, it populates every C–H, N–H, and O–H bond in biomolecules. Non-covalently, hydrogen bonds (5–25 kJ/mol) are the dominant stabilising force in protein secondary structure (α-helices, β-sheets) and nucleic acid base pairing (A:T = 2 bonds; G:C = 3 bonds). As the bare proton H⁺, its electrochemical gradient across the inner mitochondrial membrane — established by Complexes I, III, and IV of the ETC — drives F₀F₁-ATP synthase to produce ~28 of the ~32 ATP per glucose molecule: Peter Mitchell's 1961 chemiosmotic hypothesis, which earned him the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1978.

2. Hydride Transfer and Redox Carriers

In oxidoreductive metabolism, hydrogen is transferred as hydride H⁻ (a proton + 2 electrons): NAD⁺ + H⁻ → NADH (accepts from glucose in glycolysis and TCA cycle); FAD + 2H → FADH₂ (from succinate in Complex II); NADPH serves biosynthetic reduction (fatty acid synthesis, cholesterol, glutathione regeneration). The NADH:NAD⁺ ratio reports the cytosolic redox state; the NADPH:NADP⁺ ratio governs anabolic capacity and antioxidant regeneration.

3. Water Structure and Body Fluid

The ~63% body water (H₂O) provides a high dielectric constant (ε ~80, stabilising ions), high heat capacity (4.18 J/g·°C, buffering thermal fluctuations from metabolism), and the reaction milieu for all enzymatic chemistry. The extensive H-bond network of liquid water at 37°C — constantly breaking and reforming on the picosecond timescale — gives water its liquid character while maintaining transient local order sufficient to orient ligands in enzyme active sites.
