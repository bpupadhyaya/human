Coxsackievirus B

The leading infectious cause of acute myocarditis — a picornavirus whose protease 2A cleaves cardiac dystrophin, triggering a cascade that can progress to dilated cardiomyopathy and heart failure long after the virus is gone.

Coxsackievirus B (CVB, serotypes B1–B6) is a non-enveloped +ssRNA enterovirus transmitted faecal-orally that, in a subset of infections, spreads from the gut to the heart, pancreas, and CNS. Its greatest clinical significance is as the major infectious antecedent of myocarditis and dilated cardiomyopathy, with an additional hypothesised role as a trigger for autoimmune type 1 diabetes via pancreatic beta-cell tropism. No approved antiviral treatment exists; management is supportive with guideline-directed medical therapy for cardiac sequelae.

Coxsackievirus B (CVB)

Non-enveloped positive-sense single-stranded RNA virus, 6 serotypes (B1–B6), genus Enterovirus, family Picornaviridae. CAR (coxsackievirus-adenovirus receptor) is the primary internalisation receptor, concentrated at cardiomyocyte intercalated discs; DAF/CD55 is the attachment receptor. A single polyprotein is translated via cap-independent IRES-driven initiation and autocleaved by viral proteases 2Apro and 3Cpro. Protease 2Apro cleaves human cardiac dystrophin at the hinge-3 domain, disrupting the cytoskeletal-sarcolemmal link and creating a mechanistically DMD-like lesion. Adaptive immune molecular mimicry between CVB VP1 and cardiac myosin generates autoreactive T cells and antibodies that persist after viral clearance, perpetuating myocardial injury. No approved antiviral exists. Supportive care is the standard; IVIG is used in fulminant myocarditis. ~30% of severe cases progress to dilated cardiomyopathy requiring long-term heart failure therapy.

Entry, Replication, and Dystrophin Cleavage

1 · Receptor Engagement at Intercalated Discs

CAR is concentrated at cardiac intercalated discs — the cell-cell junctions that also mediate gap junction-based electrical coupling between cardiomyocytes. This anatomical localisation means CVB infection preferentially disrupts electrical conduction pathways. DAF/CD55 first captures CVB at the cell surface, presenting it to CAR for tight binding and internalisation via a CAR-mediated, actin-dependent endocytic pathway distinct from classical clathrin-coated pit internalisation in some cell types.

2 · Cytoplasmic Replication and Polyprotein Processing

The (+)ssRNA genome is directly translated as a single polyprotein (~250 kDa) via the 5′ IRES. 2Apro cleaves in cis at the VP1-2A junction, separating structural from non-structural regions; subsequent 3Cpro cleavage at 9 additional sites generates all mature structural and non-structural proteins. RNA replication occurs on ER-derived double-membrane vesicles. Progeny virions accumulate until lysosome-independent lytic release kills the cell. 2Apro also cleaves eIF4GI, shutting down host cap-dependent mRNA translation and redirecting ribosomes to viral IRES.

3 · Protease 2A Cleavage of Cardiac Dystrophin — The Mechanical Lesion

A cardinal cardiac-specific pathomechanism: viral 2Apro cleaves human dystrophin at the hinge-3 region (between spectrin-like repeats 15–17), separating the N-terminal actin-binding domain from the C-terminal dystrophin-associated glycoprotein complex (DGC). Dystrophin is the 427 kDa structural linker between intracellular F-actin and the sarcolemmal DGC, essential for mechanical coupling during cardiac contraction. Cleavage consequences: sarcolemmal membrane fragility during contraction; increased Ca2+ influx and ionic imbalance; cardiomyocyte necrosis independent of direct viral cytolysis. Mechanistically identical to the cardiomyocyte lesion in Duchenne muscular dystrophy (dystrophin-null). This explains why post-myocarditis dilated cardiomyopathy can persist and worsen long after viral clearance — the structural lesion (ongoing dystrophin deficiency due to inflammation-mediated inhibition of dystrophin repair) outlasts the virus.

Treatment — Supportive Only (No Approved Antiviral)

Supportive Care — Standard Management

The cornerstone. Rest (physical activity restriction for 3–6 months in myocarditis; exercise precipitates arrhythmia and sudden death). NSAIDs avoided in first 2 weeks of myocarditis (may impair healing). Haemodynamic support for fulminant myocarditis: IV inotropes (dobutamine, milrinone); in refractory cardiogenic shock, mechanical circulatory support (intra-aortic balloon pump, Impella, or ECMO as bridge to recovery or transplant). Arrhythmia management: beta-blockers, amiodarone, ICD if spontaneous or inducible VT with EF <35% after 3 months of optimal therapy.

Guideline-Directed Medical Therapy (GDMT) for Post-Myocarditis DCM

Once DCM with EF <40% is established, GDMT for HFrEF is indicated: ACE inhibitors or ARBs (reduce afterload, reverse remodelling); beta-blockers (carvedilol, metoprolol succinate, bisoprolol — reduce sympathetic drive, improve survival); mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (spironolactone/eplerenone — reduce fibrosis, aldosterone effects); SGLT2 inhibitors (dapagliflozin/empagliflozin — new addition to HFrEF GDMT with survival benefit regardless of diabetes status); ICD for primary prevention if EF <35% despite ≥3 months of GDMT.

IVIG in Fulminant and Neonatal Myocarditis

Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG, 2 g/kg over 24 h) is used in fulminant myocarditis and neonatal CVB sepsis-like illness despite limited RCT evidence. Proposed mechanisms: passive neutralising antibodies against CVB; Fc receptor modulation of inflammatory macrophages; anti-idiotype antibodies blocking autoreactive lymphocytes. Retrospective series suggest benefit in children; adult data from the IMAC trial (randomised, placebo-controlled) showed no significant improvement in EF at 1 year but possible trend toward better recovery in subgroups. Clinical practice varies; generally reasonable to use in fulminant cases where mortality risk is high.

Immunosuppression — Biopsy-Proven Cases

For biopsy-confirmed lymphocytic myocarditis not improving with standard therapy, particularly giant-cell myocarditis (GCM) or eosinophilic myocarditis, immunosuppression (corticosteroids + azathioprine or cyclosporine) may be considered. The TIMIC trial showed benefit of prednisone + azathioprine in biopsy-confirmed inflammatory cardiomyopathy with absence of active viral genomes (PCR-negative biopsy). Immunosuppression with active viral infection risks viral reactivation. Therefore, PCR testing of biopsy samples for enteroviral RNA is recommended before initiating immunosuppression.

Investigational Antivirals

Pleconaril (a capsid binder occupying the VP1 antiviral pocket; blocks receptor attachment and uncoating) showed antiviral activity against CVB in vitro and in phase 2 neonatal enteroviral meningitis trials but was not approved due to CYP3A4 drug interactions and marginal clinical benefit. Pocapavir (V-073) is a newer capsid inhibitor with improved resistance profile; orphan designation for neonatal enteroviral sepsis. Interferon-beta has antiviral effects in CVB3 cell and animal models; a small European trial showed potential benefit in biopsy-confirmed viral myocarditis. No agent is currently approved specifically for CVB infection.

CVB and Type 1 Diabetes — The Autoimmune Trigger Hypothesis

The molecular link between CVB and type 1 diabetes is one of the most investigated virology-autoimmunity hypotheses in medicine. Key evidence:

Direct isolation: In 1979, Yoon et al. isolated a CVB4 strain from the pancreas of a child who died of new-onset T1DM; the isolated strain induced diabetes when injected into susceptible mouse strains. Subsequent direct viral detection in human islets at autopsy has been variable.

Enteroviral RNA in islets: In situ hybridisation and RT-PCR studies have detected enteroviral RNA in pancreatic islets of T1DM patients at autopsy and in biopsy samples, suggesting persistent or recurrent low-level CVB replication in beta-cells. This finding has been reproduced in multiple centres.

Molecular mimicry: CVB VP1 shares a T-cell epitope with glutamate decarboxylase-65 (GAD65), the dominant autoantigen in early T1DM. CVB3 2C protein shares peptide sequences with anti-islet tyrosine phosphatase (IA-2). Cross-reactive T cells against these epitopes have been identified in T1DM patients.

Epidemiological evidence: Multiple prospective cohorts (DIPP, DiPiS, TEDDY) have shown associations between enteroviral infections in early childhood and subsequent development of islet autoantibodies and T1DM, particularly in HLA-DR3/DR4 carriers. The temporal relationship (infection months before seroconversion) is consistent with a triggering role.

ENVAR clinical trial: A CVB vaccine trial (VP1 protein-based, in adjuvant) is underway in Finland to test whether preventing CVB infection in high-risk children can reduce T1DM incidence — the definitive causal test of this hypothesis.
