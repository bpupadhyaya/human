Myocardium

The cardiac muscle tissue forming the bulk of the heart wall — a syncytium of electrically coupled cardiomyocytes supported by fibroblasts, a dense capillary network, and a collagen scaffold. Generates every heartbeat by orchestrating simultaneous contraction of billions of cells initiated from a single pacemaker focus.

UBERON: 0002349 FMA: 9462 Scale: 05-Tissue Status: Draft · reviewed 2026-06-03

Overview

The myocardium is the cardiac muscle tissue forming the middle and dominant layer of the heart wall, sandwiched between the inner endocardium and outer epicardium. It generates every heartbeat by orchestrating the synchronous contraction of millions of cardiomyocytes electrically coupled into a single functional unit — a syncytium — whose excitation propagates cell-to-cell across the entire wall in tens of milliseconds from a single SA node trigger.

Histologically, cardiac muscle is striated (organized sarcomeres like skeletal muscle) but involuntary (autonomically regulated, self-pacing). It is unique among striated muscles in having short, branched cells joined by intercalated discs — simultaneously mechanical (force transmission) and electrical (depolarization propagation) junctions.

Regenerative limitation: Adult human cardiomyocytes turn over at <1%/year. After injury, lost cells fill with collagenous scar. This non-regenerative response is why ischemic heart disease becomes chronic heart failure: every infarct permanently subtracts from contractile mass.

Architecture

Gap junctions at intercalated discs — predominantly connexin-43 (GJA1) — create low-resistance pores enabling depolarization to propagate cell-to-cell at ~0.3–1 m/s in working myocardium.

Fiber orientation across the ventricular wall is helical: subepicardial fibers run in a right-handed helix, midwall fibers nearly circumferential, and subendocardial fibers in a left-handed helix. This three-dimensional architecture produces the characteristic twisting (torsional) contraction, mechanically more efficient than a simple axial squeeze.

Extracellular matrix is mostly type I and type III collagen organized as endomysium (around cells), perimysium (around bundles), and epimysium (around the layer). This scaffold transmits force and prevents over-stretching.

Microvasculature: roughly one capillary per cardiomyocyte — exceptional density ensuring O₂ delivery matches the heart's high resting metabolic demand (~6–10 mL O₂/100 g/min, 5–10× resting skeletal muscle).

Function

Coordinated Contraction as Electrical Syncytium

The myocardium's defining function is converting the electrical signal from the conduction system into mechanical force, simultaneously and uniformly across the whole wall. This requires: (1) electrical coupling via connexin-43 gap junctions enabling cell-to-cell wavefront propagation; (2) mechanical coupling via fasciae adherentes and desmosomes transmitting force end-to-end; and (3) spatially organized activation via Purkinje fibers initiating depolarization apex-first, generating apex-to-base ejection.

Frank–Starling Mechanism

Within physiological range, increasing end-diastolic stretch increases the force of the next contraction — a tissue-level property arising from length-dependent calcium sensitivity at the sarcomere (greater overlap at longer sarcomere length increases TnC Ca²⁺ affinity). This lets each ventricle automatically match output to filling, without neural input.

Force–Frequency (Bowditch) Relationship

In healthy cardiac muscle, increasing stimulation frequency increases developed force (positive staircase). This is the opposite of skeletal muscle and is mediated by frequency-dependent intracellular Ca²⁺ accumulation (CaMKII phosphorylates PLN at Thr17 → partial SERCA2a disinhibition → increased SR Ca²⁺ loading per cycle). The force-frequency relationship is flattened or inverted in failing myocardium — a useful tissue-level signature of heart failure.

Metabolic Profile

Approximately 95% of ATP comes from oxidative phosphorylation, fueled primarily by fatty acids (60–70%) and glucose/lactate (30–40%) at rest. The myocardium is exquisitely sensitive to ischemia: irreversible cardiomyocyte injury begins within ~20–40 minutes of complete coronary occlusion. This profile explains why therapies shifting substrate use (e.g., SGLT2 inhibitors → ketone utilization) can improve cardiac function in heart failure.
