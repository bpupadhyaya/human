Glucocorticoid Receptor

The stress-hormone transcription factor — an 87 kDa nuclear receptor (NR3C1) that lies dormant in the cytoplasm bound to HSP90, then activates upon cortisol binding to drive anti-inflammatory gene programs via GRE transactivation and NF-κB / AP-1 transrepression.

Ubiquitously expressed across virtually every human cell type, GR is the direct molecular target of the most widely prescribed anti-inflammatory drug class in medicine: corticosteroids. Its cloning by Hollenberg and Evans in 1985 revealed the mechanistic basis of glucocorticoid action. Two distinct transcriptional mechanisms — transactivation of anti-inflammatory genes and transrepression of NF-κB/AP-1 — explain both the therapeutic utility and the metabolic side-effect profile of glucocorticoids.

Glucocorticoid Receptor (GR)

Class: Nuclear receptor (NR3C1)  ·  Ligand-activated transcription factor  |  Gene: NR3C1 (chr 5q31.3)  |  UniProt: P04150  |  Endogenous ligand: cortisol (Kd ~5 nM)

The glucocorticoid receptor is a ligand-activated transcription factor of the nuclear receptor superfamily and the principal intracellular mediator of glucocorticoid hormone action. In the absence of cortisol, GR resides in the cytoplasm bound to a large chaperone complex including HSP90, HSP70, p23, and the immunophilins FKBP51/FKBP52, which hold the receptor in a high-affinity, ligand-receptive but transcriptionally inactive state. Cortisol binding triggers HSP90 dissociation, nuclear translocation via dynein/importin, and DNA binding — driving simultaneous transactivation of anti-inflammatory genes (IκBα, GILZ, Annexin-A1, MKP-1) and transrepression of pro-inflammatory transcription factors NF-κB and AP-1, suppressing cytokine and COX-2 gene programs without directly binding DNA at these repressed sites.

Overview

The molecular cloning of the glucocorticoid receptor in 1985 by Hollenberg, Weinberger, Evans and colleagues was a landmark in endocrinology and molecular pharmacology, providing the mechanistic foundation for understanding how corticosteroids — used clinically since Hench and Kendall demonstrated their dramatic efficacy in rheumatoid arthritis in 1949 — exert their anti-inflammatory effects at the molecular level. GR belongs to the nuclear receptor superfamily alongside the mineralocorticoid receptor (MR/NR3C2), androgen receptor (AR/NR3C4), and progesterone receptor (PR/NR3C3), all of which share the canonical three-domain architecture: an N-terminal transactivation domain, a central zinc-finger DNA-binding domain, and a C-terminal ligand-binding domain.

GR is expressed in virtually every human cell type, reflecting the pleiotropic metabolic and immunological roles of glucocorticoids. Its physiological ligand, cortisol, is secreted by the adrenal cortex in a circadian rhythm (peak at ~8 am) and in pulsatile bursts; cortisol levels rise 10–100-fold within minutes of acute stress (surgery, infection, hypoglycaemia) via CRH→ACTH→cortisol activation of the HPA axis. This stress-cortisol-GR axis is the molecular basis for the metabolic adaptations to acute stress: hepatic gluconeogenesis, skeletal muscle proteolysis, and suppression of non-essential immune and reproductive functions.

Two major mechanisms account for GR’s anti-inflammatory actions: (1) Transactivation — GR homodimerizes and binds palindromic glucocorticoid response elements (GREs) to induce anti-inflammatory effectors; and (2) Transrepression — GR monomers interact via protein-protein tethering with NF-κB and AP-1 transcription factors, blocking their access to coactivators (CBP/p300) and preventing transcription of pro-inflammatory target genes including TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β, and COX-2. This transrepression mechanism is DNA-binding-independent and is the primary basis for most clinically relevant anti-inflammatory effects of glucocorticoids.

GRα vs. GRβ isoforms

GRα (canonical, 777 aa): ligand-responsive, transcriptionally active, expressed in virtually all cells. GRβ: generated by alternative splicing of exon 9; incorporates alternative exon 9β; cannot bind glucocorticoids; acts as a dominant-negative inhibitor of GRα by competing for DNA and coactivator binding. GRβ is overexpressed in steroid-resistant asthma, certain RA patients, and inflammatory bowel disease — providing a molecular explanation for glucocorticoid resistance in these conditions.

HSP90 chaperone complex (unliganded state)

In the cytoplasm without ligand, GR is held in a high-affinity, ligand-receptive conformation by: HSP90 dimer (occupies LBD, maintains open ligand-binding pocket); HSP70 (early GR folding); p23/PTGES3 (stabilizes mature complex); FKBP51 (reduces cortisol affinity to Kd ~10 nM; is itself a GR target gene forming a negative feedback loop); FKBP52 (increases nuclear transport efficiency via dynein interaction; competes with FKBP51).

  • Cortisol diffusion and binding: Only free cortisol (~3% of serum cortisol) enters cells; the rest is bound to corticosteroid-binding globulin (CBG) and albumin, acting as a reservoir. Cortisol binds GR-LBD with Kd ~5 nM; synthetic glucocorticoids (dexamethasone Kd ~0.5 nM, budesonide ~1 nM) bind more tightly.
  • Chaperone dissociation: Cortisol binding causes helix 12 closure, propagating conformational change through LBD. HSP90 dissociates, FKBP52 replaces FKBP51, and dynein associates — enabling retrograde microtubular transport toward the nucleus.
  • Nuclear import: Importin-α binds NLS1 in the hinge region; importin-β docks on the nuclear pore complex; GR translocates into the nucleus within 30–60 minutes of cortisol exposure.
  • GRE transactivation (Mode 1): GR homodimer binds palindromic GREs (GGTACAnnnTGTTCT) upstream of target genes, recruiting SRC-1/TIF2 → CBP/p300 (histone acetyltransferase) → Mediator complex → RNA Pol II initiation. Key anti-inflammatory targets: GILZ (suppresses NF-κB/AP-1 downstream), MKP-1 (inactivates p38/JNK MAPK), IκBα (direct NF-κB inhibitor), Annexin-A1 (inhibits PLA₂, reducing eicosanoid production).
  • NF-κB transrepression (Mode 2): GR monomer directly binds the Rel homology domain of NF-κB p65 via protein-protein interaction, competing with p65 for CBP/p300 coactivator binding surface. This silences TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β, COX-2, and ICAM-1 transcription — all NF-κB target genes — without GR binding DNA. AP-1 (c-Fos/c-Jun) is simultaneously tethered by GR via its bZIP domain.
  • Negative feedback: FKBP51, induced by GR as a target gene, re-associates with newly synthesised GR in the cytoplasm, reducing cortisol binding affinity and attenuating further signalling. This autofeedback loop limits the magnitude and duration of the glucocorticoid response.

Transactivation vs. transrepression and the selective glucocorticoid agonist (SEGRA) hypothesis: The side effects of chronic glucocorticoid therapy (osteoporosis, hyperglycaemia, myopathy, HPA suppression) are driven primarily by GRE transactivation of metabolic genes (PEPCK, atrogin-1, RANKL). The anti-inflammatory effects are primarily mediated by transrepression of NF-κB/AP-1 (no DNA binding required). This mechanistic separation has driven a decades-long effort to develop SEGRAs — compounds that selectively engage transrepression while minimising transactivation. Multiple SEGRA candidates have shown efficacy in preclinical models but so far failed to dissociate effects sufficiently in humans, reflecting the complexity of context-dependent GR signalling across cell types.
