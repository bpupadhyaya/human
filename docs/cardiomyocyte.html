Cardiomyocyte

The contractile cell of the heart — a branched, striated, electrically coupled muscle cell that converts every depolarization wave into a coordinated mechanical contraction via excitation–contraction coupling. Two to three billion of these cells fire synchronously with each heartbeat.

Cell type: CL:0000746 Lineage: Cardiogenic mesoderm (lateral plate) Scale: 04-Cellular Status: Draft · reviewed 2026-06-03

Overview

The cardiomyocyte is the contractile cell of the heart — a striated muscle cell, branched and joined to its neighbors at intercalated discs, whose sole job is to convert each electrical depolarization into a coordinated mechanical contraction. Every heartbeat is the synchronous shortening of roughly two to three billion of these cells in the adult ventricles, executed within a few hundred milliseconds of the SA node firing.

Three properties make the cardiomyocyte distinct from other muscle cells. First, electrical excitability with a uniquely long action potential (~200–400 ms in ventricular cells), shaped to allow full mechanical contraction before re-excitation is possible. Second, coupled excitation–contraction via SR-mediated calcium-induced calcium release (CICR) — modulated by external Ca²⁺ entry through L-type channels. Third, functional syncytium — gap junctions at the intercalated discs (predominantly connexin-43) electrically link every cell to its neighbors so a wavefront from the SA node propagates without any synapse.

Key clinical fact: Cardiomyocytes are essentially post-mitotic in adults, turning over at <1%/year. Lost cells are replaced by collagenous scar — every infarct permanently subtracts from contractile mass.

Subcellular Architecture

Sarcomeres repeat at ~2 µm spacing at rest. Each is bounded by Z-discs anchoring thin filaments, and centered on the M-line. The regulatory troponin complex sits on every thin filament every seven actin monomers alongside one tropomyosin dimer.

T-tubule system — deep sarcolemmal invaginations at every Z-disc that carry the action potential into the cell interior, ensuring depolarization simultaneously reaches the SR throughout the cell volume.

Sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR) — the intracellular Ca²⁺ store. The junctional SR apposes T-tubules at dyads — the site of EC coupling where RyR2 clusters face Cav1.2 channels. Longitudinal SR carries SERCA2a for Ca²⁺ reuptake.

Intercalated discs at each cell end contain: fascia adherens (anchors thin filaments cell-to-cell), desmosomes (anchor intermediate filaments), and gap junctions (connexin-43 hemichannels, transmitting depolarization at ~0.3–1 m/s in ventricular myocardium).

Mitochondria are densely packed between myofibrils, supplying the continuous ATP needed for contractile cycling and ion-pump function. Cardiomyocytes are obligate aerobic cells; ATP depletion from even brief ischemia disrupts both contraction and ion transport within minutes.

Specialized Variants

The cardiomyocyte family includes: working (contractile) cardiomyocytes (atrial and ventricular; bulk of myocardium), pacemaker cells (SA and AV node; no stable resting potential; spontaneous depolarization via HCN4 and ICaL), and conduction-system cells (bundle of His, bundle branches, Purkinje fibers; fast-conducting at ~2–4 m/s; express high Cx40).

β-Adrenergic Modulation

Norepinephrine → β1-AR → Gαs → adenylyl cyclase → ↑cAMP → PKA. PKA simultaneously phosphorylates: (1) Cav1.2 — more Ca²⁺ trigger; (2) phospholamban Ser16 — relieves SERCA2a inhibition, faster relaxation, higher SR Ca²⁺ load; (3) troponin I Ser23/24 — reduces myofilament Ca²⁺ sensitivity, hastens relaxation; (4) RyR2 Ser2808 — increased Ca²⁺ release gain. Net: faster, stronger, and faster-relaxing beat — essential for exercise.

Lifecycle & Pathology

Origin

Cardiomyocytes arise from cardiogenic mesoderm via two progenitor populations: the first heart field (left ventricle primarily) and the second heart field (right ventricle, outflow tract, atria). Specification requires NKX2-5, GATA4, TBX5, MEF2C, and HAND1/2. Mutations in these transcription factors cause congenital heart disease.

Postnatal Growth and Adult Turnover

Mammalian cardiomyocytes lose proliferative capacity shortly after birth; postnatal growth is by hypertrophy. Many cells become binucleated or polyploid. Adult renewal by ¹⁴C bomb-pulse dating: ~1%/year in young adults, declining to <0.5%/year past age 50. Most cardiomyocytes you have at age 60 were present at birth.

Cell Death and Its Consequences

After significant ischemic injury, cardiomyocytes die by necrosis (energy failure, membrane disruption — releases troponin I and T into blood, basis of troponin assay for MI diagnosis), apoptosis (border zone, heart failure), or pyroptosis/necroptosis (myocarditis). Lost cardiomyocytes are replaced by collagenous scar — this is why ischemic injury becomes chronic heart failure.
