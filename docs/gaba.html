GABA (γ-Aminobutyric Acid)

The brain's primary braking neurotransmitter — GABA mediates fast inhibitory synaptic transmission via Cl⁻-permeable GABA-A receptors and slow inhibition via Gi-coupled GABA-B GPCRs, maintaining the E/I balance that prevents seizures.

MW 103.12 g/mol. Synthesised from glutamate in a single step by GAD65/GAD67 (pyridoxal phosphate-dependent). Terminated by GAT-1/GAT-3 transporter reuptake and GABA-T catabolism. GABA-A is the target of benzodiazepines, barbiturates, propofol, neurosteroids, alcohol, and Z-drugs. ~20–30% of cortical neurons are GABAergic interneurons.

Atlas One · Molecular · Inhibitory Neurotransmitter

GABA (γ-Aminobutyric Acid)

Class: Non-protein amino acid  ·  Inhibitory neurotransmitter  |  Biosynthesis: GAD65/GAD67 (GAD1/GAD2 genes)  |  Reuptake: GAT-1 (SLC6A1), GAT-3 (SLC6A11)  |  Catabolism: GABA-T + SSADH → succinate

GABA (γ-aminobutyric acid) is the dominant inhibitory neurotransmitter of the mammalian CNS, estimated to mediate inhibitory signalling at approximately one-third of all brain synapses. If glutamate is the accelerator, GABA is the brake — and the excitation–inhibition (E/I) balance is the fundamental homeostatic parameter governing circuit stability, oscillatory rhythms, and information processing. GABA operates through two structurally unrelated receptor types: GABA-A (a pentameric Cl⁻-permeable ligand-gated ion channel mediating fast IPSPs within milliseconds) and GABA-B (a Gi-coupled GPCR mediating slow, prolonged inhibition over hundreds of milliseconds). The GABA-A receptor is the molecular target of the most clinically important sedative, anxiolytic, anticonvulsant, and anaesthetic drug classes in medicine.

Overview & Structure

GABA is a four-carbon amino acid (formula C₄H₉NO₂; MW 103.12) that is not incorporated into proteins — it exists only as a free signalling molecule. Unlike glutamate (α-amino), GABA is a γ-amino acid: its amino group is on the γ-carbon. It is neutral at physiological pH. GABA cannot cross the blood-brain barrier efficiently; the CNS pool is synthesised locally in GABAergic interneurons.

GABAergic interneurons — parvalbumin (PV), somatostatin (SST), and VIP-expressing subtypes — make up ~20–30% of cortical neurons and exert powerful inhibitory control over the much larger population of glutamatergic principal neurons. Their loss or dysfunction is central to epilepsy, schizophrenia, and anxiety disorders.

Barbiturates — phenobarbital, thiopental, phenobarbitone GABA-A (β TM2/3 site) — PAM; at high doses, direct channel activation ↑Channel open duration; at anaesthetic doses, can activate GABA-A without GABA → more dangerous respiratory depression than BZDs Epilepsy (phenobarbital, still first-line in LMIC), anaesthesia induction (thiopental), status epilepticus, neonatal seizures
Propofol GABA-A (β TM2/3, same general region as barbiturates) Potentiates Cl⁻ current; also modulates glycine receptors and Nav channels; ultra-short onset/offset due to rapid redistribution General anaesthesia induction and maintenance; procedural sedation; ICU sedation; anti-emesis at sub-anaesthetic doses
Z-drugs — zolpidem, zaleplon, eszopiclone GABA-A α1 subunit-selective BZD site PAM Prefer α1βγ2-containing receptors (sedation) over α2/α3 (anxiolysis, muscle relaxation) — selectivity reduces daytime sedation and motor impairment Insomnia (short-term); zolpidem most prescribed sleep aid globally
Vigabatrin GABA-T (irreversible inhibitor) Covalent inactivation of GABA transaminase → ↑brain GABA; slow onset (days); risk of permanent visual field defects (retinal toxicity) Infantile spasms (West syndrome, first-line with ACTH), focal epilepsy refractory to other agents
Valproate Multiple: GABA-T inhibition (minor), Nav blockade, Ca²⁺ channel, HDAC inhibition Raises GABA levels + blocks neuronal firing; broad-spectrum anti-seizure Epilepsy (broad-spectrum), bipolar disorder, migraine prophylaxis; teratogenic (contraindicated in pregnancy)
Brexanolone (allopregnanolone IV) GABA-A δ-subunit extrasynaptic receptors (neurosteroid site) Positive allosteric modulator of extrasynaptic GABA-A → ↑tonic inhibition; mimics the neurosteroid that surges in pregnancy and drops postpartum Postpartum depression (PPD) — FDA-approved 2019; rapid onset (days vs weeks for SSRIs)
Baclofen GABA-B agonist Gi/o → ↓cAMP + GIRK + ↓Ca²⁺ channels → presynaptic reduction of excitatory transmitter release + postsynaptic hyperpolarisation Spasticity (MS, spinal cord injury), alcohol use disorder (cravings), treatment-resistant depression (off-label), trigeminal neuralgia

BZD vs. barbiturate overdose risk: Benzodiazepines require GABA to be present — they are positive allosteric modulators and cannot activate GABA-A directly. This means BZD overdose is rarely fatal alone (ceiling effect). Barbiturates can directly activate GABA-A at high doses — producing profound respiratory depression with no ceiling. Flumazenil is a competitive BZD antagonist at the α/γ2 site — it reverses BZD but NOT barbiturate sedation, and can precipitate withdrawal seizures in BZD-dependent patients.
