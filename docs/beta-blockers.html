Beta-blockers

Class: β-Adrenergic receptor antagonist  |  Route: Oral  |  Status: First-line in HFrEF, HTN, stable angina, post-MI, AF rate control

Competitive, reversible antagonists at β-adrenergic receptors. Reduce heart rate, contractility, and renin release acutely; chronically reverse pathological cardiac remodeling in HFrEF by blunting catecholamine toxicity — one of the strongest survival signals in cardiovascular pharmacology (MERIT-HF, CIBIS-II, COPERNICUS).

beta-adrenergic blockers β-blockers beta-adrenoreceptor antagonists

Overview

Beta-blockers act by competitive, reversible antagonism at β-adrenergic receptors — primarily the β1 subtype (cardiac, renal juxtaglomerular) for agents most used in heart disease — blocking the physiological actions of norepinephrine and epinephrine at those receptors.

Their clinical utility spans multiple indications:

HFrEF — reduce all-cause mortality by ~34% vs placebo, one of the strongest survival effects in cardiovascular pharmacology. Hypertension — lower cardiac output and, over weeks, vascular resistance. Stable angina — reduce heart rate and myocardial oxygen demand; extend time-to-ischemia on exercise. Post-MI prophylaxis — reduce reinfarction and sudden cardiac death. AF rate control — slow AV-nodal conduction. Certain arrhythmias — CPVT, long-QT, inappropriate sinus tachycardia.

The paradox that agents reducing contractility benefit patients with already-reduced contractility (HFrEF) was one of the great surprises of 20th-century cardiology. It is understood through chronic catecholamine toxicity: in HFrEF, persistently elevated sympathetic drive down-regulates β1-AR, promotes cardiomyocyte apoptosis, and drives pathological remodeling. Beta-blockers interrupt this vicious cycle.

Mechanism of Action

Molecular Target

Beta-blockers bind competitively to the orthosteric binding pocket of β-adrenergic receptors — the same pocket occupied by norepinephrine and epinephrine. By occupying this pocket without activating Gαs, they prevent receptor coupling to adenylyl cyclase, blocking the cAMP → PKA signaling cascade. Key PKA substrates affected in the heart:

Acute vs. Chronic Effects

The acute effect — reduced HR, contractility, and CO — is what naive physiology predicts and why beta-blockers are dangerous in acute decompensated HF. Their benefit in HFrEF is a chronic remodeling effect apparent over weeks to months:

Acute (days 1–7): Hemodynamic depression

Reduced HR, SV, and CO. Patients may feel worse. Do NOT initiate in decompensated HF — wait until euvolemic.

Weeks 2–8: Receptor up-regulation

Chronic β1-AR overstimulation in HFrEF down-regulates receptor density by ~50%. Beta-blocker therapy reduces activation → receptors up-regulate over weeks, restoring sensitivity.

Months 2–6: Reverse remodeling

Reduced catecholamine-driven apoptosis → less ongoing cardiomyocyte loss. Ventricles often decrease in volume; EF may improve dramatically (e.g., 15% → 35%). This is reverse remodeling — one of the most robust phenomena in heart failure pharmacotherapy.

Ongoing: Reduced arrhythmic death

Beta-blockade raises VF threshold, reduces ectopic automaticity, and blunts catecholamine surges that trigger fatal arrhythmias — the primary mechanism behind reduced sudden cardiac death.

Pharmacology

β1-Selectivity

Selectivity is dose-dependent. At therapeutic doses: metoprolol is ~75× more potent at β1 vs β2; bisoprolol ~100×; carvedilol is non-selective (β1 = β2), plus α1 blockade. At high doses, "cardioselective" agents lose β2 selectivity — important in patients with asthma or COPD.

Lipophilicity and CNS Penetration

Lipophilic agents (metoprolol, carvedilol, propranolol) cross the blood-brain barrier → greater CNS side effects (vivid dreams, fatigue, depression). Hydrophilic agents (atenolol, nadolol; bisoprolol is intermediate) have less CNS penetration and may be preferred in susceptible patients.

Side Effects

  • Bradycardia and AV block — dose-limiting; monitor HR and PR interval; titrate up slowly
  • Hypotension — especially carvedilol (α1 blockade); initiate at low dose in HFrEF and titrate over weeks
  • Fatigue — common at initiation; often improves at 4–8 weeks as remodeling progresses
  • Worsening HF symptoms at initiation — defer start if decompensated; begin only when euvolemic
  • Bronchospasm — avoid non-selective agents in asthma; β1-selective agents can be used cautiously in stable COPD
  • Sexual dysfunction — particularly with non-selective and lipophilic agents
  • Masking hypoglycemia — blunts tachycardia (not sweating) in insulin-dependent diabetics; use cautiously
  • CNS effects — vivid dreams, fatigue, depression with lipophilic agents (metoprolol, propranolol); consider switching to hydrophilic agent
