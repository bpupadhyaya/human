Peripheral Nerve

A peripheral nerve is a cable-like bundle of axons conveying motor commands and sensory signals between the CNS and the body periphery, organised by three concentric connective tissue sheaths and classified by conduction velocity, diameter, and myelination state.

Schwann cells are the defining glial partners of the peripheral nervous system: myelinating Schwann cells ensheath large-diameter axons in ~150 compact myelin layers enabling saltatory conduction at up to 120 m/s, while non-myelinating Schwann cells cradle multiple unmyelinated C fibres in Remak bundles.

Peripheral Nerve

Peripheral nerves are bundles of axons enveloped in three hierarchical connective tissue sheaths — endoneurium, perineurium, epineurium — and populated by Schwann cells that myelinate large-diameter fibres for rapid saltatory conduction or ensheath small C fibres in Remak bundles. The peripheral nervous system uniquely supports axon regeneration after injury via Wallerian degeneration, Schwann cell dedifferentiation, and Bands of Büngner.

Overview

A peripheral nerve is a discrete anatomical structure that acts as a bidirectional communication highway between the central nervous system and effector organs or sensory receptors in the body wall, limbs, and viscera. Unlike CNS white-matter tracts, peripheral nerves are encased in robust connective tissue sheaths and are served by an extrinsic blood supply (vasa nervorum), allowing them to be surgically identified, repaired, and grafted.

The functional diversity of peripheral nerves reflects the extraordinary range of information they carry: fast motor commands to skeletal muscle via thick, heavily myelinated Aα fibres; fine touch and vibration via Aβ fibres; sharp pain and temperature via thinly myelinated Aδ fibres; and slow burning pain, itch, and postganglionic autonomic signals via unmyelinated C fibres. Dysregulation of any fibre class — through demyelination, axon loss, or channel dysfunction — produces distinct, clinically recognisable sensory and motor deficits.

Schwann Cell Biology

Myelinating Schwann cells engage in a 1:1 relationship with large-diameter axons (≥1 µm). Each cell wraps its plasma membrane spirally around a segment of axon, compacting the cytoplasm to produce up to ~150 concentric myelin lamellae of 10–12 nm periodicity. The compact myelin is rich in specific proteins: PMP22 (peripheral myelin protein 22; ~16% of total myelin protein; mutations in 70% of Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease cases), MPZ/P0 (myelin protein zero; the major structural protein; mediates myelin compaction via homophilic adhesion at the extracellular domain), MBP (myelin basic protein; stabilises cytoplasmic apposition of myelin lamellae), and periaxin (PRX; maintains the myelin sheath through PDZ-domain scaffolding; mutations cause Dejerine–Sottas syndrome CMT4F).

Non-myelinating Schwann cells ensheath multiple unmyelinated C fibres (typically 3–10 axons per cell) within a shared cytoplasmic trough, forming Remak bundles. Each C fibre remains individually invested by a Schwann cell process but without compact myelin. The Remak configuration allows slow conduction of pain and autonomic signals; these fibres are selectively lost early in small-fibre neuropathy, detected by skin punch biopsy and quantification of intraepidermal nerve fibre density (IENFD).

Nodal molecular organisation is the mechanistic basis for saltatory conduction. Each myelinated internode is ~1–1.5 mm long and ends at a node of Ranvier — a naked 1–2 µm axon segment where action potential regeneration occurs:

Saltatory Conduction

In an unmyelinated C fibre, a depolarisation wave advances continuously along the membrane at 0.5–2 m/s, limited by membrane capacitance and internal axial resistance. In a myelinated axon, compact myelin increases membrane resistance ~500-fold and reduces capacitance ~50-fold, confining regenerative Na⁺ currents to nodes of Ranvier. The AP “jumps” from node to node — saltatory (from Latin saltare, to leap) conduction — at 70–120 m/s in Aα fibres. The energy cost is dramatically reduced because Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase activity only needs to restore ionic gradients at nodes rather than along the entire axon. A 100-fold increase in diameter in an unmyelinated fibre would only produce a ~10-fold increase in velocity, making myelination the evolutionarily favoured solution for fast conduction within the size constraints of peripheral nerves.

Sensory Specificity and Nociceptor Sensitisation

Sensory information is encoded both by the fibre type activated (labelled-line theory) and by the pattern of activity across fibre populations. Dorsal root ganglion (DRG) neurons are pseudo-unipolar: their central axon runs in the dorsal root to the spinal cord (synapsing in the dorsal horn or ascending directly in the dorsal columns), while the peripheral branch extends into skin, muscle, or viscera. Molecular transducers at peripheral terminals convert physical or chemical stimuli into receptor potentials:

Nociceptor sensitisation — the basis of inflammatory allodynia and hyperalgesia — occurs when local mediators lower the activation threshold of nociceptors. PGE&sub2; (cyclo-oxygenase product) activates EP2/EP4 receptors → Gs → adenylyl cyclase → cAMP → PKA phosphorylates Nav1.8 (shifts activation threshold ~10–15 mV negative). Bradykinin activates B1/B2 receptors → PLC → DAG → PKC phosphorylates Nav1.8 and TRPV1. NGF binds TrkA → PI3K/Akt → increased Nav1.8 trafficking to membrane; NGF also sensitises TRPV1 via PLC-mediated PIP&sub2; depletion. These cascades converge on a peripheral sensitised state in which fibres fire at stimuli that previously did not activate them (allodynia) or fire more vigorously (hyperalgesia).

Wallerian Degeneration and Peripheral Nerve Regeneration

The peripheral nervous system retains a regenerative capacity entirely absent from the CNS, owing to the dedifferentiation programme of Schwann cells. After axon transection or crush:

  1. Early axonal events (0–24 h): The distal axon stump undergoes rapid granular degeneration (Wallerian degeneration); axonal proteases (calpain, caspase) activated by Ca²⁺ influx; microtubule disassembly; axonal contents fragmented
  2. Myelin breakdown (days 1–14): Schwann cells downregulate myelin genes (PMP22, MPZ, MBP, periaxin) and upregulate c-Jun — the master transcription factor of the repair Schwann cell; c-Jun drives expression of GDNF, BDNF, NGF, artemin, p75NTR, and cell adhesion molecules
  3. Macrophage recruitment (days 2–14): Schwann cells and degenerating axons secrete MCP-1/CCL2 and MIP-1α/CCL3 → haematogenous macrophages infiltrate → phagocytose myelin debris (crucial clearance step absent in CNS Wallerian degeneration, explaining the slower CNS regeneration failure)
  4. Bands of Büngner formation (days 3–21): Dedifferentiated Schwann cells proliferate and align within the intact endoneurial tubes (basal lamina tubes), forming longitudinal cellular columns — Bands of Büngner — that secrete ECM (laminin, fibronectin, collagen IV) and pro-regenerative factors (BDNF, GDNF, CNTF, NGF) to guide regrowing axons
  5. Axon regrowth (~1 mm/day): Regenerating axon growth cone (actin + microtubule dynamics) advances distally at ~1–3 mm/day guided by laminin-1/integrin α6β1 interactions; Schwann cell “tunnel” provides directional cues; reinnervation of targets (muscle, skin) may take weeks to months for proximal injuries
  6. Re-myelination: Once axons contact their targets, Schwann cells cease c-Jun expression, re-differentiate, and re-myelinate regrowing axons; new internodes are shorter and thinner than the originals, accounting for reduced conduction velocity after regeneration

CNS axons fail to regenerate because: (1) CNS myelin-associated inhibitors (Nogo-A, MAG, OMgp) activate RhoA/ROCK growth cone collapse; (2) oligodendrocytes do not support the c-Jun repair programme; (3) astrocytic glial scar deposits CSPGs; (4) injured CNS axons have reduced intrinsic growth capacity (low PTEN/mTOR signalling). Overcoming these barriers is a major focus of spinal cord injury research.

Pathology

Diabetic Peripheral Neuropathy (DPN)

Most common peripheral neuropathy worldwide; affects ~50% of patients with long-standing diabetes. Pathomechanisms: hyperglycaemia activates polyol pathway (aldose reductase → sorbitol accumulation → osmotic stress + NAD⁺ depletion); advanced glycation end-products (AGEs) cross-link nerve structural proteins and activate RAGE → NF-κB → inflammatory cytokines; PKC-β activation → impairs vasodilatory prostaglandin production → endoneurial ischaemia; reactive oxygen species damage mitochondria in DRG neurons and Schwann cells. Pattern: length-dependent, distal-symmetric (glove-and-stocking); small fibres earliest affected (burning pain, impaired temperature sensation); large fibres later (vibration loss, areflexia, gait ataxia); autonomic neuropathy coexists (orthostatic hypotension, gastroparesis, erectile dysfunction). IENFD reduced on skin biopsy. No disease-modifying treatment; management: glycaemic control, duloxetine/pregabalin/gabapentin for pain.

Guillain-Barré Syndrome (GBS)

Acute immune-mediated polyradiculoneuropathy; discussed in detail on the Guillain-Barré page. Rapidly ascending weakness and areflexia following molecular mimicry to ganglioside epitopes. Treatment: IVIG 2 g/kg or plasma exchange; 20–30% require ventilatory support.

Charcot–Marie–Tooth Disease (CMT)

Most common inherited peripheral neuropathy; >80 causative genes. CMT1A (~70% of CMT1): PMP22 duplication (chromosome 17p11.2) → PMP22 overexpression → Schwann cell dysfunction and onion-bulb formation (repeated demyelination-remyelination). CMT1B: MPZ/P0 mutations → impaired myelin compaction. CMTX1: Cx32 (GJB1) mutations → gap junction loss in paranodal loops. CMT2A: MFN2 mutations → mitochondrial fusion defect → axonal CMT. Clinical phenotype: distal muscle weakness and wasting (peroneal muscles first, “inverted champagne bottle” legs), high-arched feet (pes cavus), hammer toes, slow nerve conduction velocities (<38 m/s in CMT1); usually presents in first two decades.

Carpal Tunnel Syndrome (CTS)

Most common mononeuropathy; compression of the median nerve at the carpal tunnel by thickened flexor retinaculum. Paraesthesiae, numbness, and pain in the median nerve territory (lateral 3½ digits); nocturnal waking; weak thenar eminence in severe cases. Associations: pregnancy, hypothyroidism, rheumatoid arthritis, acromegaly, DM, obesity. NCS: prolonged distal motor latency (DML) and sensory NCS across the wrist. Treatment: splinting, corticosteroid injection, surgical carpal tunnel decompression.

Hereditary Neuropathy with Liability to Pressure Palsies (HNPP)

PMP22 deletion (17p11.2; reciprocal of CMT1A duplication) → reduced PMP22 expression → focally thickened myelin (tomacula) at common compression sites. Recurrent, painless pressure palsies (peroneal nerve at fibular head, ulnar at elbow, median at wrist) lasting days to weeks; complete recovery typical. NCS: prolonged distal latencies and conduction block at compression sites.
