Placenta

The transient, semi-allogeneic organ of mammalian pregnancy — a marvel of evolutionary engineering that simultaneously accomplishes gas and nutrient exchange, hormone production, active immune tolerance of the genetically foreign fetus, and selective passive transfer of maternal antibodies to the newborn.

Overview

The human placenta is a discoid, hemochorial organ of dual fetal–maternal origin that is established during the first trimester of pregnancy and expelled at delivery. It is the primary site of physiological exchange between the maternal and fetal circulations — mediating bidirectional transfer of O2, CO2, glucose, amino acids, fatty acids, vitamins, water, IgG antibodies, and waste products — while physically separating the two bloodstreams to prevent immune conflict and ensure the fetus retains its own distinct circulation at lower pressure.

Functionally, the placenta acts simultaneously as lung (gas exchange), gut (nutrient acquisition), kidney (waste excretion), endocrine organ (hCG, progesterone, estrogen, hPL, leptin), and immune organ (tolerance induction and antibody transfer). Its immunological achievement is particularly remarkable: it must prevent the maternal immune system from destroying the semi-allogeneic fetus (which expresses paternal HLA antigens) while simultaneously permitting passage of protective maternal IgG to immunize the newborn.

Placental dysfunction underlies some of the most serious complications of pregnancy, including preeclampsia, intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR), and placenta accreta spectrum — conditions that collectively account for significant maternal and perinatal morbidity and mortality worldwide.

Development and Villous Architecture

Implantation and early trophoblast invasion (days 6–12)

Approximately 6–7 days post-fertilization, the blastocyst adheres to the endometrial epithelium and trophoblast cells begin invading the decidua (progesterone-primed maternal endometrium). The trophoblast lineage rapidly differentiates into two major populations:

  • Villous cytotrophoblasts (CTBs): mononuclear proliferative stem cells that line the chorionic villi; serve as a progenitor pool for both syncytiotrophoblast and extravillous trophoblast
  • Syncytiotrophoblast (STB): formed by fusion of CTBs; the continuous multinucleated outer layer of the floating chorionic villi that is bathed in maternal blood in the intervillous space from ~10–12 weeks; the primary site of nutrient and gas exchange; also the dominant placental endocrine tissue

Extravillous trophoblasts (EVTs) and spiral artery remodeling

Extravillous trophoblasts (EVTs) are non-villous invasive cells that migrate from anchoring villi deep into the maternal decidua and inner myometrium. Their most critical function is remodeling the maternal spiral arteries: physiologically, EVTs replace vascular smooth muscle and endothelium in the spiral arterioles with a trophoblast lining, transforming these narrow, high-resistance vessels into wide, low-resistance, non-reactive conduits that deliver high-flow maternal blood into the intervillous space. This process, called physiological transformation, increases spiral artery diameter 5–10-fold and is complete by 20 weeks.

Proper EVT invasion is critical for adequate uteroplacental perfusion. Inadequate remodeling (shallow EVT invasion, incomplete spiral artery transformation) is the central defect in preeclampsia, causing ischemia-reperfusion injury, oxidative stress, and excess release of anti-angiogenic factors from the placenta into the maternal circulation.

Intervillous space and villous tree

From approximately 10–12 weeks, following the lysis of the endometrial Nitabuch layer and plugs previously sealing the spiral artery ostia, maternal blood flows into the intervillous space — the lacunar network between the branching chorionic villi. The villous tree consists of:

  • Stem villi: large, structurally supportive villi with a fibrous stroma and large fetal vessels; carry the bulk of fetal blood into the peripheral villi
  • Intermediate villi: transitional branching structures
  • Terminal (floating) villi: the primary exchange units; thin syncytiotrophoblast layer (<1 μm at term), fetal capillaries pushed to the periphery, minimal stromal tissue; surface area estimated at 10–14 m2 at term

Endocrine Functions

Human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG)

hCG is a glycoprotein hormone (LH/FSH family, α-β dimer) secreted predominantly by the syncytiotrophoblast, beginning at implantation and peaking at approximately 8–10 weeks of gestation. Its primary function in early pregnancy is to maintain the corpus luteum — preventing luteolysis and thereby sustaining progesterone secretion until the placenta itself assumes progesterone production (“luteal-placental shift”) at approximately 8–10 weeks. hCG also has immunomodulatory roles (promoting uterine NK cell tolerance) and promotes trophoblast differentiation and invasion via LH/CG receptors on EVTs and decidual cells. Serum hCG doubling every 48 hours in weeks 4–8 is the basis of pregnancy tests; failure to rise appropriately signals ectopic pregnancy or impending miscarriage.

Progesterone

From week 8–10 onward, the syncytiotrophoblast takes over progesterone synthesis from the corpus luteum, utilizing maternal cholesterol (via LDL receptor uptake and mitochondrial CYP11A1/StAR pathway) as the precursor. Placental progesterone production rises progressively throughout pregnancy, reaching 250–400 mg/day at term. Progesterone maintains uterine quiescence (smooth muscle relaxation via cGMP and K-channel mechanisms), promotes cervical mucus plugging, suppresses maternal T cell responses (Th2 bias, PIBF — progesterone-induced blocking factor), and is required for continued pregnancy — progesterone receptor antagonism (mifepristone) induces labor and is used medically for pregnancy termination and labor induction.

Estrogens

The placenta produces large amounts of estrone (E1), estradiol (E2), and uniquely estriol (E3) via a feto-placental unit: the fetal adrenal cortex produces DHEA-S → the placenta converts it via aromatase (CYP19A1) to estrogens. Estriol predominates in pregnancy and is used as a maternal serum marker in prenatal screening (low uE3 = marker of trisomy 18 and Smith-Lemli-Opitz syndrome). Estrogens drive uterine growth, upregulate oxytocin receptors (transition to labor), increase uteroplacental blood flow, and promote mammary gland development.

Human placental lactogen (hPL) and other hormones

hPL (GH family) rises throughout pregnancy and acts as an anti-insulin, lipolytic agent — mobilizing maternal free fatty acids for fetal energy use while inducing maternal insulin resistance (redirecting glucose toward the fetus). It also has lactogenic effects similar to prolactin. Leptin is produced by STB and regulates maternal energy metabolism and fetal growth. Placental growth hormone (GH-V) gradually replaces pituitary GH in the maternal circulation from mid-gestation, driving IGF-1-mediated nutrient partitioning toward the fetus.

Maternal Immune Tolerance of the Semi-Allogeneic Fetus

The immunological paradox of pregnancy — why a fetus expressing paternal alloantigens is not rejected like an organ transplant — has fascinated immunologists for decades. The solution is multifactorial and operates at the level of the trophoblast surface, the decidua, and systemic maternal immune modulation.

HLA class I expression by trophoblasts

Trophoblasts at the maternal–fetal interface (EVTs and STB) have a unique HLA pattern: they do not express the classical polymorphic transplant antigens HLA-A and HLA-B, which would trigger direct allorecognition by maternal cytotoxic T cells (CTLs). Instead, they express:

  • HLA-C: classical, polymorphic; interacts with maternal decidual NK (dNK) cell KIR receptors; KIR2DL1/KIR2DS1–HLA-C2 and KIR2DL2/KIR2DS2–HLA-C1 interactions modulate dNK tolerance vs. activation; certain KIR–HLA-C combinations are associated with higher risk of preeclampsia and IUGR when inhibitory tolerance signals are insufficient
  • HLA-E: non-classical, non-polymorphic; presents peptides derived from signal sequences of other HLA molecules; binds inhibitory NKG2A/CD94 on dNK cells, preventing their activation; also expressed on STB and provides broad tolerance signal
  • HLA-G: non-classical, limited polymorphism; strongly expressed on EVTs; binds ILT2 (LILRB1) and ILT4 (LILRB2) inhibitory receptors on macrophages, DCs, NK cells, and T cells → suppresses cytotoxic activity; induces Treg differentiation; a “master tolerance molecule” of pregnancy; soluble HLA-G shed into maternal blood has systemic immunosuppressive effects

Decidual NK cells (dNK) — CD56bright, CD16−

The decidua is massively infiltrated by decidual NK cells (dNK), comprising up to 70% of decidual lymphocytes in the first trimester. Unlike peripheral blood NK cells (CD56dimCD16+ cytotoxic), dNK are CD56brightCD16− — the same immunophenotype as the least cytotoxic peripheral NK subset. Under the influence of progesterone, TGF-β, HLA-G, and local decidual signals, dNK are reprogrammed away from cytotoxicity and toward tolerance and tissue remodeling: they secrete VEGF, PlGF, and angiopoietin to support spiral artery remodeling, produce cytokines (IL-8, CXCL10) that guide EVT invasion, and do not kill HLA-G+ EVTs despite their NK effector capacity in other contexts.

Regulatory T cells and Th2 bias

Pregnancy is associated with a systemic expansion of Foxp3+ regulatory T cells (Tregs) specific for paternal alloantigens. Tregs are induced in early pregnancy during T cell priming by paternal antigen-presenting DCs in the decidua and draining lymph nodes; progesterone and IDO expressed by trophoblast and decidual cells promote Treg differentiation. Maternal CD4+ effector T cell responses are skewed toward a Th2 cytokine profile (IL-4, IL-10, IL-13) which suppresses inflammatory Th1 (IFN-γ, TNF-α) responses that would threaten fetal survival. This Th2 shift is also why some autoimmune diseases (rheumatoid arthritis, multiple sclerosis) remit during pregnancy, while others with Th2 pathology (SLE, asthma) can worsen.

IDO and tryptophan depletion

Trophoblast and decidual macrophages express indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO), which catabolizes the essential amino acid tryptophan to kynurenine. Local tryptophan depletion inhibits T cell activation (tryptophan is required for mTOR signaling in T cells) and kynurenine metabolites directly suppress T cell proliferation and promote Treg differentiation via the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR). IDO-deficient mice show fetal resorption in allogeneic but not syngeneic pregnancies, demonstrating the critical role of this pathway in preventing fetal rejection.

Maternal IgG Transfer to the Fetus

The human fetus is born with a naive immune system that has never been challenged by pathogen or self-antigen in an inflammatory context. Neonatal protection against infection in the first weeks to months of life therefore depends on maternal IgG transferred across the placenta during gestation. This transfer is active, selective, and substantial.

FcRn-mediated transcytosis

The neonatal Fc receptor (FcRn, encoded by FCGRT) is expressed on syncytiotrophoblast and mediates pH-dependent IgG transcytosis:

  1. Maternal IgG in the intervillous space is taken up by STB via fluid-phase or FcRn-dependent endocytosis at physiological pH (~7.4, low binding)
  2. Endosomal acidification (pH ~6.0–6.5) dramatically increases FcRn–IgG Fc affinity; IgG is retained and protected from lysosomal degradation
  3. The FcRn–IgG complex is transcytosed to the basal membrane and IgG is released into fetal circulation at physiological pH

Transfer is most active in the third trimester (peak from week 28–40), when fetal IgG levels rise steeply and can exceed maternal levels at term due to active accumulation. At birth, neonatal IgG concentrations approximate or slightly exceed maternal levels.

IgG subclass selectivity

FcRn binds all four IgG subclasses but with differential affinities (at pH 6.0: IgG1 ≈ IgG4 > IgG3 > IgG2). The hierarchy of placental IgG transfer is approximately: IgG1 > IgG4 > IgG3 > IgG2. IgG2, which is important for protection against encapsulated bacteria (pneumococcus, meningococcus) in older children, is transferred least efficiently — this partly explains the susceptibility of young infants to these pathogens.

Clinical implications: protection and harm

Protective transfer

Maternal IgG against measles, RSV, pertussis, tetanus, herpes simplex, CMV, and most vaccine-targeted antigens provides passive immunity to the neonate for 3–6 months. Maternal pertussis and flu vaccination in pregnancy is specifically recommended to boost transfer of protective IgG to the fetus before birth, when the newborn is most vulnerable.

Pathological transfer

Maternal IgG against fetal red cell antigens (anti-D, anti-K, anti-c) causes hemolytic disease of the fetus and newborn (HDFN); anti-HPA-1a against fetal platelet GPIIIa causes fetal/neonatal alloimmune thrombocytopenia (FNAIT); anti-Ro/SSA and anti-La/SSB in SLE mothers cause neonatal lupus and congenital heart block; maternal anti-TSH receptor antibodies (Graves’) cause neonatal hyperthyroidism.

Placental Pathology

Preeclampsia

Preeclampsia (PE) complicates 2–8% of pregnancies and is a leading cause of maternal and perinatal mortality. The primary defect is inadequate EVT invasion and spiral artery remodeling, resulting in persistently narrow, high-resistance, ischemic-prone placental vasculature. Placental ischemia–reperfusion generates oxidative stress and triggers the release of anti-angiogenic factors — most critically sFlt-1 (soluble FMS-like tyrosine kinase-1, sVEGFR-1) — into maternal circulation. sFlt-1 acts as a decoy receptor, sequestering free VEGF and PlGF and causing maternal endothelial dysfunction, hypertension, proteinuria, and multi-organ damage.

The PlGF:sFlt-1 ratio in maternal blood is now an established biomarker for PE risk stratification (PlGF < 100 pg/mL or PlGF/sFlt-1 ratio < 38 at 20–35 weeks is predictive). Low-dose aspirin prophylaxis (150 mg/day from 11–14 weeks in high-risk women) reduces the risk of early preeclampsia by approximately 62% (Rolnik et al., ASPRE trial, 2017). Severe preeclampsia and HELLP syndrome (hemolysis, elevated liver enzymes, low platelets) require delivery as definitive treatment.

SARS-CoV-2 placentitis

Severe SARS-CoV-2 infection during pregnancy has been associated with a specific pathological pattern of placental injury termed SARS-CoV-2 placentitis, characterized by three histological findings: (1) massive perivillous fibrin deposition encasing villi and impairing intervillous perfusion; (2) chronic histiocytic intervillositis with CD68+ macrophage infiltration of the intervillous space; and (3) syncytiotrophoblast necrosis, with STB expressing ACE2 and TMPRSS2 and being directly infected by SARS-CoV-2. This triad leads to severe placental dysfunction, fetal hypoxia, stillbirth (risk increased 2–4-fold), and preterm birth. The finding of SARS-CoV-2 antigen (by immunohistochemistry) or RNA within STB has been documented in multiple series.

Fetal programming: the Barker hypothesis

The Developmental Origins of Health and Disease (DOHaD) hypothesis, originally proposed by David Barker, holds that placental insufficiency and IUGR program the developing fetus for increased susceptibility to cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, hypertension, and metabolic syndrome in adult life, through permanent epigenetic modification of gene expression in critical organs (hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis, pancreatic β cells, vascular endothelium, liver). Mechanisms include altered glucocorticoid exposure (11βHSD2 in placenta normally inactivates maternal cortisol protecting the fetus; IUGR reduces 11βHSD2 expression), altered methylation of HPA axis genes, and permanent reduction in nephron number predisposing to hypertension.

Cross-Atlas Connections

  • Transfers IgG — FcRn-mediated active transcytosis of maternal IgG across syncytiotrophoblast is the primary source of neonatal humoral immunity; IgG1>IgG4>IgG3>IgG2 hierarchy; peak transfer in third trimester; can transfer both protective and pathological antibodies
  • IgA context Secretory IgA — unlike IgG, IgA does not cross the placenta efficiently (no placental FcRα); neonates receive sIgA via colostrum and breast milk rather than transplacental transfer; this distinction is clinically important for mucosal immunity in the newborn gut
  • Vaccine design Vaccines — maternal vaccination during pregnancy is a key public health strategy to boost IgG transfer of vaccine-specific antibodies to the neonate; pertussis (Tdap), influenza, and COVID-19 mRNA vaccines are recommended in pregnancy specifically to leverage placental IgG transfer
  • Hypertension Hypertension — preeclampsia is a major form of pregnancy-induced hypertension driven by sFlt-1/VEGF imbalance from the ischemic placenta; placental origin is evidenced by resolution after delivery; elevated sFlt-1 precedes clinical hypertension by 4–5 weeks
  • RAAS Aldosterone — pregnancy is associated with marked activation of the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (plasma renin activity rises 3–5-fold, aldosterone rises 10–20-fold); the placenta produces prorenin and contributes to RAAS activation; normal pregnancy is refractory to Ang II despite high levels due to endothelial PGI2 and NO production — preeclampsia abolishes this refractoriness
  • GC / IgA Germinal Center — maternal IgG transferred via placenta is the product of germinal center affinity maturation in the mother; IgG1 (the best-transferred isotype) is generated by Tfh-driven GC reactions; vaccine immunization in pregnancy relies on GC responses to generate high-affinity, class-switched IgG for efficient FcRn transfer
