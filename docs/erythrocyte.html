Erythrocyte

The oxygen courier — an anucleate biconcave disc exquisitely engineered around haemoglobin, delivering ~250 mL O₂/min to tissues at rest while cycling ~300 km of vasculature over a 120-day lifespan.

The most abundant cell in the human body (~25 trillion circulating), the erythrocyte is terminally differentiated and almost entirely devoted to gas exchange. Each cell packs ~280 million haemoglobin tetramers, each binding four O₂ cooperatively (Hill coefficient ~2.7). Production is driven by erythropoietin (EPO) from the kidney in response to hypoxia; clearance is handled by splenic macrophages recognising senescence signals at day ~120. Haemoglobin disorders — sickle-cell disease, thalassaemia — represent the world's most common single-gene diseases, affecting hundreds of millions.

Atlas One · Cellular · Gas Transport / Cardiovascular Component

Erythrocyte

Lineage: Erythroid  ·  Origin: Bone marrow (HSC → BFU-E → reticulocyte)  |  Scale: 04-Cellular  |  Key molecule: Haemoglobin A (HbA; 2α + 2β subunits)

The erythrocyte (red blood cell) is the body's principal oxygen courier and the most abundant human cell by count. Terminally differentiated and devoid of nucleus, mitochondria, and most organelles, it is built almost entirely around ~280 million haemoglobin tetramers that bind O₂ with exquisite cooperativity. The biconcave disc geometry, maintained by a spectrin-actin cytoskeletal lattice, maximises surface-area-to-volume ratio and enables deformability through capillaries 3 µm wide. Gas exchange is bidirectional: O₂ is loaded in the lungs (PO₂ ~100 mmHg) and unloaded at tissues (PO₂ ~40 mmHg), while CO₂ returns largely as bicarbonate via carbonic anhydrase II. Erythropoietin (EPO) from the kidney drives production at ~2 million cells per second; senescent cells at day ~120 are cleared by splenic red-pulp macrophages with iron recycled back into the bone marrow. Disorders of haemoglobin structure and production — sickle-cell disease, thalassaemia — are the world's most prevalent inherited conditions.

Overview

The erythrocyte is the most abundant cell in the human body — approximately 25 trillion circulate in an adult — and the principal vehicle for respiratory gas transport. Normal reference ranges: 4.5–5.5 × 10¹² cells/L (males), 4.0–5.0 × 10¹² cells/L (females). Haematocrit: 40–52% males, 36–46% females. Mean corpuscular volume (MCV): 80–100 fL. The average adult contains ~5 litres of blood, of which ~45% is cellular (overwhelmingly erythrocytes).

Despite its near-universal familiarity, the erythrocyte is among the most structurally and biochemically specialised cells in biology. Terminally differentiated and devoid of nucleus, it is built almost entirely around haemoglobin and exquisitely engineered for deformability in capillaries as narrow as 3 µm. Without mitochondria, it relies entirely on anaerobic glycolysis for ATP and the pentose phosphate pathway for oxidative protection via NADPH and glutathione.

Erythrocytes are produced in red bone marrow at a rate of ~2 million per second (~200 billion per day) and survive ~120 days before being cleared by splenic and hepatic macrophages. Production is tightly regulated by erythropoietin (EPO), a glycoprotein hormone synthesised primarily by peritubular interstitial cells of the renal cortex in response to hypoxia (HIF-2α → EPO transcription). Understanding erythrocyte physiology is foundational for anaemia management, transfusion medicine, haemoglobinopathy treatment, and sports medicine.

Structure

FeatureDetail
Size & Shape Biconcave disc, 7.5–8.0 µm diameter, 2.0 µm thick at rim, 1.0 µm at centre. Volume: ~90 fL. Biconcave geometry maximises surface-area-to-volume ratio (~90 µm²) to minimise O₂/CO₂ diffusion distances and confers elastic deformability for passage through 3–4 µm capillaries. No nucleus, no mitochondria — the most streamlined gas-exchange platform in biology.
Membrane Lipid bilayer (outer leaflet: phosphatidylcholine + sphingomyelin; inner: phosphatidylethanolamine + phosphatidylserine) anchored to a 2-D spectrin–actin cytoskeletal lattice via ankyrin-1 and protein 4.1R. Phospholipid asymmetry maintained by flippase ATP11C — its collapse (PS externalisation) is the primary eat-me signal for senescent RBC clearance. Band 3 (AE1/SLC4A1): Cl−/HCO₃− exchanger and primary cytoskeletal anchor; its oxidative clustering in aged RBCs triggers IgG binding and phagocytosis.
Haemoglobin ~250–280 million HbA tetramers per cell (2α + 2β subunits). Each subunit: one iron-protoporphyrin IX (haem) group coordinated by proximal histidine (His F8). O₂ binds at distal face with cooperativity (T-state ↔ R-state quaternary transitions; Hill coefficient ~2.7–3.0; P₅₀ ≈ 26 mmHg). 2,3-Bisphosphoglycerate (2,3-BPG) binds T-state cleft, stabilising deoxyhaemoglobin and right-shifting the dissociation curve to facilitate tissue O₂ unloading. HbF (2α + 2γ) does not bind 2,3-BPG → left-shifted curve → higher O₂ affinity (advantageous for placental gas exchange).
Organelles None in mature erythrocyte. Reticulocytes retain ribosomes and mitochondria (lost within ~24 hours of entering circulation via selective autophagy). Absence of mitochondria eliminates aerobic respiration — prevents the cell from consuming the O₂ it is transporting.
Surface markers CD235a (glycophorin A — blood group MN antigens; malaria receptor for P. falciparum EBA-175); CD235b (glycophorin B); CD47 ("don't-eat-me" signal, downregulated on senescent RBCs); ABO antigens (glycolipid); Rh antigens (RhD, RhCE — clinically critical for transfusion compatibility). CD35 (CR1 — complement receptor); Duffy antigen (DARC — P. vivax receptor; absent in Duffy-null West Africans)
Metabolism Entirely anaerobic. Embden-Meyerhof pathway (glycolysis) produces 2 ATP per glucose; ATP maintains Na&sup+/K&sup+-ATPase, Ca²&sup+-ATPase, and flippase. Rapoport-Luebering shunt: 1,3-BPG → 2,3-BPG (bypasses ATP-generating step; ~20% of glucose flux at altitude or anaemia). Pentose phosphate pathway (PPP): glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) generates NADPH → glutathione reductase → reduced glutathione (GSH) → neutralises H₂O₂ and oxidant stress. G6PD deficiency (>400 million affected) renders RBCs susceptible to oxidative haemolysis with primaquine, dapsone, fava beans.

Function

Oxygen Transport

RBCs load O₂ in pulmonary capillaries (PO₂ ~100 mmHg; Hb ≥97% saturated) and unload at tissues (PO₂ ~40 mmHg; ~75% saturation). Net delivery: ~22% fractional release × 150 g Hb/L × 1.34 mL O₂/g Hb × 5 L/min CO = ~250 mL O₂/min at rest, rising to 3–4 L/min at maximal exercise.

CO₂ Transport & pH Buffering

~70% of CO₂ transported as HCO₃− (carbonic anhydrase II: CO₂ + H₂O → H&sup+ + HCO₃−; >10,000-fold acceleration). HCO₃− exits via band-3/AE1 in exchange for Cl− (chloride shift). ~23% as carbamino compounds on globin. ~7% dissolved. HbA acts as intracellular buffer (pKa ~6.8), dampening tissue-level pH swings.

Bohr & Haldane Effects

Rising tissue CO₂ lowers pH → H&sup+ stabilises T-state (tense/deoxy) → right-shifts O₂ dissociation curve (Bohr effect) → enhanced O₂ unloading. Reciprocally, deoxygenation increases haemoglobin CO₂ capacity (Haldane effect). Temperature rise at exercising muscle also right-shifts the curve. These are allosteric reciprocals of the same T↔R quaternary change.

Nitric Oxide & Vascular Tone

Oxyhaemoglobin scavenges free NO (NO + HbO₂ → metHb + NO₃−) limiting vasodilation. S-nitrosylation of Hbβ-Cys93 in the lungs may allow NO release at deoxygenation, contributing to hypoxic vasodilation. ATP released from RBCs via pannexin-1/CFTR under deformation or hypoxia activates endothelial purinergic receptors → eNOS → vasodilation.

Pathology

DiseaseMechanismClinical FeaturesTreatment
Iron-Deficiency Anaemia Insufficient iron for haem synthesis → microcytic, hypochromic RBCs (low MCV, low MCH). Most common nutritional deficiency globally; causes: poor intake, malabsorption (coeliac), blood loss (GI, menstrual). EPO-driven erythropoiesis continues but fails to produce normal Hb. Fatigue, pallor, dyspnoea on exertion, koilonychia (spoon nails), pica. Hb typically <12 g/dL (women), <13 g/dL (men); MCV <80 fL; ferritin low; TIBC high. Oral ferrous sulphate/gluconate; IV iron (ferric carboxymaltose) for malabsorption or severe cases; identify and treat underlying cause
Sickle-Cell Disease HbS: β-Glu6Val substitution. Under hypoxia, deoxyHbS polymerises into rigid fibres → RBC sickling → vaso-occlusion, haemolysis. Sickling cycle: hypoxia/dehydration → polymerisation → cell rigidity → microvascular occlusion → ischaemia → more hypoxia. Homozygous (HbSS) most severe. Painful vaso-occlusive crises; acute chest syndrome (pulmonary infarction); stroke; splenic infarction → functional asplenia; haemolytic anaemia (Hb ~7–9 g/dL); priapism; renal papillary necrosis; avascular necrosis of femoral head. Hydroxyurea (↑ HbF; anti-sickling); voxelotor (Hb polymerisation inhibitor); crizanlizumab (anti-P-selectin); transfusion; curative gene therapy (LentiGlobin, exa-cel CRISPR-based γ-globin induction)
Thalassaemia α-thalassaemia: deletion of 1–4 α-globin genes (HBA1/HBA2 on chr 16). β-thalassaemia: point mutations reducing/abolishing β-globin synthesis. Imbalanced globin chain production → excess unpaired chains precipitate → ineffective erythropoiesis + haemolysis. β-thal major (transfusion-dependent): <1 g/dL Hb without transfusion; marrow expansion → facial bone deformity, hepatosplenomegaly. β-thal major: severe anaemia requiring lifelong transfusion from infancy; iron overload → cardiac siderosis, cirrhosis, endocrine failure (diabetes, hypogonadism). HbH disease (3-gene α-deletion): haemolytic anaemia. Regular transfusion + iron chelation (deferoxamine, deferasirox); luspatercept (TGF-β trap reduces ineffective erythropoiesis); curative BMT / gene therapy (betibeglogene, LentiGlobin)
Hereditary Spherocytosis Mutations in spectrin (α or β), ankyrin-1, band 3, or protein 4.2 weaken vertical interaction between lipid bilayer and cytoskeleton. Membrane vesiculation → progressive loss of membrane → sphere shape (↓ deformability) → selective splenic trapping and haemolysis. Haemolytic anaemia, splenomegaly, jaundice, pigment gallstones. Osmotic fragility test positive. MCHC elevated (>36 g/dL). Coombs-negative haemolysis. Folic acid supplementation; splenectomy (reduces haemolysis but increases infection risk — prophylactic penicillin + pneumococcal/meningococcal vaccination required)
Polycythaemia Vera Myeloproliferative neoplasm: JAK2 V617F somatic mutation (~97% of cases) → constitutive JAK2/STAT5 activation → EPO-independent RBC overproduction. Also excess platelets and neutrophils. Erythrocytosis (Hb >16.5 g/dL men, >16 g/dL women), facial plethora, aquagenic pruritus, splenomegaly, hyperviscosity → thrombosis risk (stroke, DVT, Budd-Chiari syndrome). Phlebotomy to target Hct <45%; low-dose aspirin; hydroxyurea for high-risk patients; ruxolitinib (JAK1/2 inhibitor) for refractory disease

Malaria and the erythrocyte — evolution's battleground: Plasmodium falciparum requires the erythrocyte as its obligate intraerythrocytic host, invading via glycophorin A (EBA-175 ligand). The parasite digests haemoglobin as its nutrient source, exporting malarial proteins that remodel the RBC cytoskeleton. Sickle-cell trait (HbAS) provides ~90% protection against severe malaria — the clearest example of heterozygote advantage in human evolutionary biology. G6PD deficiency, α-thalassaemia, and Duffy-null phenotype are similarly concentrated in malaria-endemic regions, all representing evolved erythrocyte-level malaria resistance mechanisms.
