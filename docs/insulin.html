Insulin

The master anabolic hormone — a 51-amino acid peptide from pancreatic beta cells that gates glucose entry into cells, suppresses hepatic glucose production, and integrates energy storage across liver, muscle, and fat.

Secreted in two phases in response to rising blood glucose: a rapid first-phase (pre-formed vesicle release, 1–3 min) followed by a sustained second phase (newly synthesised insulin, 20–90 min). Absolute deficiency (T1DM) or relative deficiency with resistance (T2DM) together affect ~500 million people globally. The DCCT trial established that intensive insulin therapy reduces microvascular complications by 50–76%.

Insulin

Class: Peptide hormone  ·  Growth factor  ·  RTK ligand  |  Gene: INS (chr 11p15.5)  |  UniProt: P01308  |  Produced in: pancreatic β cells (islets of Langerhans)

Insulin is the central hormone of energy anabolism. Its discovery by Banting, Best, Macleod, and Collip in 1921–22 transformed type 1 diabetes from a universally fatal disease into a manageable chronic condition — one of the most consequential medical breakthroughs in history. Structurally, mature insulin is a two-chain disulfide-linked polypeptide (A-chain 21 aa + B-chain 30 aa) derived from the single-chain precursor proinsulin by removal of the C-peptide. It acts via the insulin receptor (INSR) — a receptor tyrosine kinase — to drive GLUT4 translocation in muscle and fat, suppress hepatic glucose output, and promote glycogen synthesis, lipogenesis, and protein synthesis. Understanding insulin signalling is foundational for understanding T1DM, T2DM, metabolic syndrome, and cardiovascular risk.

Structure and Processing

Insulin is synthesised as preproinsulin (110 aa) in pancreatic beta cells. The signal peptide (24 aa) is co-translationally cleaved in the ER to yield proinsulin (86 aa), a single chain that folds and forms two disulfide bonds between A and B chains (A7–B7, A20–B19) plus one intrachain bond in the A chain (A6–A11). In the Golgi/secretory granule, PC1/3 and PC2 (prohormone convertases) cleave the connecting C-peptide (31 aa), releasing mature insulin (A + B chains, 51 aa, 5.8 kDa) and C-peptide in equimolar amounts.

C-peptide as a clinical marker: C-peptide has a longer half-life than insulin (~30 min vs. ~5 min) and is not cleared by the liver on first pass — making it the preferred marker for endogenous insulin secretion. C-peptide is near-zero in T1DM (beta cell destruction) but present in T2DM and MODY; it distinguishes exogenous insulin injection from endogenous secretion in hypoglycaemia workup.

In solution, insulin forms hexamers (3 dimers) in the presence of Zn²⁺ — the storage form in secretory granules. This hexameric form is pharmacologically important: rapid-acting analogues (lispro, aspart, glulisine) have mutations that prevent hexamerisation → faster absorption as monomers from the SC injection site.

The K𝐘TP channel is the pharmacological nexus of sulfonylureas and GLP-1 agonists: Sulfonylureas (glibenclamide, glipizide, gliclazide) bind the SUR1 subunit and close K𝐘TP channels independent of glucose — forcing insulin release regardless of blood glucose level and explaining their hypoglycaemia risk. GLP-1 receptor agonists (semaglutide, liraglutide) and DPP-4 inhibitors amplify insulin secretion in a glucose-dependent manner (amplifying pathways only active when glucose is elevated), explaining their lower hypoglycaemia risk.

Key downstream effects of insulin signalling

Liver: Inhibits glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis (via FOXO1 nuclear exclusion and PEPCK/G6Pase suppression); promotes glycogen synthesis (GSK-3 inhibition → glycogen synthase active); promotes lipogenesis (SREBP-1c activation → ↑FAS, ACC).

Skeletal muscle: GLUT4 translocation is the dominant postprandial glucose disposal pathway. Also promotes glycogen synthesis, protein synthesis (mTORC1 activation), and amino acid uptake.

Adipose: GLUT4 translocation; inhibits hormone-sensitive lipase (HSL) → suppresses lipolysis — this is insulin's most sensitive metabolic effect (half-maximal effect at ~10–20 pmol/L). In insulin resistance, this effect is impaired → elevated FFA → ectopic lipid deposition → hepatic and skeletal muscle insulin resistance (lipotoxicity).

Glucolipotoxicity, ER stress, amyloid (IAPP) deposits accelerate β cell loss Often asymptomatic early; detected on screening. Insidious onset. Complications driven by duration and glycaemic control. Typically in elderly T2DM with reduced thirst sensation and renal impairment. No significant ketoacidosis (residual insulin suppresses lipolysis) Gradual onset (days–weeks), altered consciousness, seizures, focal deficits. High mortality (5–20%). Hypoglycaemia Insulin excess relative to glucose availability Exogenous insulin overdose; insulin secretagogue (sulfonylurea, meglitinide); insulinoma; IGF-2-secreting tumour. Counter-regulatory response: glucagon (hepatic glycogenolysis), catecholamines, cortisol, GH. Hypoglycaemia unawareness develops after recurrent episodes (impaired autonomic response) Adrenergic symptoms (tremor, tachycardia, diaphoresis, anxiety) at BG ~3.5–4 mmol/L; neuroglycopenic symptoms (confusion, slurred speech, seizure, coma) at <2.8 mmol/L. Treatment: oral glucose if conscious; IV dextrose (25–50 ml 50% dextrose) or IM glucagon if not

The DCCT Trial — evidence that tight glycaemic control prevents microvascular complications: The Diabetes Control and Complications Trial (DCCT, 1993) randomised 1,441 T1DM patients to intensive insulin therapy (HbA1c target ~7%) vs. conventional therapy (~9%). After 6.5 years, intensive therapy reduced: retinopathy by 76% (primary prevention) and 54% (secondary prevention); nephropathy by 50%; neuropathy by 60%. Long-term follow-up (EDIC study) showed continued benefit 30+ years later ("metabolic memory") — early intensive control durably reprograms epigenetic marks in vascular tissue. The DCCT established HbA1c as the primary therapeutic target and <7% as the benchmark for intensive control in T1DM.
