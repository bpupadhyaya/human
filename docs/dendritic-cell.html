Dendritic Cell

The dendritic cell is the most potent professional antigen-presenting cell and the master bridge between innate detection and adaptive T cell priming. Immature DCs patrol tissues via macropinocytosis and receptor-mediated endocytosis; upon PAMP/DAMP sensing, NF-κB activation drives maturation — MHC-II and CD80/86 upregulation, CCR7 induction, lymph-node migration — where they prime naïve T cells. Three subsets: cDC1 (cross-presentation, CTL priming), cDC2 (MHC-II, Th priming), pDC (IFN-α/β factory).

Overview

The dendritic cell (DC) is the most potent professional antigen-presenting cell in the body and the master link between the innate and adaptive immune systems. Described by Ralph Steinman and Zanvil Cohn in 1973 (Steinman received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine 2011), DCs are the only cells that can efficiently prime naïve T lymphocytes — a capacity that sets them apart from macrophages, B cells, and other APCs that activate only pre-sensitized lymphocytes.

DCs reside in virtually every tissue in an immature state, continuously sampling the environment by macropinocytosis, phagocytosis, and receptor-mediated endocytosis. Upon encountering a pathogen-associated molecular pattern (PAMP) or danger signal (DAMP), they undergo maturation — upregulating MHC-II and co-stimulatory molecules, producing inflammatory cytokines, and migrating via lymphatics to the draining lymph node to initiate adaptive immunity.

DCs are also the cells that mRNA vaccine platforms must efficiently reach. Spike-encoding mRNA delivered by LNPs is taken up by tissue-resident DCs at the injection site and draining lymph-node DCs; these cells translate spike protein, process it, and prime both CD4+ and CD8+ T cell responses alongside B cell help.

Cross-presentation (cDC1): Exogenous antigen (tumour-shed proteins, viruses that do not infect DCs) is shuttled into the cytosolic MHC-I pathway (phagosomal escape → proteasomal processing → TAP → MHC-I loading) to prime CD8+ CTLs — critical for anti-tumour and anti-viral immunity. Requires Batf3 transcription factor.

Pathology

SARS-CoV-2 impairment of DC function

Plasmacytoid DCs are susceptible to SARS-CoV-2 infection; viral NSP1 blocks mRNA translation of IFN response genes; ORF6 blocks STAT1/STAT2 nuclear import, suppressing IFN-β production. This pDC IFN failure is a critical early event in severe COVID-19. cDC subsets are also depleted in blood of severe COVID-19 patients, impairing antigen presentation and adaptive priming — contributing to the dysregulated immune response.

HIV-1 — DC impairment and viral dissemination

HIV-1 infects plasmacytoid and myeloid DCs via CD4 + CCR5/CXCR4, suppressing type I IFN production and MHC-II-mediated antigen presentation. DCs can transport HIV to lymph nodes and facilitate viral transfer to CD4+ T cells via the "infectious synapse" — contributing to systemic dissemination. Nef-mediated MHC-II downregulation impairs DC antigen presentation capacity.

Dendritic cell deficiency — rare primary immunodeficiency

GATA2 mutations cause a complex syndrome including monocytopenia, DC deficiency (pDC and cDC2 depleted), NK cell deficiency, and susceptibility to disseminated non-tuberculous mycobacteria, viral warts (HPV), and myeloid malignancy. IRF8 mutations cause isolated cDC1 deficiency — susceptibility to mycobacteria and herpesvirus; treated with HSCT.

Tumour-associated DCs — tolerogenic polarization

Tumour microenvironment converts DCs toward a tolerogenic phenotype: VEGF, IL-10, TGF-β, and PGE2 from tumour cells impair DC maturation; tumour-associated DCs express low CD80/86 and high PD-L1, promoting T cell anergy and Treg induction rather than effector priming. cDC1 cross-presentation is critical for anti-tumour CTL responses — tumours that exclude cDC1s are refractory to checkpoint immunotherapy.
