Serotonin (5-HT)

5-hydroxytryptamine — the dual-function monoamine that governs gut motility and CNS mood, synthesized from dietary tryptophan via TPH1/2 and AADC.

~95% lives in enterochromaffin cells of the GI tract; only ~5% crosses the blood-brain barrier. Fourteen-plus receptor subtypes spanning Gi, Gq, and Gs G-proteins plus a ligand-gated ion channel (5-HT3). SERT (SLC6A4) is the primary reuptake transporter and the target of the most-prescribed antidepressants on earth.

Atlas One · Molecular · Monoamine Neurotransmitter / Enteric Hormone

Serotonin (5-HT)

Class: Monoamine  ·  Neurotransmitter  ·  Enteric hormone  ·  Paracrine mediator  |  Gene: TPH1 (peripheral), TPH2 (CNS)  |  UniProt (SERT): Q9Y3Q4

Serotonin (5-hydroxytryptamine, 5-HT) is a monoamine derived from the essential amino acid tryptophan. Despite its fame as a "brain chemical," the vast majority of the body's serotonin resides in the gut, where it regulates motility and acts as a paracrine signal. In the central nervous system, raphe nucleus neurons project widely to modulate mood, appetite, sleep, and cognition. The discovery that selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) elevate synaptic 5-HT and alleviate depression transformed psychiatry — SSRIs became the most-prescribed class of psychiatric drugs worldwide. The 14+ receptor subtypes of 5-HT mediate diverse and sometimes opposing effects across the body.

Rate-limiting step: Tryptophan hydroxylation by TPH1/2. Availability of dietary tryptophan limits 5-HT synthesis, particularly in the CNS where tryptophan competes with other large neutral amino acids for the LAT1 transporter across the blood-brain barrier.

Peripheral vs. central synthesis: TPH1 is expressed in enterochromaffin (EC) cells of the gut mucosa, platelets, and the pineal gland. TPH2 is expressed exclusively in neurons of the raphe nuclei in the brainstem — the sole source of CNS serotonin. These two pools are largely independent: peripherally produced serotonin does not cross the blood-brain barrier.

Receptor Subtypes

The serotonin receptor family is the largest in the CNS. All subtypes except 5-HT3 are GPCRs. 5-HT3 is a ligand-gated cation channel (related to the nAChR superfamily).

SERT Reuptake and SSRI Mechanism

SERT (Serotonin Transporter, SLC6A4)

After synaptic release, the primary mechanism for terminating 5-HT signalling is reuptake via the serotonin transporter SERT (gene SLC6A4). SERT is a Na⁺/Cl⁺-dependent secondary active transporter that co-transports Na⁺ and Cl⁺ down their electrochemical gradient, coupled to uptake of 5-HT against its concentration gradient. Intracellular K⁺ moves outward to reset the cycle.

  1. Release: Action potential at serotonergic terminal → Ca²⁺ influx → vesicle fusion → 5-HT released into synapse
  2. Receptor binding: 5-HT binds post-synaptic and pre-synaptic (autoreceptor) receptors, initiating downstream signalling
  3. Reuptake: SERT on presynaptic membrane transports 5-HT back into the terminal; repackaged into vesicles by VMAT2
  4. Degradation (overflow): Excess 5-HT is metabolized by MAO-A → 5-HIAA, which is renally excreted
  5. SSRI block: Fluoxetine, sertraline, escitalopram bind the central allosteric site of SERT → competitive + non-competitive inhibition → 5-HT accumulates in the synapse → prolonged receptor stimulation

Onset paradox: SSRIs elevate synaptic 5-HT within hours, yet antidepressant effects require 2–6 weeks. This lag is partly explained by desensitization of 5-HT1A autoreceptors (which initially counteract the increased 5-HT by reducing raphe firing) and downstream neuroplastic changes (BDNF upregulation, hippocampal neurogenesis).

Physiological Functions

Peripheral (GI Tract — ~95% of total body 5-HT)

Enterochromaffin cells in the gut mucosa are the main serotonin factory. 5-HT released by EC cells acts on myenteric plexus neurons (via 5-HT4) to initiate the peristaltic reflex: ascending contraction above, descending relaxation below a bolus. It also activates vagal afferents (via 5-HT3) that signal satiety and nausea to the brainstem. Platelets take up 5-HT from the portal circulation via SERT and release it at sites of vascular injury, contributing to vasoconstriction (5-HT2A on smooth muscle).

Central (Raphe Nuclei — ~5%)

The dorsal and median raphe nuclei project diffusely throughout the brain. Major projections include limbic system (mood, anxiety, fear memory), frontal cortex (executive function, impulse control), hypothalamus (appetite, sleep-wake), striatum (reward, motor control), and spinal dorsal horn (descending pain modulation). The wide reach of raphe projections explains why manipulating 5-HT has such diverse behavioral effects.

Other roles

Serotonin also contributes to bone metabolism (gut-derived 5-HT inhibits osteoblast proliferation; CNS-derived 5-HT may promote it — a key finding from LRP5 pathway studies), platelet aggregation, fetal neurodevelopment, and cardiovascular function via 5-HT2A-mediated vasoconstriction.

Migraine Transient 5-HT fluctuations Platelet 5-HT release and 5-HT1B/1D-mediated changes in cranial vessel tone and trigeminal activation Unilateral throbbing headache, nausea, photophobia. Triptans (5-HT1B/1D agonists) abort acute attacks

Serotonin Syndrome vs. Neuroleptic Malignant Syndrome (NMS): Both cause hyperthermia and altered mental status. Key differentiator: clonus and hyperreflexia are hallmarks of serotonin syndrome (excess serotonin), whereas NMS features lead-pipe rigidity and bradyreflexia (dopamine blockade). Onset: serotonin syndrome is rapid (hours); NMS develops over days. This distinction is clinically critical because treatment differs fundamentally — cyproheptadine (5-HT2A antagonist) for serotonin syndrome vs. bromocriptine/dantrolene for NMS.
