Photon

The quantum of electromagnetic radiation — massless, travelling at c, carrying energy E = hν. The particle that allows humans to see, synthesise vitamin D, entrain circadian rhythms, and that simultaneously damages DNA and drives carcinogenesis when its energy exceeds ~3.1 eV (UV).

Photons interact with matter in discrete quanta. In biology their energy ranges from harmless infrared (thermal) through visible (vision, circadian) to UVB (vitamin D, DNA damage) and UVC/ionising (carcinogenic, therapeutic). Each biological photon interaction — absorbing a quantum to excite an electron — illustrates that biology is fundamentally grounded in quantum physics.

Photon

Symbol: γ  ·  Charge: 0  ·  Mass: 0  ·  Spin: 1 (boson)  |  Biologically relevant as: phototransduction trigger, vitamin D precursor activator, circadian zeitgeber, UV mutagen, therapeutic radiation

The photon is the force carrier of electromagnetism and the quantum of light. Its energy E = hν (Planck constant × frequency) determines its biological effects: visible photons (∼1.8–3.1 eV) excite the conjugated pi-electron system of retinal, initiating vision and entraining circadian biology via melanopsin-containing intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells (ipRGCs). UVB photons (3.1–4.4 eV, 290–315 nm) are absorbed by 7-dehydrocholesterol in skin, cleaving the B-ring to begin vitamin D₃ synthesis. UV photons above ~3.3 eV cause direct DNA damage through formation of cyclobutane pyrimidine dimers (CPDs) and 6-4 photoproducts (6-4PPs) — the molecular initiating events of UV-induced carcinogenesis. At longer wavelengths, red and near-infrared photons stimulate mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase (photobiomodulation), with emerging clinical applications.

Structure — E = hν and the Biologically Relevant Spectrum

The photon carries energy quantised by E = hν = hc/λ, where h is Planck's constant (6.626 × 10⁻³4 J·s), ν is frequency, c is the speed of light, and λ is wavelength. A photon has no rest mass and travels at c. Its interaction with matter is entirely through absorption (electron excitation), emission, or scattering.

Biological Roles — Vision and Retinal Photoisomerisation

Vision is the most evolutionarily ancient and precisely characterised biological photon detector. In rod photoreceptors, the chromophore 11-cis-retinal is covalently bound (Schiff base) to lysine-296 of opsin (rhodopsin). Absorption of a single visible photon causes ultrafast (200 femtosecond) isomerisation of the C11=C12 double bond from 11-cis to all-trans-retinal.

Cone Photoreceptors and Colour Vision

Three cone opsin types (L-cone ~560 nm, M-cone ~530 nm, S-cone ~420 nm) share the same phototransduction cascade but differ in the protein environment around the retinal Schiff base, shifting the absorption spectrum via electrostatic interactions that alter the energy gap of the retinal chromophore. Colour vision emerges from comparison of cone outputs — a biological spectrophotometer built on quantum optics.

Biological Roles — Circadian Rhythm and Melanopsin/ipRGC

The master circadian clock resides in the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN) of the hypothalamus. It is entrained to the external light-dark cycle by a non-image-forming photoreceptive system: intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells (ipRGCs) expressing the photopigment melanopsin (OPN4), with peak sensitivity ~480 nm (blue light).

The melanopsin/ipRGC-to-SCN pathway (the retinohypothalamic tract) signals dawn/dusk — phase-shifting the circadian pacemaker by modulating CLOCK/BMAL1 transcription-translation feedback loops. ipRGCs also project to the olivary pretectal nucleus (pupillary light reflex) and the intergeniculate leaflet. Melanopsin is a Gq-coupled opsin activating a phospholipase C pathway rather than the Gt/cGMP cascade of rods/cones, making ipRGCs intrinsically light-sensitive even without rod/cone input.

Clinical implications of circadian photon deprivation: Blue-light blocking by modern indoor lighting, screen use after dark, and shift work suppress melanopsin-driven SCN entrainment. This disrupts cortisol and melatonin rhythms, impairs sleep quality, and is epidemiologically associated with increased risk of metabolic syndrome, breast cancer, cardiovascular disease, and mood disorders. Evening blue-light exposure (<500 nm) as little as 1 hour before sleep suppresses melatonin onset by 90 minutes in controlled studies.

Skin Vitamin D Synthesis

UVB photons (290–315 nm) penetrating the epidermis are absorbed by 7-dehydrocholesterol (7-DHC, provitamin D₃) in keratinocytes and dermal fibroblasts. The photon energy cleaves the B-ring of the sterol via a 6-electron ring-opening electrocyclic reaction to produce pre-vitamin D₃, which thermally isomerises (non-photochemically) to vitamin D₃ (cholecalciferol). Vitamin D₃ undergoes 25-hydroxylation in the liver (CYP2R1/CYP27A1) and then 1alpha-hydroxylation in the kidney (CYP27B1) to produce the active hormone 1,25-(OH)₂D₃ (calcitriol), which acts on VDR (vitamin D receptor, a nuclear receptor) to regulate >1000 genes including calcium absorption (TRPV6, calbindin), immune modulation, and cell differentiation. Latitudes above ~35°N have insufficient UVB in winter months to sustain synthesis.

Biological Roles — Photobiomodulation

Photobiomodulation (PBM) refers to the therapeutic application of red and near-infrared light (630–1100 nm) at low irradiances (1–1000 mW/cm²) to modulate cellular function. The primary cellular photoacceptor is cytochrome c oxidase (Complex IV) of the mitochondrial ETC, whose copper centres (Cu₂ and Cu₂) and haem groups (Fe-haemₐ and Fe-haemₐₐₐ) have absorption bands in the red/NIR window. Photon absorption increases Complex IV activity, elevating the proton motive force and ATP synthesis.

Downstream effects in pre-clinical and early clinical studies include: enhanced wound healing, neuroprotection (Parkinson's, TBI models), reduction of inflammation, and improvement of mitochondrial function in aged tissue. The field remains mechanistically contested and many clinical trials lack rigorous blinding, but the photoacceptor identity (Complex IV) is well established. Nitric oxide (NO) displacement from Complex IV by photons may also contribute to vasodilation effects.

Photodermatoses

A spectrum of disorders arises from abnormal photosensitivity. Xeroderma pigmentosum (XP) is the prototypical NER-deficiency disorder: mutations in XPA–XPG or POLH genes cause accumulation of CPDs and 6-4PPs, resulting in 10,000-fold elevated skin cancer risk and neurological degeneration. Polymorphic light eruption (PLE) is the most common photodermatosis (∼15% of European populations), characterised by delayed eczematous/papular rash after first sun exposure of spring; mechanism involves UVA-induced delayed-type hypersensitivity with neutrophil infiltration. Porphyrias (e.g., erythropoietic protoporphyria) involve accumulation of porphyrins that are potent photosensitisers: porphyrin excited states generate singlet oxygen (¹O₂), a non-radical ROS, causing cutaneous phototoxicity.
