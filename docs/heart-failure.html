Heart Failure

A clinical syndrome in which the heart cannot pump sufficient blood to meet the body’s metabolic needs, or can only do so at abnormally elevated filling pressures. Globally ∼64 million people are affected — the leading cause of hospitalization in adults over 65.

Prevalence: 64 million worldwide 5-year mortality: ∼50% ICD-10: I50 Status: Draft · reviewed 2026-06-05

Overview

Heart failure (HF) is a clinical syndrome, not a single disease. It results from any structural or functional cardiac abnormality that impairs the ability of the ventricle to fill or eject blood adequately. The common final pathway is an imbalance between cardiac output and peripheral demand, compounded by progressive neurohormonal activation that initially compensates but ultimately accelerates cardiac deterioration.

The leading causes of HF in developed countries are ischemic heart disease (∼50% of cases), systemic hypertension, valvular disease (mitral regurgitation, aortic stenosis), and dilated cardiomyopathy. Less common but important etiologies include tachycardia-induced cardiomyopathy, myocarditis (viral, immune-checkpoint inhibitor), arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy (ARVC), hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM), cardiac amyloidosis (AL and ATTR), alcohol-related cardiomyopathy, cardiotoxic chemotherapy (doxorubicin, trastuzumab), and peripartum cardiomyopathy.

Classification by ejection fraction is central to modern management: left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) determines both prognosis and which pharmacological therapies carry randomized-trial evidence. The three phenotypes — HFrEF, HFmrEF, HFpEF — differ in epidemiology, pathophysiology, and response to treatment.

The NYHA functional classification (Class I–IV, based on symptom burden) stratifies severity and guides device decisions. Staging systems (ACC/AHA Stages A–D) additionally recognize pre-HF states (structural disease without symptoms) and advanced/refractory HF requiring mechanical support or transplantation.

HFpEF now accounts for >50% of HF presentations in most high-income countries, driven by aging and the obesity-hypertension-diabetes epidemic. Its under-treatment relative to HFrEF is an active area of trial research.

Pathophysiology

Axis 1 — Neurohormonal Activation

Reduced cardiac output is sensed by arterial baroreceptors, triggering two major adaptive—then maladaptive—cascades:

Sympathetic nervous system (SNS): Norepinephrine (NE) release acts on cardiac β1-adrenergic receptors (β1-AR) → acutely increases heart rate and contractility. Chronically, sustained β1-AR stimulation causes: downregulation and uncoupling of β1-ARs (reducing their density by 50–70% in end-stage HF); hyperphosphorylation of ryanodine receptor 2 (RyR2) via CaMKII and PKA → diastolic SR Ca2+ leak; pathological hypertrophy through CaMKII-HDAC4-MEF2 transcriptional axes; and elevated circulating NE, which independently predicts mortality (CONSENSUS substudy).

Renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (RAAS): Reduced renal perfusion → JG cells release renin → angiotensinogen → angiotensin I → ACE → angiotensin II (Ang II). Ang II acting at AT1R mediates: efferent arteriolar vasoconstriction; Na+ reabsorption; aldosterone secretion from the adrenal cortex (Na+ retention, K+ and Mg2+ loss, direct myocardial fibrosis via MR activation); and pro-fibrotic TGF-β signaling in the heart and kidneys. ADH (vasopressin) release from the posterior pituitary further promotes free water retention → hyponatremia and congestion.

Together these mechanisms produce the clinical hallmarks: fluid retention (elevated filling pressures → dyspnea, oedema), peripheral vasoconstriction (↑afterload → further impairs stroke volume), and structural remodeling (dilation, fibrosis, adverse geometry).

Axis 2 — Cardiomyocyte Molecular Changes

At the cellular level, failing cardiomyocytes exhibit a stereotyped but progressive pattern of dysfunction centered on disrupted Ca2+ handling:

  • ↓SERCA2a expression and activity (30–50% reduction in end-stage HF): the SR Ca2+-ATPase responsible for active Ca2+ re-uptake into the SR after systole. Reduced SERCA2a → slower cytosolic Ca2+ removal → diastolic Ca2+ overload → impaired relaxation and reduced SR Ca2+ load for the next beat. See SERCA2a.
  • ↑NCX1 (sodium-calcium exchanger): Compensatory upregulation attempts to extrude excess cytosolic Ca2+. However, NCX1 operates in reverse during the action potential plateau, introducing net Ca2+ influx and extending the AP duration → arrhythmia risk. See NCX1.
  • RyR2 hyperphosphorylation: CaMKII-dependent phosphorylation at Ser2814 (and PKA at Ser2808) causes RyR2 to adopt a leaky open state during diastole → spontaneous SR Ca2+ sparks → delayed afterdepolarizations (DADs) → ventricular arrhythmia, the substrate for sudden cardiac death. See RyR2.
  • Connexin-43 (Cx43) remodeling: Cx43 gap junctions lateralize away from intercalated discs and are downregulated overall → slowed and anisotropic conduction → widened QRS, increased re-entrant arrhythmia risk.
  • Fetal gene program re-activation: β-MHC (slow, energy-inefficient) replaces α-MHC; ANP and BNP are secreted in large quantities; skeletal α-actin replaces cardiac α-actin. These isoform shifts depress myofibrillar ATPase activity and contractile efficiency.
  • Mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress: Impaired electron transport chain complex activity → ↓ATP availability; excess ROS activates CaMKII → further Ca2+ handling disruption and DNA damage.
Force–frequency inversion: In healthy myocardium, increasing heart rate increases developed force (positive Bowditch staircase) because CaMKII loads the SR with more Ca2+. In failing myocardium this relationship is flattened or inverted: at higher rates, already-leaky RyR2 channels further deplete SR load, and SERCA2a cannot keep pace. This is a mechanistic basis for rate-control strategies in HF.

Diagnosis

Clinical Presentation

FeatureDetails
Symptoms Dyspnoea on exertion (DOE); orthopnoea (dyspnoea when supine — fluid redistribution to lungs); paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnoea (PND); reduced exercise tolerance; ankle oedema; fatigue and muscle weakness; nocturia; abdominal distension (ascites in severe right HF)
Signs Raised JVP (jugular venous pressure); S3 gallop (passive rapid ventricular filling → pathological in adults ≥40 years); S4 gallop (atrial contraction against a stiff ventricle, suggests diastolic dysfunction); bibasal inspiratory crackles; peripheral pitting oedema; hepatomegaly and hepatojugular reflux; pleural effusion (dullness to percussion, reduced air entry); low pulse pressure in severe low-output state; cool peripheries, cyanosis

Biomarkers

BNP and NT-proBNP are secreted by ventricular myocytes (and to a lesser extent atrial myocytes) in direct proportion to wall stress. They are the cornerstone of HF biomarker diagnosis:

  • Rule-out thresholds (ESC 2021): BNP <35 pg/mL or NT-proBNP <125 pg/mL in a non-acute presentation essentially excludes HF as the cause of dyspnoea (negative predictive value >95%).
  • Rule-in: Values at or above these thresholds require echocardiographic confirmation; they are elevated by AF, renal impairment, older age (independently), pulmonary hypertension, and sepsis — so specificity is lower.
  • NT-proBNP in acute HF: ≥300 pg/mL suggests acute decompensation; ≥2000 pg/mL is associated with high in-hospital mortality. Serial NT-proBNP to guide decongestion is supported by the GUIDE-IT and PRIMA trials.

Echocardiography

Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) is the single most important investigation. Key parameters:

  • LVEF by Simpson biplane (modified biplane area-length method) — defines HF phenotype
  • LV volumes (LVEDV, LVESV) — dilation in eccentric remodeling
  • LV geometry (RWT and LV mass index): concentric hypertrophy (pressure overload, HTN), eccentric dilation (volume overload, post-MI)
  • Diastolic function: E/A ratio, E/e′ (≥15 strongly suggests elevated filling pressures), LA volume index (≥34 mL/m²), TR velocity (RVSP estimate)
  • RV function: TAPSE, FAC, S′ — RV involvement worsens prognosis
  • IVC diameter and collapsibility — estimates RAP
  • Valvular disease: MR/AS/AR as cause or consequence

Treatment

Disease-Modifying Pharmacotherapy for HFrEF

Four drug classes have demonstrated robust mortality reduction in HFrEF: RAAS blockers (ACE-I/ARB/ARNi), β-blockers, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (MRAs), and SGLT2 inhibitors. Current ESC 2021 guidelines recommend initiating all four simultaneously or in rapid sequence rather than sequential up-titration over years.

HFpEF Treatment

HFpEF management has historically been symptom-based (diuretics for congestion, rate control, aggressive management of hypertension, AF, and diabetes). Two randomised trials now provide disease-modifying evidence:

  • EMPEROR-Preserved: Empagliflozin in HFpEF (LVEF >40%) → 21% ↓ CV death or HF hospitalization (primary endpoint, driven by HF hospitalization); first positive trial in HFpEF.
  • DELIVER: Dapagliflozin in HFmrEF/HFpEF → 18% ↓ primary composite; consistent across EF subgroups above 40%.
  • TOPCAT: Spironolactone in HFpEF → mixed (primary endpoint neutral; Americas subgroup ↓ HF hospitalization); MRA now Class IIb in HFpEF.
  • PARAGON-HF: Sacubitril/valsartan vs. valsartan in HFpEF → near-miss on primary endpoint; benefit in women and lower EF range (≤57%), now Class IIb in HFmrEF/lower range HFpEF.

Biomarker Monitoring and Comorbidities

NT-proBNP-Guided Therapy

Serial NT-proBNP measurement guides decongestion during acute HF admission (target >30% reduction before discharge) and outpatient optimisation. Persistent elevation despite OMT predicts early re-admission and mortality. The GUIDE-IT trial found NT-proBNP-guided therapy non-inferior to intensive clinical assessment alone in the overall group, but a pre-specified subgroup with NT-proBNP >1000 pg/mL at randomisation derived significant benefit from biomarker guidance.

Iron Deficiency

Functional iron deficiency (ferritin <100 μg/L, or ferritin 100–299 μg/L with TSAT <20%) is present in 40–55% of ambulatory HFrEF patients and in an even higher proportion during acute decompensation. Iron is required for mitochondrial electron transport chain complexes I and IV, myoglobin, and SERCA2a function — deficiency directly impairs cardiomyocyte energetics independent of anaemia.

The AFFIRM-AHF trial (intravenous ferric carboxymaltose vs. placebo, initiated during acute HF hospitalisation) demonstrated a 26% reduction in recurrent HF hospitalization at 52 weeks. IV iron (ferric carboxymaltose or ferric derisomaltose) is now Class I (ESC 2021) in HFrEF patients with iron deficiency to reduce symptom burden and HF hospitalizations.

Renal Function — Cardio-Renal Syndrome

Worsening renal function (WRF: ↑creatinine ≥0.3 mg/dL or ≥25%) occurs in 20–40% of acute HF admissions and predicts poor prognosis. It results from ↓renal perfusion pressure, venous congestion transmitted to renal venous system, and RAAS-mediated efferent constriction. SGLT2 inhibitors cause an initial modest eGFR dip (hemodynamic, reversible) but confer long-term renoprotection (DAPA-CKD, EMPA-KIDNEY).

  • cell mechanismCardiomyocyte — contractile dysfunction, Ca2+ handling failure, fetal gene program, and bioenergetic crisis occur at the single-cell level
  • mediatorAngiotensin II — central RAAS effector driving vasoconstriction, Na+ retention, aldosterone release, and fibrosis; primary target of ACE-I/ARB/ARNi
  • targetSERCA2a — SR Ca2+-ATPase reduced 30–50% in HFrEF; key determinant of diastolic Ca2+ removal and SR Ca2+ load; gene therapy target (CUPID trials)
  • targetRyR2 — hyperphosphorylated by CaMKII in HF; diastolic leak drives DADs and arrhythmia risk; structural target for stabilizing agents (flecainide at low dose, S107)
  • receptorβ1-Adrenergic Receptor — sustained SNS stimulation downregulates and desensitises β1-ARs in HF; β-blocker therapy partially restores receptor density and coupling
  • treatmentACE Inhibitors — RAAS blockade reduces mortality in HFrEF (CONSENSUS, SOLVD); cornerstone of neurohormonal antagonism since 1980s
  • treatmentStatins — indicated in HFrEF with ischemic etiology for secondary prevention of recurrent MI; pleiotropic effects on endothelial function and inflammation; neutral effect on pure non-ischemic HF (CORONA, GISSI-HF)
  • treatmentLoop Diuretics — NKCC2 inhibitors for symptomatic decongestion; essential for all fluid-overloaded HF patients; no mortality benefit, guided by clinical assessment and NT-proBNP
