Aedes aegypti

The world's most dangerous mosquito — primary vector for dengue fever, Zika, chikungunya, and yellow fever, responsible for hundreds of millions of infections annually.

Aedes aegypti (yellow fever mosquito) belongs to the subgenus Stegomyia. A peridomestic, day-biting species with a taste for human blood, it thrives in tropical and subtropical cities wherever artificial containers collect water. Its salivary proteins actively modulate host immunity to facilitate feeding, and its midgut and salivary gland barriers govern which arboviruses successfully complete transmission. Understanding Ae. aegypti biology is inseparable from understanding the global arbovirosis burden.

Aedes aegypti — Yellow Fever Mosquito

Adult Aedes aegypti are ~3–4 mm dipterans, identifiable by the distinctive white lyre-shaped marking on the thoracic mesonotum and banded black-and-white tarsi. Only females blood-feed; blood proteins are obligate for vitellogenin synthesis and egg maturation. The species is restricted to tropical and subtropical latitudes (roughly 20°S–35°N) where temperatures support larval development and adult survival. Its holometabolous lifecycle proceeds from desiccation-resistant eggs through four aquatic larval instars and a pupal stage to winged adult in 10–14 days at 28°C. A single female completes multiple gonotrophic cycles, ovipositing in peridomestic artificial containers — the combination of intimate human-habitat association and competence for four major arboviruses makes it the highest-priority vector in global health.

Feeding & Reproduction

Blood-feeding behaviour

Only females blood-feed. Blood meal proteins, particularly albumin and globulins, are proteolytically processed to amino acids essential for vitellogenin synthesis in the fat body — the yolk protein deposited into maturing oocytes. The gonotrophic cycle from blood meal to oviposition takes approximately 3–5 days at tropical temperatures. Females are highly anthropophilic, preferentially biting humans over other vertebrates; olfactory attraction to human-specific compounds including lactic acid, 1-octen-3-ol, and nonanal guides host-seeking. Feeding preference is crepuscular — peak activity at dawn (07:00–09:00) and dusk (16:00–18:00) — distinguishing Ae. aegypti from night-biting Culex species.

Salivary gland proteins and host immunomodulation

During probing and blood-feeding, female Ae. aegypti inject saliva containing a complex cocktail of bioactive molecules that suppress haemostasis and local immunity: aegyptin binds collagen to inhibit platelet adhesion; apyrase (CD39-like) hydrolyses ADP to prevent platelet aggregation; SAAG-4 is a vasodilator; additional salivary proteins modulate dendritic cell maturation and Th1/Th2 balance at the bite site, creating an immunosuppressive microenvironment. This modulation is functionally relevant to arboviral transmission — saliva co-inoculation with dengue or Zika virus enhances local replication.

Flight range and dispersal

Typical flight range is ~100–200 m from larval source; mark-recapture studies confirm most females stay within 200 m of their emergence site throughout their lifespan. Passive dispersal in traded goods (tires with water, cut flowers, bamboo) drives long-distance geographic spread. Average female lifespan in the field is ~14–21 days (laboratory up to 60 days). Each female may blood-feed 3–8 times over her lifespan, potentially transmitting virus with each subsequent feeding after the extrinsic incubation period.

Vectorial Capacity

Arboviral competence spectrum

Ae. aegypti is the primary or co-primary vector for four major arboviruses: dengue virus (DENV 1–4), Zika virus (ZIKV), chikungunya virus (CHIKV), and yellow fever virus (YFV). Vectorial competence requires the virus to successfully breach two sequential anatomical barriers in the mosquito: the midgut infection barrier (MIB) and the midgut escape barrier (MEB), after which virus disseminates haematogenously to reach the salivary glands. Salivary gland infection barrier (SGIB) and salivary gland escape barrier (SGEB) are the final gatekeepers before virus is present in saliva. Each barrier has a genetic basis in the mosquito and is modulated by temperature.

Extrinsic incubation period (EIP)

The EIP is the interval between a mosquito ingesting an infectious blood meal and the virus appearing in the saliva. For dengue at 28°C, EIP is 8–12 days; at cooler temperatures (20°C) it extends to ~14–21 days, reducing transmission efficiency. ZIKV EIP is similar (~10 days at 28°C); CHIKV EIP is shorter (~3–5 days, reflecting efficient midgut replication). YFV EIP is ~9–12 days. Temperature-dependence of EIP is a key reason arbovirus transmission tracks ambient temperature and underpins climate-change range expansion risk.

Vectorial capacity formula components

Vectorial capacity (C) integrates: vector density relative to hosts (m); daily biting rate (a); probability of surviving EIP (pn, where p = daily survival probability and n = EIP in days); vector competence (b); and intrinsic incubation period (n). For dengue, the high biting rate (female may feed every 2–4 days) and peridomestic habitat concentration of Ae. aegypti around human dwellings produce high m and a values, driving epidemic potential. Basic reproductive number R₀ in naïve populations can exceed 3–8 under optimal conditions.

Disease Burden

Dengue (DENV 1–4)

Approximately 390 million dengue infections per year globally (Bhatt et al., Nature 2013), of which ~96 million manifest apparent disease. Severe dengue (DHF/DSS) accounts for ~500,000 hospitalizations and ~20,000–25,000 deaths annually. Case fatality rate for severe dengue is 1–5% with treatment; ~20% without ICU care. Dengue burden is concentrated in South and Southeast Asia, Latin America, and sub-Saharan Africa.

Zika (ZIKV) and congenital syndrome

The 2015–2016 epidemic in the Americas caused an estimated 500,000–1.5 million infections in Brazil alone. Zika was declared a PHEIC (Public Health Emergency of International Concern) by WHO in February 2016 due to the association with congenital Zika syndrome (microcephaly, corpus callosum agenesis, ventriculomegaly) in offspring of infected pregnant women and with Guillain-Barré syndrome in infected adults. ZIKV entry into neural progenitor cells is mediated by AXL receptor tyrosine kinase — also the mosquito entry receptor.

Chikungunya (CHIKV) and chronic arthralgia

CHIKV (alphavirus) causes acute febrile polyarthralgia that in 30–40% of patients persists as chronic inflammatory arthritis for months to years, causing long-term disability. Estimated 100–200 million cumulative cases since the 2004 Indian Ocean emergence. The CHIKV E1-A226V mutation dramatically increased fitness in Ae. albopictus, but Ae. aegypti remains competent for both genotypes.

Yellow fever (YFV)

Yellow fever remains endemic in tropical Africa and South America; urban cycles involving Ae. aegypti are the most dangerous because of explosive human-to-mosquito-to-human transmission. Estimated 200,000 cases and 30,000 deaths per year globally (WHO). The 17D live-attenuated vaccine is highly effective (>99% immunity for life with one dose) but cold-chain requirements limit coverage in resource-limited settings.

Climate Change and Range Expansion

Northward expansion and temperate establishment

Ae. aegypti is thermophilic; its range is tightly constrained by January isotherm ~10°C and annual precipitation. Climate projections (2050 RCP 4.5) predict range expansion into the US Gulf Coast and southern Europe, and significant southward expansion in Australia. Local transmission of dengue has occurred in Florida (2009–2023) and Texas (2013). In Europe, Ae. aegypti is established in Madeira (Portugal) and parts of the Black Sea coast. By contrast, Culex quinquefasciatus — a temperate-adapted Culex species — is comparatively poor at transmitting DENV/ZIKV/CHIKV but is an important West Nile virus vector; distinguishing which species dominates in a given region is critical for predicting epidemic risk.

Temperature effects on transmission dynamics

Rising mean temperatures accelerate larval development (shortening larval-to-adult time), increase biting frequency (shorter gonotrophic cycle), and shorten the EIP — all increasing the basic reproductive number R₀. A 2°C temperature increase is modelled to expand the window of dengue transmission by 3–5 months in currently marginal areas. However, at temperatures above ~34–36°C, mosquito survival declines, introducing a non-linear upper thermal limit that may constrain transmission in some hyperarid or extremely hot regions.
