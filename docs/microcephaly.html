Microcephaly

A condition defined by head circumference more than 2 standard deviations below the mean for gestational age and sex, reflecting reduced brain volume. Caused by genetic defects in neural progenitor cell proliferation or by acquired insults — infections (Zika, CMV, toxoplasmosis), ischemia, or toxins — that disrupt corticogenesis.

Definition and Classification

Microcephaly is defined clinically as an occipitofrontal circumference (OFC) more than 2 standard deviations below the mean for gestational age and sex using validated growth standards (WHO, Fenton/Intergrowth-21st charts). Severe microcephaly (more commonly used for research and prognostic purposes) is defined as OFC >3 SD below the mean; this threshold is more specifically associated with intellectual disability and seizures.

Two broad etiological categories exist:

  • Primary (congenital) microcephaly: present at birth; typically reflects a developmental defect in brain growth during the period of active neurogenesis (conception → mid-gestation); causes include genetic mutations affecting neural progenitor cell (NPC) proliferation, chromosomal aneuploidy, and first/second trimester infections
  • Secondary (postnatal) microcephaly: head circumference normal at birth but growth falls below the threshold postnatally due to a brain insult (perinatal asphyxia, metabolic disease, postnatal infection, trauma); reflects failure of brain growth rather than failure of initial neurogenesis

OFC measurement must be performed correctly (maximum circumference, non-compressible tape, supine in infants) and plotted on appropriate charts with gestation correction for preterm infants. Serial measurements over time are more informative than a single measurement. Head circumference is a proxy for brain volume; MRI provides direct assessment of cortical architecture.

Normal Cortical Development

Understanding microcephaly requires understanding the cellular and molecular machinery that normally generates the human neocortex — a structure with ~16 billion neurons organized into six cortical layers, all derived from a thin neuroepithelium lining the lateral ventricles.

Neural progenitor cell hierarchy

Corticogenesis begins around gestational week 5–6. Neuroepithelial cells (NECs) in the ventricular zone (VZ) divide symmetrically to expand the progenitor pool, then transform into radial glial cells (RGCs) — the primary progenitors of the cerebral cortex. RGCs have a characteristic bipolar morphology with apical processes touching the ventricular surface and basal fibers extending to the pial surface, serving dual functions as both progenitors and scaffolding for neuronal migration.

RGC divisions generate neurons by two routes:

  1. Direct neurogenesis: RGC divides asymmetrically → one RGC (self-renewal) + one post-mitotic neuron that migrates radially along the RGC fiber to the cortical plate
  2. Indirect neurogenesis (amplifying): RGC → intermediate progenitor cell (IPC) in the subventricular zone (SVZ) → IPC divides symmetrically (once or twice) → 2–4 neurons; greatly amplifies neuron output per RGC division; the SVZ is substantially expanded in primates vs. rodents

In humans and great apes, a third progenitor type — outer radial glia (oRG, basal RGCs) in the outer SVZ (oSVZ) — is particularly abundant. oRG cells lack an apical process but retain the basal fiber; they undergo mitotic somal translocation and generate large numbers of IPCs. The oSVZ expansion in humans is the primary driver of the immense surface area amplification (gyrification) that distinguishes the human brain. Disruption of oRG production or self-renewal reduces cortical surface area disproportionately.

Inside-out cortical layering: Early-born neurons form the deepest layers (L6, L5 — corticospinal and corticothalamic projections); later-born neurons migrate past earlier cohorts to form progressively more superficial layers (L2/3 — callosal and corticocortical projections). Six layers are established by approximately gestational week 24. Key regulators: Reelin (secreted by Cajal-Retzius cells in layer I) — attracts migrating neurons toward the pial surface; LIS1/PAFAH1B1 and DCX (doublecortin) regulate nucleokinesis during migration.

Molecular regulation of NPC self-renewal: CDK5RAP2 (centrosomin homolog) localizes to centrosomes and regulates mitotic spindle orientation — symmetric (planar) divisions expand the NPC pool; asymmetric (oblique) divisions favor neuronal differentiation. ASPM (Abnormal Spindle-like Microcephaly Associated) similarly regulates mitotic spindle pole size and symmetric division. WDR62 anchors CDK5RAP2 at spindle poles. Mutations in all three genes cause primary autosomal recessive microcephaly (MCPH family).

Zika Virus — Molecular Mechanism of Microcephaly

The 2015–2016 Brazilian Zika epidemic established conclusively that congenital ZIKV infection causes microcephaly. ZIKV (Flaviviridae, single-stranded positive-sense RNA) preferentially infects and disrupts neural progenitor cells during cortical neurogenesis.

Cellular entry via AXL receptor

ZIKV enters neural progenitor cells primarily via AXL (a receptor tyrosine kinase of the TAM family — Tyro3, AXL, MerTK) which is highly expressed on radial glia and outer radial glia in the human fetal brain. AXL normally binds GAS6 (growth arrest-specific 6) and Protein S — ligands that bridge phosphatidylserine on apoptotic cell membranes to AXL, mediating efferocytosis. ZIKV exploits this phosphatidylserine-mimicry pathway to enter cells (apoptotic mimicry). AXL is far more highly expressed in the human oSVZ (on oRG cells) than in rodent cortex — which partly explains why mouse models recapitulate microcephaly poorly while human cerebral organoids faithfully reproduce the pathology.

Intracellular mechanisms

Once inside NPCs, ZIKV triggers multiple converging pathogenic mechanisms:

  • TLR3 activation: ZIKV dsRNA replication intermediates activate Toll-like receptor 3 (TLR3) on NPCs → downstream TRIF/IRF3 signaling → dysregulation of genes controlling neurogenesis and axon guidance (Courchet et al. transcriptome analysis: ~15% of neurogenesis genes disrupted in ZIKV-infected organoids)
  • NPC apoptosis: Caspase-3 activation in ZIKV-infected NPCs → premature apoptosis → reduced progenitor pool; early-stage organoids show marked reduction in ventricular zone thickness
  • Premature differentiation: Infected RGCs and oRGCs show reduced self-renewal — prematurely exiting the cell cycle and differentiating into neurons → depletion of the progenitor pool before sufficient neurons have been generated; analogous to genetically caused MCPH
  • Centrosome abnormalities: ZIKV NS2A and NS4A proteins localize to the centrosome and Golgi apparatus → centrosome duplication failure → mitotic spindle defects → increased oblique/asymmetric divisions → fewer symmetric divisions → NPC pool depletion; ZIKV NS2A disrupts adherens junctions at the ventricular surface → delamination of NPCs from the neuroepithelium
  • Cell cycle arrest: ZIKV activates ATM/Chk1 DNA damage response pathway → G2/M arrest → reduced mitotic rate independent of apoptosis

Congenital Zika syndrome

The full clinical spectrum of congenital ZIKV infection includes:

  • Microcephaly (often severe, >3 SD; brain:body size discordance — disproportionate microcephaly)
  • Cortical calcifications (periventricular, cortical, and subcortical — especially at gray-white junction): necrotic foci from NPC destruction; visible on CT without contrast
  • Abnormal cortical gyration: lissencephaly, pachygyria, or simplified gyral pattern from insufficient neuron production
  • Ophthalmologic manifestations: macular atrophy, chorioretinal atrophy, optic nerve hypoplasia, and pale optic disc — reflecting retinal RGC/RPE involvement
  • Clubfoot (talipes equinovarus): from cortical motor neuron loss and consequent reduced fetal movement; also arthrogryposis
  • Ventriculomegaly and corpus callosum abnormalities

Severity correlates strongly with gestational age at maternal infection: infection in the first trimester (when neurogenesis and progenitor expansion are most active) produces the most severe microcephaly; second-trimester infection still causes significant injury but the cortical progenitor pool is partially established.

Clinical Evaluation

A systematic approach is required to determine etiology, which guides prognosis, recurrence risk counseling, and management:

  1. OFC measurement and serial plotting: tape measure technique; corrected gestational age for preterm; plot on sex-specific and gestation-specific growth charts; assess head circumference trajectory over time
  2. Brain MRI: preferred over CT (avoids radiation, superior soft tissue contrast); evaluates cortical malformations (lissencephaly, pachygyria, polymicrogyria, heterotopia), white matter volume, myelination pattern, corpus callosum morphology, cerebellar vermis; calcifications may be less visible on MRI (CT better for calcifications)
  3. Infectious workup: congenital CMV (urine PCR within 3 weeks of birth — after 3 weeks cannot distinguish congenital from postnatal acquisition); toxoplasma serology (maternal + infant IgG/IgM); rubella IgM; ZIKV PCR/serology if exposure history; syphilis RPR on mother and infant; HSV PCR from surface swabs/CSF if encephalitis suspected
  4. Genetic evaluation: chromosomal microarray (detects copy number variants, aneuploidies); targeted gene panel (ASPM, CDK5RAP2, WDR62, CENPJ, and other MCPH genes) or whole-exome sequencing; metabolic screen (organic acids, amino acids, lysosomal enzymes) if metabolic etiology suspected
  5. Ophthalmologic examination: CMV retinitis, ZIKV chorioretinal atrophy, toxoplasma scars, rubella cataracts, optic atrophy; early referral mandatory
  6. Audiology: SNHL in CMV, rubella, HSV, syphilis; newborn hearing screen (automated auditory brainstem response); diagnostic ABR for infants who fail screen
  7. EEG: if seizures suspected or confirmed; seizures occur in ~50–60% of infants with symptomatic congenital CMV and ZIKV-associated microcephaly
  8. Multidisciplinary therapy: physical therapy (hypotonia, motor delay), occupational therapy (feeding, fine motor), speech-language therapy (communication); antiepileptic drugs for seizures; early intervention programs; hearing aids / cochlear implants for SNHL (CMV)

For congenital CMV with CNS involvement or hearing loss, oral valganciclovir (16 mg/kg twice daily for 6 months) has been shown to improve hearing outcomes at 24 months and neurodevelopmental scores (Kimberlin et al., NEJM 2015).
