Cardiovascular system

The body's transport network: heart, blood vessels, and blood integrated into two circuits in series — pulmonary and systemic. Delivers oxygen, removes CO₂, distributes nutrients, hormones, and immune cells, and regulates heat. Cardiac output scales from ~5 L/min at rest to ~25 L/min at peak exercise — a five-fold dynamic range set largely by heart rate and stroke volume.

Overview

The cardiovascular system is the only system most cells of the body interact with directly. Every cell lives within ~100 µm of a capillary — beyond that distance, diffusion alone cannot sustain aerobic metabolism. The system has three coordinated components: a pump (the heart), a distribution network (arteries → arterioles → capillaries → venules → veins), and a carrier fluid (blood) — each with its own pathologies and therapeutic targets, but functioning only as a coordinated whole.

The Two Circuits

The cardiovascular system is two circuits in series, sharing the heart as a common pump:

              ┌─────────────────┐
              │  Pulmonary      │   low pressure
              │  circulation    │   (RV peak ~25 mmHg)
              │   (lungs)       │
              └─────────────────┘
                 ↑           ↓
           ┌────────────────────────┐
           │   Heart (4 chambers)   │
           │   R-side  ←→  L-side   │
           └────────────────────────┘
                 ↑           ↓
         ┌──────────────────────────┐
         │     Systemic             │   high pressure
         │     circulation          │   (LV peak ~120 mmHg)
         │  (brain, muscle, gut,    │
         │   kidneys, skin, …)      │
         └──────────────────────────┘

Pulmonary circuit: Right ventricle → pulmonary trunk → pulmonary arteries → pulmonary capillaries (gas exchange) → pulmonary veins → left atrium. Low pressure, high compliance.

Systemic circuit: Left ventricle → aorta → arteries → arterioles → capillaries → venules → veins → vena cavae → right atrium. High pressure, multiple parallel beds (cerebral, coronary, renal, splanchnic, muscular, cutaneous) each with local autoregulation.

Function — Cardiac Output

Cardiac output (CO) is the system's primary throughput metric — the volume of blood the heart pumps per minute:

CO = HR × SV
where HR = heart rate (bpm), SV = stroke volume (mL/beat)
VariableRestingPeak exercise
Heart rate (HR)60–80 bpm180–200 bpm
Stroke volume (SV)~70 mL~120–150 mL
Cardiac output~5 L/min~25 L/min

This five-fold dynamic range explains why aerobic exercise capacity is set largely by cardiovascular function — not by muscle metabolism alone.

Pressure, Flow, and Resistance

Across any vascular bed, the governing relationship is an analogue of Ohm's law:

ΔP = Q × R
ΔP = pressure drop, Q = flow, R = resistance

Resistance scales with Poiseuille's law — inversely with the fourth power of vessel radius:

R ∝ 8ηL / πr⁴
η = viscosity, L = length, r = radius. Halving radius → 16× resistance

This radius4 dependence is why arterioles dominate systemic resistance — small changes in arteriolar tone produce large changes in flow distribution. Mean arterial pressure:

MAP ≈ CO × SVR (resting MAP ~93 mmHg)
SVR = systemic vascular resistance; also approximated as DBP + ⅓(SBP − DBP)

Local Autoregulation

Each major vascular bed holds flow constant across a range of perfusion pressures. Cerebral circulation autoregulates from MAP ~60–150 mmHg. Coronary, renal, and splanchnic beds have their own ranges. Mechanisms:

Myogenic response

Smooth muscle contracts when stretched by increased intraluminal pressure, limiting flow increase.

Metabolic vasodilation

Adenosine, CO₂, K⁺, and lactate — local metabolic products — dilate arterioles to match flow to demand.

Endothelial signaling

NO (vasodilatory), endothelin-1 (vasoconstrictive), prostacyclin — the endothelium actively tunes vascular tone.

Coronary autoregulation

The heart extracts ~75% of delivered O₂ at rest (vs ~25% elsewhere). Further demand is met almost entirely by increased coronary flow — making coronary stenosis acutely dangerous.

System-Level Pathology

Hypertension

Sustained arterial pressure elevation; multifactorial (genetic, sodium, RAAS, sympathetic tone, vascular stiffness). The single largest modifiable risk factor for cardiovascular and cerebrovascular disease globally.

Atherosclerosis

Lipid deposition + inflammation in the intima of large and medium arteries; progressive plaque growth; rupture or erosion → thrombosis → tissue infarction. The substrate of MI, ischemic stroke, and peripheral arterial disease.

Shock states

Failure of systemic perfusion. Subtypes: hypovolemic (volume loss), cardiogenic (pump failure), distributive (sepsis, anaphylaxis — vasodilation + capillary leak), obstructive (tamponade, PE, tension pneumothorax).

Pulmonary hypertension

Sustained elevated pulmonary artery pressure → progressive right-heart failure. Multiple etiologies: idiopathic, drug-induced, left-heart disease, hypoxic, thromboembolic.

Thromboembolic disease

Deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, AF-related cardioembolic stroke. Target of anticoagulant and antiplatelet therapy.

Cardiac arrest

Cessation of effective circulation — typically ventricular fibrillation or asystole. Survival falls ~10% per minute without defibrillation.
