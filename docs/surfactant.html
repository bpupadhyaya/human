Pulmonary Surfactant

A phospholipid-protein complex secreted by type II alveolar pneumocytes that reduces alveolar surface tension from ~70 mN/m to below 5 mN/m at end-expiration — preventing alveolar collapse, stabilizing lung volumes, and providing innate immune defense at the air-liquid interface.

Composition: ~90% lipids, ~10% proteins Scale: L02 Molecular / Biochemistry System: Respiratory Type: Bioactive complex Reviewed: 2026-06-05

Overview

Pulmonary surfactant is a complex of phospholipids and specific proteins that lines the interior of the ~300–500 million alveoli of the human lung. First characterized by Pattle (1955) and Clements (1957), it was identified as the factor responsible for the extraordinary low surface tension in lung extracts — a property that cannot be achieved by any simple lipid film alone and requires the precise combination of saturated and unsaturated phospholipids with the four surfactant proteins (SP-A, SP-B, SP-C, SP-D).

The primary biophysical function of surfactant is to oppose the LaPlace pressure that would otherwise collapse small alveoli during expiration. Without surfactant, the alveolar surface would behave as a simple air-water interface with surface tension ~70 mN/m, generating a collapsing pressure of ~28 cmH₂O in a 100 µm alveolus — far exceeding the available transpulmonary pressure (~5–10 cmH₂O). Surfactant reduces this tension to <5 mN/m, making normal tidal breathing physically possible.

Clinical significance: Surfactant deficiency causes neonatal respiratory distress syndrome (RDS) in premature infants — the leading cause of morbidity and mortality in preterm neonates before the introduction of exogenous surfactant therapy (first successful use by Fujiwara 1980; FDA approval 1990). Antenatal betamethasone to accelerate fetal surfactant maturation (Liggins and Howie 1972 trial) remains one of the most cost-effective interventions in perinatal medicine.

Surfactant Proteins

The four surfactant proteins (SP-A, SP-B, SP-C, SP-D) account for ~10% of surfactant dry weight but are essential for both the biophysical function and the immune activity of the complex. They fall into two functional classes: the hydrophilic collectins (SP-A, SP-D — innate immune functions) and the hydrophobic proteins (SP-B, SP-C — biophysical functions).

Biophysics: LaPlace Law and Surface Film Dynamics

The physical basis for surfactant's necessity is captured by the LaPlace Law for a spherical bubble:

P = 2T / r

where P is the pressure inside the alveolus (collapsing pressure), T is surface tension (N/m), and r is the alveolar radius (~100 µm at FRC). Without surfactant (T ≈ 70 mN/m), small alveoli experience higher collapsing pressures than large ones — a physically unstable situation in which small alveoli empty into large ones (Laplace instability). This would cause massive atelectasis and require pathologically high inspiratory pressures to reopen collapsed units.

Surfactant resolves this in two ways:

  • Surface tension reduction: Surfactant reduces T from ~70 mN/m to ~25–30 mN/m at full lung volume (inspiration) and to <5 mN/m at low lung volume (end-expiration), when the DPPC monolayer is compressed to maximum density. This near-zero surface tension at end-expiration eliminates the Laplace instability.
  • Area-dependent surface tension (hysteresis): During inspiration, the surface area increases → the film is diluted → T rises slightly → larger negative intrapleural pressure is needed to expand the lung. During expiration, area decreases → film compresses → T falls dramatically → alveoli resist collapse. This hysteresis loop is a stabilizing, energy-conserving feature that reduces work of breathing.

Tubular myelin is a highly organized, lattice-like lipid structure formed in the alveolar subphase from secreted lamellar body contents by the combined action of SP-A, SP-B, and calcium. It serves as the immediate reservoir for rapid lipid adsorption to the surface monolayer during inspiration. SP-A forms the corner junctions of the tubular myelin lattice (~25 nm square unit cell); SP-B is required for lamellar body-to-tubular myelin conversion.

"Squeeze-out" of unsaturated lipids: At end-expiration, as the alveolar surface area contracts to minimum, unsaturated phospholipids (which pack less efficiently due to kinked acyl chains) are selectively excluded ("squeezed out") from the compressed monolayer, enriching it in DPPC — the optimal surface-tension-lowering lipid. This dynamic lipid sorting is the physical mechanism by which surface tension approaches zero during exhalation.

Production, Secretion, and Recycling

Synthesis in Type II Pneumocytes

Pulmonary surfactant is synthesized and stored exclusively in type II alveolar pneumocytes (AT2 cells). The lipid synthesis pathway proceeds through the endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi complex. DPPC synthesis requires de novo fatty acid synthesis (glucose → acetyl-CoA → palmitate → CoA-activated → CDP-choline pathway → DPPC). SP-B and SP-C are produced as large precursors (proSP-B, proSP-C) that are proteolytically processed in multivesicular bodies (MVBs).

Mature lipids and proteins are packaged into lamellar bodies — lysosome-related organelles with concentrically laminated phospholipid bilayers (~0.1–1 µm diameter) — by the ABCA3 (ATP-binding cassette transporter A3) transporter. ABCA3 actively transports phospholipids (DPPC, POPG) into the lamellar body lumen. ABCA3 mutations → lamellar body dysgenesis → fatal neonatal RDS or childhood ILD.

Regulated Exocytosis

Lamellar bodies fuse with the AT2 apical plasma membrane via SNARE-mediated exocytosis, releasing their contents into the alveolar subphase. Secretion is stimulated by:

  • Mechanical stretch: Lung inflation during inspiration → AT2 cell membrane deformation → Ca²⁺ influx and IP3-mediated Ca²⁺ release → lamellar body exocytosis (the most physiologically important stimulus)
  • Beta-adrenergic agonists: β₂ receptor → cAMP → PKA → enhanced exocytosis; basis for clinical use of beta-agonists to stimulate surfactant secretion in preterm infants
  • SP-A: At low concentrations, SP-A stimulates secretion; at high concentrations (replete pool), SP-A inhibits exocytosis via its receptor on AT2 cells — a homeostatic feedback loop
  • Cholinergic stimulation: Muscarinic M3 receptor → IP3 → Ca²⁺ → secretion
  • ATP (purinergic): P2Y₂ receptor → PLC → IP3 → Ca²⁺ → exocytosis

Surfactant Recycling

Approximately 85–95% of secreted surfactant is recycled by type II pneumocytes rather than degraded, making the lung highly efficient. Recycling pathway: surface film → large aggregate forms → endocytosis by AT2 cells (via clathrin-coated vesicles and macropinocytosis) → endosomes → repackaged into lamellar bodies by ABCA3 → re-secretion. Small aggregates (biophysically inactive vesicular forms) are either re-endocytosed by AT2 cells or cleared by alveolar macrophages (via SR-A and SP-A-mediated uptake). The turnover time of the alveolar surfactant pool is approximately 5–10 hours.

Biosynthesis Regulation

Surfactant production is under transcriptional control by several master regulators:

  • NKX2-1 (TTF-1 / thyroid transcription factor-1): A homeodomain transcription factor essential for lung development and the primary driver of SP-A, SP-B, SP-C, and SP-D gene expression in AT2 cells. NKX2-1 binds TATA proximal elements in SFTPA/B/C/D promoters; loss of NKX2-1 → complete absence of surfactant protein expression. NKX2-1 is also required for AT2 cell identity and lung morphogenesis (NKX2-1 null → failure of trachea-esophageal separation).
  • Glucocorticoids (fetal lung maturation pathway): Cortisol → glucocorticoid receptor (GR) → binds GRE in SFTPB/SFTPC promoters → ↑SP-B/SP-C transcription + ↑fatty acid synthesis enzymes + ↑ABCA3 expression → net increase in lamellar body formation and surfactant secretion. The Liggins-Howie trial (1972) demonstrated that antenatal betamethasone (2 × 12 mg IM, 24 h apart) given between 24–34 weeks reduces neonatal RDS incidence by ~40–50% and is standard of care worldwide.
  • Thyroid hormones: T3 synergizes with glucocorticoids to accelerate surfactant maturation; TRα1 in AT2 cells activates SFTPB/SFTPC genes; hypothyroidism in neonates → delayed surfactant maturation.
  • cAMP / β-adrenergic pathway: cAMP → PKA → CREB phosphorylation → enhanced SFTPB/SFTPC promoter activity; basis for experimental use of beta-agonists and theophylline (cAMP-elevating) to augment surfactant.
  • FoxA2 (HNF-3β): Forkhead box transcription factor required for lipid gene activation in AT2 cells; FoxA2 null → absent surfactant lipid production despite preserved NKX2-1 expression.

Administration Techniques

Traditional surfactant delivery requires intubation and mechanical ventilation (InSurE — intubate, surfactant, extubate technique). Increasingly, LISA (Less Invasive Surfactant Administration) via a thin catheter (MAD Nasal or Oporta catheter) is used in spontaneously breathing neonates on nCPAP without formal intubation. LISA trials (CURPAP, NINSAPP, LION) demonstrate equivalent efficacy with reduced intubation rates, BPD risk, and air-leak complications.

Surfactant in Adult ARDS

Despite the central role of surfactant dysfunction in adult ARDS — alveolar surfactant pool is depleted, SP-A and SP-B are inactivated by inflammatory proteins and oxidized lipids, and surface tension is elevated — exogenous surfactant therapy has failed in multiple large adult ARDS trials. Reasons for failure include: (1) adult ARDS is driven by inflammatory injury rather than primary surfactant deficiency; (2) the large, fluid-filled adult lung is difficult to deliver surfactant uniformly to; (3) inflammatory proteins (fibrin, albumin) inhibit exogenous surfactant. The ARDS Network (Anzueto 1996, n=725) and Spragg 2004 (n=448) calfactant/poractant trials both showed no mortality benefit.
