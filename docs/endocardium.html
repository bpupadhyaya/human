Endocardium

The innermost tissue layer of the heart — a blood-contacting endothelial monolayer lining all four chambers and valve leaflets, continuously producing paracrine mediators (NO, ET-1, neuregulin-1) that modulate subjacent cardiomyocytes. Embryological progenitor of the cardiac valves via endocardial-to-mesenchymal transition (EndMT).

Aliases: cardiac endothelium, endocardial endothelium Scale: 05-Tissue Status: Draft · reviewed 2026-06-03

Overview

The endocardium is the innermost tissue layer of the heart, lining all four chambers (RA, RV, LA, LV), the valve leaflets and their tendinous attachments, and the papillary muscles. It is continuous with the endothelium of the great vessels — a single uninterrupted endothelial lining interfacing with blood throughout the cardiovascular system.

The endocardium is not merely a passive lining. It is an active signaling layer that communicates continuously with the underlying myocardium through paracrine mediators, modulates contraction and relaxation, and serves as the embryological progenitor of the cardiac valves. The proximity of endocardial endothelial cells to subjacent cardiomyocytes (as close as 1–5 µm) enables potent paracrine regulation of myocardial function.

Bidirectional communication: Endocardial NO (negative inotrope, positive lusitrope) and ET-1 (positive inotrope) together tune the contractile state of subjacent cardiomyocytes moment-to-moment. Endocardial injury in inflammatory conditions therefore directly alters myocardial function.

Valve Leaflets as Specialized Endocardium

The four cardiac valves (tricuspid, mitral, pulmonary, aortic) are specialized endocardial structures — folds of endocardium enclosing a trilaminar connective tissue core (spongiosa, fibrosa, ventricularis), populated by valve interstitial cells (VICs, quiescent fibroblast-like) and covered on both surfaces by valve endothelial cells (VECs). VECs have distinct gene expression from endocardial endothelium elsewhere, adapted to the unique shear-stress and pressure environment of valve surfaces.

Function

Thromboresistance

The endocardial endothelium maintains a non-thrombogenic blood-surface interface through continuous production of: prostacyclin (PGI₂) and nitric oxide (NO) — potent platelet aggregation inhibitors and vasodilators; heparan sulfate proteoglycans (activating antithrombin III); and thrombomodulin (converts thrombin to an activator of protein C → inactivates clotting factors Va and VIIIa).

Disruption of the endocardium (by infection, turbulent flow, or trauma) exposes subendocardial matrix and triggers platelet adhesion and fibrin deposition — the basis of infective endocarditis and non-bacterial thrombotic endocarditis.

Embryological Origin of Valves (EndMT)

During cardiac development, specialized endocardium undergoes endocardial-to-mesenchymal transition (EndMT): endothelial cells lose their identity, gain migratory and invasive mesenchymal properties, and populate the endocardial cushions that remodel into the four cardiac valves. This process, driven by TGF-β and NOTCH signaling, is pathologically recapitulated in adult valve disease — particularly calcific aortic stenosis and myxomatous mitral valve disease, where VICs re-activate EndMT-like programs.

Pathology

DiseaseEndocardial mechanism
Infective endocarditis (IE)Bacterial colonization of disrupted valve endocardium. Common organisms: S. viridans (previously damaged valves), S. aureus (structurally normal valves). Vegetation = fibrin-platelet matrix with embedded bacteria. High embolic and metastatic sepsis risk.
Loeffler endocarditisEosinophilic infiltration of endocardium; major basic protein and eosinophil peroxidase damage endothelium and myocardium → restrictive cardiomyopathy. Associated with hypereosinophilic syndrome.
Non-bacterial thrombotic endocarditis (NBTE)Sterile fibrin-platelet vegetations on valve endocardium in cachexia, malignancy, or hypercoagulable states. Major embolic risk without infection.
Endocardial fibroelastosis (EFE)Abnormal collagen and elastin deposition in endocardium — complicates congenital heart disease (aortic stenosis, HLHS) or viral infection in infancy. Produces stiff, thickened inner wall restricting filling.
