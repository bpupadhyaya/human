Ibuprofen (Advil, Nurofen)

Non-selective NSAID — reversibly occupies the arachidonic acid-binding channel of both COX-1 and COX-2, blocking prostaglandin synthesis and delivering the analgesic, antipyretic, and anti-inflammatory triad.

The world's most widely used OTC analgesic — 30 million daily users. Discovered in 1961, Nobel Prize mechanism (Vane 1982). GI and cardiovascular risks define clinical limits. Essential for dysmenorrhea, musculoskeletal pain, fever, and inflammatory arthritis.

Ibuprofen

Class: NSAID / Propionic Acid Derivative  ·  Route: Oral, topical, IV  |  Brands: Advil, Nurofen, Motrin, Brufen  |  OTC + prescription doses available

Ibuprofen is a racemic propionic acid NSAID; the S(+)-enantiomer is pharmacologically active (R(−) partially converted in vivo). It reversibly occupies the hydrophobic cyclooxygenase channel of both COX-1 (PTGS1) and COX-2 (PTGS2), blocking arachidonic acid access to the catalytic Tyr385 residue. This suppresses synthesis of all prostanoids: PGE₂ (pain sensitization, fever), PGI₂ (vasodilation), TXA₂ (platelet aggregation). Unlike aspirin, binding is reversible — platelet function returns within 24–48h of last dose. COX-1 inhibition explains GI toxicity; relative COX-2 inhibition provides anti-inflammatory effect. Short half-life (~2 h) requires q6–8h dosing for sustained effect.

Clinical Risks — The GI and Cardiovascular Trade-Off

  • GI toxicity (COX-1 mechanism): COX-1-derived PGE₂/PGI₂ protect gastric mucosa (stimulate mucus/bicarbonate secretion, maintain mucosal blood flow, reduce acid secretion); ibuprofen depletes these → superficial erosions → peptic ulcer and bleeding risk 3.7× vs non-users; higher risk at doses >1200 mg/day, age >65, prior PUD, NSAIDs + anticoagulants. Gastroprotection with PPI indicated in high-risk patients
  • Cardiovascular risk (COX-2/COX-1 imbalance): COX-2 inhibition reduces endothelial PGI₂ (anti-thrombotic, vasodilatory) while platelet TXA₂ (pro-thrombotic, vasoconstrictor) is less suppressed at typical doses → prothrombotic imbalance → MI/stroke relative risk ~1.3–1.5×; greatest at higher doses (>1200 mg/day) and in patients with pre-existing CVD
  • Renal effects: Prostaglandins (PGE₂, PGI₂) maintain renal perfusion in volume-depleted states by vasodilating afferent arterioles; NSAIDs block this → acute kidney injury in: hypovolemia, diuretic use, ACEi/ARB use, CKD. "Triple whammy" (NSAID + ACEi + diuretic) → high AKI risk. Contraindicated in eGFR <30; caution in eGFR 30–60
  • Aspirin interaction: Ibuprofen taken shortly before aspirin (or first on an empty stomach) can prevent aspirin from accessing its COX-1 binding site; reduces aspirin's cardioprotective antiplatelet effect. Patients on cardioprotective aspirin should take aspirin ≥30 min before ibuprofen, or use paracetamol instead
  • Pregnancy: Avoid after 20 weeks (premature ductal constriction, oligohydramnios via reduced renal PG); contraindicated after 30 weeks. Safe in first trimester; theoretical miscarriage risk with early use is debated
  • NSAID-exacerbated respiratory disease (AERD/Aspirin triad): ~10% of asthmatics; COX-1 inhibition diverts arachidonic acid to lipoxygenase → ↑ cysteinyl leukotrienes (LTC4/D4/E4) → bronchospasm; avoid NSAIDs; safe alternative: paracetamol
