Cortisol

The principal endogenous glucocorticoid — a C21 steroid hormone produced by the adrenal zona fasciculata under hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis control, mediating stress response, metabolic adaptation, and immune suppression.

Plasma cortisol peaks at 6–8 am and troughs near midnight, entrained by the suprachiasmatic nucleus. Acts via the glucocorticoid receptor NR3C1 — a ligand-activated nuclear transcription factor that directly contacts DNA glucocorticoid response elements (GREs). Excess (Cushing's syndrome) or deficiency (Addison's disease / adrenal crisis) are life-threatening endocrine emergencies.

Cortisol

Class: Steroid hormone  ·  Glucocorticoid  ·  HPA axis hormone  |  Produced in: zona fasciculata of adrenal cortex  |  Receptor: NR3C1 (GR)

Cortisol is the body's primary stress hormone and the endpoint of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis — one of the most tightly regulated endocrine feedback loops in physiology. At physiological concentrations, cortisol is essential: it maintains vascular tone, promotes gluconeogenesis during fasting and stress, modulates immune responses, and governs circadian rhythm-coordinated metabolism. At supraphysiological concentrations — whether from endogenous overproduction (Cushing's syndrome) or exogenous glucocorticoid therapy — it drives a recognizable syndrome of central obesity, hypertension, diabetes, osteoporosis, and immunosuppression. Abrupt withdrawal from exogenous corticosteroids can precipitate life-threatening adrenal crisis, making this one of the most consequential drug-endocrine interactions in medicine.

Cortisol physiology in testing: The standard morning serum cortisol (8 am sample) reflects the daily peak. Random cortisol has limited value. The cosyntropin (Synacthen) stimulation test (250 µg IM/IV synthetic ACTH) is the gold-standard test for adrenal insufficiency — a peak cortisol <500 nmol/L at 30–60 minutes is diagnostic. The 1 mg overnight dexamethasone suppression test (ODST) is used to screen for Cushing's syndrome — failure to suppress to <50 nmol/L is abnormal.

Glucocorticoid Receptor (NR3C1) Signalling

Genomic Mechanism (Transactivation / Transrepression)

  1. Cytoplasmic inactive state: GR (NR3C1) held in cytoplasm by chaperone complex (Hsp90, Hsp70, FKBP51, p23). In the absence of ligand, GR is unable to translocate to the nucleus.
  2. Cortisol binding: Cortisol (lipophilic; diffuses freely across cell membranes) binds the ligand-binding domain (LBD) of GR. Conformational change dissociates chaperone complex; FKBP51 is replaced by FKBP52.
  3. Nuclear translocation: GR-cortisol complex translocates to nucleus via importin-α/β machinery. This process takes ~20 min — explaining why glucocorticoid effects have a delayed onset relative to, e.g., catecholamines.
  4. DNA binding (GRE): GR homodimerizes and binds palindromic glucocorticoid response elements (GREs: 5'-GGTACAnnnTGTTCT-3') in promoters of target genes. This is transactivation — drives expression of metabolic genes (PEPCK, G6Pase for gluconeogenesis; tyrosine aminotransferase; lipocortin-1).
  5. Transrepression (anti-inflammatory mechanism): GR monomer physically interacts with NF-κB (RelA/p65) and AP-1 (c-Fos/c-Jun) transcription factors, blocking their DNA binding → ↓IL-1β, ↓IL-6, ↓TNF-α, ↓COX-2 expression. Most anti-inflammatory effects are via this mechanism.
  6. Non-genomic effects (rapid): Membrane-associated GR and cytosolic GR-activated second messenger pathways (PI3K, PKB/Akt) contribute to rapid effects on vascular reactivity and immune cell function within minutes of cortisol exposure.

Glucocorticoid-induced osteoporosis mechanism: GR activation in osteoblasts induces sclerostin and Dickkopf-1 (Wnt inhibitors), reduces OPG (osteoprotegerin), and increases RANKL — net effect is reduced bone formation and increased osteoclast-driven resorption. This is the mechanistic basis for why bisphosphonates are co-prescribed with long-term glucocorticoid therapy.

Steroid-induced adrenal suppression — the tapering problem: Exogenous glucocorticoids (prednisone, dexamethasone) suppress endogenous ACTH and cortisol via HPA feedback within days of supraphysiological dosing. After prolonged therapy (>3 weeks of significant doses), the adrenal cortex atrophies. Abrupt withdrawal risks adrenal crisis. The rule of thumb: anyone on ≥10 mg prednisone/day for >3 weeks should be assumed to have HPA suppression and needs gradual tapering, not abrupt cessation — and needs stress-dose steroids (hydrocortisone 50–100 mg IV) for major surgery or severe illness even during taper.
