Proton

The hydrogen nucleus (H⁺) — the simplest ion in biology yet one of the most consequential. Proton gradients across mitochondrial membranes synthesise virtually all cellular ATP; proton concentration defines pH; proton pumps in the stomach, kidney, and lysosome are critical to digestion, acid-base homeostasis, and nutrient uptake.

Peter Mitchell's 1961 chemiosmotic hypothesis — that a proton gradient across the inner mitochondrial membrane is the direct intermediate between electron transport and ATP synthesis — was one of the most conceptually revolutionary ideas in twentieth-century biology, earning him the 1978 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. The proton is the currency of that revolution.

Proton (H⁺)

Symbol: H⁺ (in solution, actually H₃O⁺ hydronium)  ·  Charge: +1  ·  Mass: 1.007 u  |  Biologically relevant as: chemiosmotic coupling agent, pH determinant, gastric acid, acid-base buffer, proton-coupled transporter substrate

In physics, the proton is a baryon composed of two up quarks and one down quark, carrying a net charge of +1. In biology, the proton manifests as the hydrogen ion (H⁺) — or more precisely, the hydronium ion (H₃O⁺) in aqueous solution — and it is the central currency of cellular bioenergetics. Mitchell's chemiosmotic hypothesis established that the proton electrochemical gradient (proton motive force, ~200 mV) across the inner mitochondrial membrane is the immediate energy source for ATP synthase (Complex V). Beyond energy metabolism, protons define the pH of every biological compartment from the lysosome (pH ~4.5) to the blood (7.35–7.45): enzyme activity, protein folding, drug ionisation, and membrane transport all depend on [H⁺]. Pharmacological proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) targeting the gastric H⁺/K⁺-ATPase are among the most prescribed drugs globally.

Structure and Electrochemical Nature

A proton is the nucleus of a hydrogen atom — a single positive charge with no surrounding electron. In aqueous solution it cannot exist as a bare H⁺; instead it associates with water molecules to form hydronium (H₃O⁺) and extended Eigen and Zundel cation clusters (H₉O₄⁺, H₅O₂⁺). Proton transfer in water is unusually fast (Grotthuss mechanism): H⁺ propagates through a chain of water molecules by sequential proton hopping along hydrogen bonds, achieving an apparent mobility roughly 7× greater than other ions. This speed is critical for rapid pH equilibration in biological buffers and for proton conduction through specialized protein channels (e.g., cytochrome c oxidase, ATP synthase F₀ ring, influenza M2 proton channel).

pH definition: pH = −log₁₀[H⁺]. A change of one pH unit represents a 10-fold change in proton concentration. Human arterial blood is maintained at pH 7.35–7.45 (corresponding to [H⁺] = 35–45 nmol/L) with extraordinary precision by three buffer systems operating at different time scales.

Biological Roles — Mitchell Chemiosmotic Hypothesis and ATP Synthase

Peter Mitchell proposed in 1961 that the free energy released by the mitochondrial ETC is not coupled directly to ATP synthesis by substrate-level phosphorylation but instead stored as a transmembrane electrochemical proton gradient — the proton motive force (PMF, ΔμH⁺). This gradient has two components: an electrical potential (Δψ, inside negative, ~−180 mV) and a pH gradient (ΔpH, inside alkaline, ~0.5 pH units), together constituting a PMF of ~200 mV under physiological conditions.

Mitchell's Nobel lecture (1978): The chemiosmotic hypothesis was initially met with intense scepticism by biochemists who expected a high-energy phosphorylated intermediate to link electron transport and ATP synthesis. Mitchell's elegantly simple proposal — that the membrane itself, by being impermeable to protons, could store free energy in the form of an electrochemical gradient — required a paradigm shift in how biologists thought about energy. It now stands as one of the most important unifying principles in bioenergetics and applies equally to bacterial plasma membranes, chloroplast thylakoids, and mitochondria.

Biological Roles — Gastric Acid and H⁺/K⁺-ATPase

The stomach maintains an intraluminal pH of 1.5–3.5 — an extraordinarily acidic environment that denatures ingested proteins (facilitating pepsin proteolysis), kills most ingested microorganisms, and initiates iron (Fe³⁺ to Fe²⁺ reduction) and vitamin B12 absorption. This acidification is achieved by the gastric H⁺/K⁺-ATPase (proton pump) expressed on the apical membrane of parietal cells in the oxyntic glands of the gastric corpus and fundus.

The H⁺/K⁺-ATPase is a P-type ATPase (like Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase and SERCA) that exchanges intracellular H⁺ for extracellular K⁺ at a 1:1 stoichiometry, using one ATP per exchange. It can concentrate H⁺ up to 10⁶-fold across the apical membrane (from pH ~7.2 in the cytoplasm to pH ~1 in the canaliculus) — the largest ion concentration gradient achieved by any transporter in the human body.

Stimulation of acid secretion is controlled by three convergent pathways on parietal cells: histamine (via H₂ receptor, Gαs → cAMP → PKA), acetylcholine (via M3 receptor, Gαq → PLC → IP3/DAG → Ca²⁺), and gastrin (via CCK2 receptor, Gαq pathway). These pathways converge on fusion of tubulovesicles (containing inactive H⁺/K⁺-ATPase) with the apical canalicular membrane — activating pumps by membrane insertion.

Proton-Coupled Nutrient Transporters

The transmembrane H⁺ gradient (outside acidic in intestinal lumen, brush border) is harnessed by several co-transporters to drive uphill nutrient uptake:

SGLT1 (SLC5A1): Not proton-coupled per se — driven by Na⁺ gradient maintained by basolateral Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase. However, indirectly depends on apical H⁺ gradient via Na⁺/H⁺ exchange. Absorbs glucose and galactose in the small intestine.

DMT1 (Divalent Metal Transporter-1, SLC11A2): A proton-coupled divalent metal transporter; uses the H⁺ gradient at the duodenal brush border (pH ~5.5–6.0) to co-transport Fe²⁺, Zn²⁺, Mn²⁺, and other metals. Iron absorption rate is therefore pH-dependent: gastric acid reduces Fe³⁺ to Fe²⁺ and maintains the acidic microenvironment at the intestinal brush border necessary for DMT1 activity. PPIs raise gastric pH and impair iron absorption over the long term.

PepT1 (SLC15A1): Proton-coupled oligopeptide transporter in the small intestinal brush border; uses H⁺ gradient to absorb di- and tripeptides. The predominant mechanism of dietary protein absorption by mass.

Proton Pump Inhibitors (PPIs)

PPIs (omeprazole, pantoprazole, lansoprazole, esomeprazole, rabeprazole) are prodrugs activated in the acidic environment of the parietal cell canaliculus. The acid converts the pro-drug to a reactive sulfenamide or sulfenamide-like species that irreversibly alkylates the H⁺/K⁺-ATPase at cysteine residues (Cys813 and Cys892), inactivating the pump for 18–24 hours (until new pump protein is synthesised). PPIs are first-line for GERD, peptic ulcer disease, H. pylori eradication (triple therapy), NSAID-gastroprotection, and Zollinger-Ellison syndrome. Concerns with long-term use include: impaired Fe²⁺ and vitamin B12 absorption, increased susceptibility to enteric infections (C. difficile, Salmonella), magnesium deficiency, and potential association with dementia (mechanism unclear; epidemiological evidence contested).

Proton Beam Radiotherapy

At the therapeutic scale, proton beam therapy harnesses the physical properties of accelerated protons (not H⁺ ions, but atomic protons stripped of electrons and accelerated to ~70% of c in a cyclotron or synchrotron) for precision cancer treatment. Protons deposit most of their ionising energy at the Bragg peak — a sharp energy deposition maximum at a defined tissue depth determined by initial kinetic energy — then stop, depositing negligible exit dose. This contrasts with photon (X-ray) radiotherapy, which has exponential dose fall-off and significant exit dose. Proton therapy is particularly advantageous for paediatric tumours, skull-base tumours, and tumours adjacent to critical structures (optic chiasm, brainstem, spinal cord).
