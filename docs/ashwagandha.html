Ashwagandha

Botanical: Withania somnifera (Solanaceae)  |  Route: Oral  |  Category: Adaptogen, rasayana, immunomodulator

Withania somnifera (ashwagandha) is an Ayurvedic rasayana adaptogen with over 3,000 years of documented use. Active withanolides reduce serum cortisol by 14–32% in placebo-controlled RCTs, improve anxiety scores (GAD-7, PSS), and increase testosterone and VO₂max in trials. GABA-A receptor modulation and HPA axis normalisation are the proposed primary mechanisms. Commercial extracts KSM-66, Sensoril, and Shoden are standardised by withanolide content and represent the clinical evidence base.

Overview

Ashwagandha (Withania somnifera, family Solanaceae) is a small woody shrub native to the Indian subcontinent, North Africa, and the Mediterranean. It has been used in Ayurvedic medicine for over 3,000 years as a rasayana — a rejuvenative tonic intended to promote longevity, vitality, and resilience. The Sanskrit name ashwagandha ("smell of horse") refers both to the root's characteristic odour and to the traditional belief that the herb imparts the strength and vitality of a horse. The plant is also used in Unani medicine as asgandh and across North African and Middle Eastern herbal traditions. In contemporary Western markets it is one of the best-selling herbal supplements, marketed primarily for stress, sleep, and athletic performance.

Active constituents are concentrated in the root and to a lesser extent the leaves. The most pharmacologically characterised are the withanolides — a class of steroidal lactones (C₂₈) unique to Withania spp; over 40 have been identified, including withaferin A, withanolide A and D, and withanoside IV and VI. Supporting constituents include alkaloids (isopelletierine, anaferine, cuseohygrine), saponins (sitoindosides VII–X) that contribute to adaptogenic and immunomodulatory effects, and triethylene glycol in leaf extracts, shown to have sleep-promoting effects in animal models.

Three proprietary standardised extract forms dominate the clinical evidence base: KSM-66 (5% withanolides, root only), Sensoril (10% withanolides, root + leaf), and Shoden (35% withanosides + withanolides, the highest-concentration form). Most RCTs have used KSM-66 at 300–600 mg/day. Traditional raw-root powder preparations (churna) have minimal clinical trial data; their withanolide content (~0.1–0.5%) is far lower than standardised extracts, though the effective withanolide dose at traditional serving sizes may be loosely comparable.

Mechanism of Action

HPA Axis Modulation and Cortisol Reduction

  Chronic Stress Signal

  1. Hypothalamic level: Animal studies show reduced CRH expression in the paraventricular nucleus under chronic stress conditions with ashwagandha treatment; the precise receptor mediating this is not confirmed in humans.
  2. Adrenal level: Withanolides reduce cortisol secretion from adrenocortical cells in vitro. Whether this constitutes adrenal suppression or normalisation of excessive output is debated — DHEA-S is maintained or increased in RCTs, suggesting selective modulation.
  3. Net effect in RCTs: Serum cortisol reduced 14–32% from baseline vs. placebo in participants with chronic stress. PSS (Perceived Stress Scale) reduced 44.0% vs. 5.9% placebo at 60 days (Chandrasekhar 2012). CRP also significantly reduced.

Pleiotropic Effects

GABAergic / Anxiolytic

Withanolides bind GABA-A receptors with modest affinity at a site distinct from the benzodiazepine allosteric site (α-subunit). Withaferin A also inhibits GABA transaminase (GABA-T), increasing synaptic GABA availability and reducing neuronal excitability without addiction liability.

NF-κB Suppression

Withaferin A inhibits IKKβ (IκB kinase β), blocking NF-κB nuclear translocation and reducing transcription of TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β, and COX-2. Withanolides also bind Hsp90 and inhibit its ATPase activity, disrupting pro-inflammatory signalling scaffolds.

Dual Immunomodulation

Enhances innate immunity (NK cell cytotoxicity, macrophage phagocytosis, IFN-γ production) while dampening excessive NF-κB-driven inflammation via withaferin A. Profile is consistent with classical rasayana immunomodulation; clinical immunology data are less robust than cortisol data.

Testosterone / Anabolic

Reducing cortisol disinhibits Leydig cell testosterone synthesis (cortisol suppresses LH-driven testosterone production). 600 mg/day KSM-66 raised testosterone +96.2 ng/dL and LH +34% vs. placebo (Wankhede 2015); direct anabolic mechanisms (myostatin inhibition) proposed but not confirmed in humans.

Neuroprotective Mechanisms

Withanoside IV promotes axon and dendrite regeneration in hippocampal neurons and reverses memory deficits in scopolamine-treated mice. This neuroprotective effect is mechanistically distinct from the GABA-A anxiolytic pathway and may underlie the cognitive and sleep benefits observed in trials. Triethylene glycol (leaf extract component) has sleep-promoting effects in mice via unknown mechanisms. Direct human neuroimaging evidence for these neuroprotective pathways is currently absent.

Traditional Ayurvedic dosing: 3–6 g of raw root powder per day (churna) taken with milk, honey, or ghee. Significantly higher gram dose than modern extracts, but withanolide content of raw powder (~0.1–0.5%) is much lower than standardised extracts. Ashwagandha ghrita (root extract in clarified butter) is a traditional lipid formulation that may improve bioavailability, as withanolides are lipophilic.

Testosterone +96.2 ng/dL vs. +18.0 ng/dL (p=0.004). CK recovery improved. Limitation: small n, industry-adjacent; large effect sizes warrant independent replication.

Evidence interpretation: Cortisol and stress reduction are the most consistent and replicated findings across ashwagandha trials. Athletic performance and testosterone data from Wankhede 2015 are promising but effect sizes are large and the trial is small — independent replication is needed before these are considered well-established. Long-term safety beyond 6 months has not been evaluated in formal RCTs. Mechanisms in humans remain largely extrapolated from preclinical studies; no head-to-head comparisons with established anxiolytics exist.

Safety & Interactions

  • Generally well-tolerated at doses ≤600 mg/day. Most common adverse effects: GI upset, drowsiness, loose stools — usually mild and resolve with dose titration.
  • Thyroid interactions: Ashwagandha elevates T3 and T4 in euthyroid subjects in one small trial. Caution in hyperthyroidism or patients on levothyroxine — thyroid function monitoring is advisable.
  • Sedative potentiation: Additive CNS depression with benzodiazepines, barbiturates, alcohol. Clinical significance is unclear at standard doses but caution is warranted when co-prescribed with sedating medications.
  • Immunosuppressant interactions: Immunoenhancing effects create theoretical antagonism. Avoid in transplant patients on calcineurin inhibitors (tacrolimus, cyclosporine) without explicit medical supervision.
  • Pregnancy: Contraindicated — traditionally used as an abortifacient; uterotonic activity confirmed in animal models. Avoid throughout pregnancy and breastfeeding.
  • Autoimmune disease: Immunostimulatory effects could theoretically exacerbate autoimmune flares (rheumatoid arthritis, lupus, multiple sclerosis). Human data are lacking; risk remains theoretical but mechanistically plausible.
  • Hepatotoxicity (rare): 5 published case reports of liver injury 2019–2023, all with additional confounders. FDA has not issued a formal warning but the cases highlight the need for monitoring in high-dose or prolonged use. Risk appears low but not zero.
