Peyer’s Patches

Aggregated lymphoid follicles embedded in the ileal submucosa — the primary sites of mucosal antigen surveillance and intestinal IgA induction. Peyer’s patches continuously sample luminal contents via specialized M cells, initiating adaptive immune responses that govern gut homeostasis, oral tolerance, and defence against enteric pathogens.

Overview

Peyer’s patches (PPs) are the principal organized component of gut-associated lymphoid tissue (GALT), forming dome-shaped aggregates of lymphoid follicles beneath the follicle-associated epithelium (FAE) of the terminal ileum. They are named after the Swiss anatomist Johann Conrad Peyer who described them in 1677. In humans, approximately 30–40 Peyer’s patches develop along the anti-mesenteric wall of the ileum, with the density greatest in the terminal ileum.

Unlike peripheral lymph nodes, Peyer’s patches lack afferent lymphatics — they have no incoming lymphatic vessels. Instead, luminal antigens are delivered directly through the overlying FAE, principally via specialized microfold (M) cells that constitutively transcytose particles, microorganisms, and soluble antigens from the intestinal lumen into the subepithelial dome (SED). This makes Peyer’s patches the primary inductive site for intestinal IgA responses.

The functional output of the Peyer’s patch GC reaction is a continuous stream of IgA+ B cells that upregulate the gut-homing integrin α4β7 and chemokine receptor CCR9, exit via efferent lymphatics, transit through mesenteric lymph nodes (MLN) and the thoracic duct into blood, and home to the small intestinal lamina propria where they terminally differentiate into IgA-secreting plasma cells. These plasma cells produce the bulk of the body’s ~10–15 g/day of secretory IgA.

Anatomy and Microstructure

Zonal organization

Each Peyer’s patch is organized into three distinct anatomical zones that recapitulate — but are more complex than — the B cell follicle/T cell zone organization of peripheral lymph nodes:

  • Follicle-associated epithelium (FAE): a monolayer of enterocytes interspersed with M cells (∼5–10% of FAE cells) that overlies the subepithelial dome. FAE lacks the villus/crypt structure of adjacent absorptive epithelium; enterocytes here have shorter microvilli and reduced mucus production, facilitating direct contact between luminal contents and M cell apical surfaces. The basal surface of the FAE is not separated from the SED by a basement membrane — immune cells can directly contact the epithelium.
  • Subepithelial dome (SED): immediately beneath the FAE, the SED contains a dense population of macrophages, conventional dendritic cells (cDC1 CD103+CX3CR1lo and cDC2), B cells, T cells, and NK cells. Antigens transcytosed by M cells are deposited here and sampled by antigen-presenting cells (APCs). DCs in the SED can extend dendrites through M cell channels directly into the lumen, a more active sampling mechanism paralleling the CX3CR1+ macrophage transepithelial dendrites in the villous epithelium.
  • Germinal centers (GC zone): beneath the SED lie the organized lymphoid follicles containing B cell zones with active germinal centers, surrounded by mantle zones of naive IgM+IgD+ B cells. T cell zones (interfollicular areas) containing CD4+ T cells, including Tfh cells, are located between follicles. Efferent lymphatics collect activated lymphocytes and carry them to draining mesenteric lymph nodes.

Absence of afferent lymphatics

The absence of incoming lymphatics is a key anatomical distinction from peripheral lymph nodes. Peyer’s patches are inductive sites that receive antigen solely from the lumen via M cell transcytosis — they are not downstream recipients of lymph-borne antigen from peripheral tissues. This topology means Peyer’s patches respond specifically to luminal (gut-derived) antigens, making them ideally suited to calibrate mucosal immunity to the constant antigenic challenge from food proteins, commensal microbiota, and pathogens.

M Cell Biology

Morphology and the basolateral pocket

Microfold (M) cells are highly specialized epithelial cells uniquely adapted for transcytosis rather than absorption. Their defining morphological feature is the basolateral pocket — a deep invagination of the basolateral membrane that houses lymphocytes (B cells, T cells) and macrophages in intimate apposition to the transcytotic machinery. This architecture minimizes the distance antigens must travel after transcytosis before encountering immune cells. M cells have sparse, irregular microvilli (hence “microfold”) on the apical surface, a thin glycocalyx (compared to absorptive enterocytes), and a reduced mucus coat, all facilitating direct contact with luminal particles.

Transcytosis mechanism

M cells constitutively transcytose luminal contents without prior activation — they continuously sample the intestinal lumen regardless of the nature of the antigen. The transcytotic route is:

  1. Apical binding: antigen or particle adsorbs to M cell apical surface via specific receptors or non-specifically via charge/hydrophobic interactions
  2. Macropinocytosis / endocytosis: M cells take up material via clathrin-mediated endocytosis, macropinocytosis, and phagocytosis (for large particles including bacteria up to 10 μm)
  3. Vesicular transport: endocytic vesicles bypass the endo-lysosomal degradation pathway (unlike conventional enterocytes) and are transported across the cell intact
  4. Exocytosis into the pocket: vesicles fuse with the basolateral membrane facing the pocket, releasing intact antigens, particles, or even live microorganisms into the subepithelial space where immune cells immediately sample them

M cell differentiation: RANKL and SpiB

M cell differentiation requires two key signals. RANKL (TNFSF11, receptor activator of NF-κB ligand) is secreted by stromal cells (subepithelial myofibroblasts) in the SED and signals through RANK on FAE crypt progenitor cells, driving commitment to the M cell fate. RANKL-deficient mice completely lack M cells and have severely impaired mucosal IgA responses. SpiB, an ETS family transcription factor, is a downstream target of RANK signaling and is the master regulator of M cell differentiation — it directly transactivates GP2 and other M cell identity genes. Notch signaling also cooperates with RANKL/SpiB to specify M cell fate and suppress the goblet cell default.

IgA Induction: From Antigen Sampling to Luminal Secretion

T-dependent IgA pathway

The dominant, high-affinity IgA pathway in Peyer’s patches requires T cell help and is analogous to the germinal center-based IgG response in peripheral lymph nodes, with the key difference that cytokine signals (TGF-β + IL-10) drive IgA class-switch recombination rather than IgG:

  1. Antigen sampling: M cell transcytoses luminal antigen (bacteria, food proteins, viruses) into the SED
  2. APC processing: SED DCs (CD103+ cDC1 and cDC2) capture antigen and process it into peptide–MHC II complexes; CD103+ DCs also produce retinoic acid (RA, derived from vitamin A metabolism via RALDH2/ALDH1a2) which imprints gut-homing receptors (α4β7, CCR9) on T and B cells during activation
  3. T cell priming: activated DCs migrate to the T cell zone of the Peyer’s patch and prime antigen-specific CD4+ T cells; cytokine environment (TGF-β, RA, IL-2) supports Tfh differentiation and Foxp3+ Treg induction
  4. GC reaction with IgA CSR: T-B conjugate interaction in the Peyer’s patch triggers GC formation; Tfh-derived TGF-β + IL-10 (and IL-21) drive germline transcript induction at the Sα switch region → AID-mediated CSR from IgM to IgA; affinity maturation proceeds in the dark/light zone cycle
  5. IgA+ B cell egress: class-switched IgA+ B cells exit via efferent lymphatics, transit MLN and thoracic duct, and enter blood; α4β7 integrin interacts with MAdCAM-1 on intestinal lamina propria venules (HEV-like) to home specifically to the gut
  6. Plasma cell differentiation: IgA+ B cells in the lamina propria differentiate into IgA plasma cells, stimulated by APRIL and BAFF from epithelial cells and stromal cells
  7. pIgR transcytosis to lumen: dimeric IgA (J-chain-linked) binds the polymeric immunoglobulin receptor (pIgR) on the basolateral surface of enterocytes; the complex is transcytosed and cleaved at the apical surface; secretory component (remaining pIgR ectodomain) remains attached, protecting sIgA from proteolysis in the lumen
Scale of IgA production: The gut is by far the largest antibody-secreting organ in the human body, producing 10–15 g of secretory IgA per day — exceeding the daily production of all other immunoglobulin isotypes combined (∼2–3 g IgG). This reflects the continuous antigenic challenge of the intestinal lumen and the unique reliance on IgA for mucosal immune exclusion rather than inflammatory effector mechanisms.

T-independent IgA pathway

A second IgA pathway operates without T cells and occurs in GALT as well as the bone marrow and spleen. In the gut, the cytokines APRIL (TNFSF13) and BAFF (TNFSF13B) secreted by intestinal epithelial cells, goblet cells, and stromal cells can drive IgA class-switch recombination in lamina propria B cells independently of GC reactions. This T-independent IgA is produced faster, is more polyreactive (lower specificity), and is present at lower concentrations than T-dependent IgA. It is particularly important for maintaining homeostatic IgA coating of commensal bacteria in the microbiota, contributing to intestinal ecosystem stability without requiring specific adaptive recognition.

Oral Tolerance

Peyer’s patches, in concert with mesenteric lymph nodes (MLN), are the primary inductive sites for oral tolerance — the systemic unresponsiveness to antigens that are first encountered by ingestion. The failure of oral tolerance underlies food allergy and celiac disease. The mechanism involves:

  • Tolerogenic DCs: CD103+ DCs in Peyer’s patches and MLN produce retinoic acid (RA) and TGF-β; these cytokines, during T cell priming, promote differentiation of antigen-specific naïve CD4+ T cells into Foxp3+ regulatory T cells (iTregs) rather than effector T cells
  • Foxp3+ Tregs: RA-imprinted iTregs upregulate CCR9 and α4β7 and home to the small intestinal lamina propria, where they suppress local immune responses to dietary and microbial antigens via IL-10, TGF-β, and cell contact mechanisms
  • Clonal deletion and anergy: very high doses of oral antigen can also lead to clonal deletion (apoptosis of antigen-specific T cells) or clonal anergy (T cell functional inactivation), contributing a second layer of oral tolerance
  • Systemic tolerance spread: RA-imprinted Tregs from the gut recirculate and can suppress antigen-specific immune responses at peripheral sites, explaining why oral antigen feeding can desensitize systemic immune responses — the basis of oral immunotherapy (OIT) for food allergy

Breakdown of oral tolerance mechanisms is observed in food allergy (IgE-mediated sensitization instead of tolerance), celiac disease (HLA-DQ2/DQ8-restricted Th1/Th17 response to gliadin peptides in genetically susceptible individuals), and inflammatory bowel disease (loss of tolerance to commensal antigens).

Pathogens Exploiting Peyer’s Patches as Entry Routes

The very efficiency of M cell sampling that enables mucosal immune surveillance also makes Peyer’s patches vulnerable to exploitation by enteric pathogens as a “Trojan horse” entry site — transcytosis through M cells delivers live pathogens directly beneath the epithelium to a site rich in macrophages and DCs, enabling systemic dissemination.

Cross-Atlas Connections

  • Produces Secretory IgA — Peyer’s patches are the principal inductive site for gut IgA; GC reactions here generate IgA+ B cells that home to lamina propria and secrete dimeric IgA that transits via pIgR into the gut lumen
  • Samples via Antibody (Immunoglobulin) — IgA generated in Peyer’s patches is the most abundant antibody isotype in the body by mass; sIgA differs structurally from serum IgG through its dimeric form, J-chain, and secretory component
  • GC site Germinal Center — active germinal centers within Peyer’s patches drive TGF-β/IL-10-directed IgA class switching and affinity maturation; Tfh cells here co-produce IL-21 and TGF-β, unlike the IL-4/IL-21 profile in peripheral lymph nodes
  • Microbiome sensing Gut Microbiome — Peyer’s patches continuously sample commensal bacteria, shaping the microbiome through targeted sIgA coating; IgA coats ~70% of gut bacteria in the terminal ileum; alterations in IgA selection correlate with dysbiosis
  • Pathogen entry Pathogens — enteric pathogens (Salmonella, Yersinia, poliovirus, reovirus, prions) exploit M cell transcytosis to breach the gut barrier; Peyer’s patches are simultaneously the immune inductive site and the pathogen Achilles heel of mucosal defence
  • Antigen presentation MHC Class II — CD103+ DCs in the SED and T cell zones process and present luminal antigens on MHC-II, driving both tolerogenic (Treg) and immunogenic (Tfh) T cell responses depending on cytokine context and antigen nature
