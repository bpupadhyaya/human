HPV-16

The dsDNA oncovirus that hijacks the cell cycle by degrading p53 and dismantling Rb — and is almost entirely preventable by a single vaccine series.

HPV-16 (Human Papillomavirus type 16) is a ~7.9 kb circular dsDNA Alphapapillomavirus and the most carcinogenic HPV type, responsible for ~50–60% of all cervical cancers and the dominant driver of HPV-positive oropharyngeal cancer. Its two oncoproteins, E6 (which recruits E6AP ubiquitin ligase to degrade p53) and E7 (which binds and inactivates the Rb tumour suppressor), collaboratively disable cell cycle checkpoints, prevent apoptosis, and activate telomerase — the core molecular programme of HPV-driven carcinogenesis. Gardasil-9 prevents >90% of HPV-attributable cancers.

HPV-16

Non-enveloped icosahedral dsDNA oncovirus with a ~7.9 kb circular genome. L1 capsid pentamers bind heparan sulphate proteoglycans (HSPGs) on basal keratinocytes for initial attachment; L2 minor capsid protein mediates endosomal escape and nuclear genome delivery after slow clathrin- or caveolin-mediated endocytosis. In productive infection the genome is maintained as an episome (50–200 copies/cell). Malignant transformation is triggered by integration into host chromosomes, disrupting the E2 repressor ORF and causing overexpression of E6 and E7 oncoproteins: E6 recruits E6AP ubiquitin ligase to polyubiquitinate and degrade p53, abolishing the G1/S checkpoint, apoptosis induction, DNA damage responses, and immortalising cells via hTERT activation; E7 binds pRb via its LXCXE motif, releasing E2F transcription factors and driving S-phase entry, and also degrades pRb, p21, and p27. Together E6/E7 drive multistep carcinogenesis through CIN1→CIN3→invasive cancer. Gardasil-9 L1 VLP vaccine prevents infection with >95% efficacy against covered types in seronegative recipients.

Infection Mechanism

1 · Basal keratinocyte entry via HSPG and slow endocytosis

HPV-16 exclusively infects stratified squamous epithelium, requiring access to the basal keratinocyte layer (the proliferating stem cell compartment) through microtraumas or wounds. The cervical transformation zone — where columnar and squamous epithelia meet — is particularly vulnerable. L1 pentamers bind heparan sulphate proteoglycans (HSPGs) on basal keratinocytes and extracellular matrix exposed at wound sites. A conformational change in L1 reveals the L2 N-terminus for secondary receptor engagement (likely α6 integrin and growth factor receptors). Clathrin- or caveolin-mediated endocytosis follows over 12–24 hours. L2 inserts into the acidifying endosomal membrane, redirecting the viral genome via the trans-Golgi network to the nucleus, where it establishes as a circular episomal minichromosome.

2 · E6 oncoprotein — p53 degradation and immortalisation

E6 (158 aa) drives cell immortalisation by eliminating the p53 tumour suppressor. E6 recruits E6AP (UBE3A ubiquitin ligase) to form a ternary E6–E6AP–p53 complex; E6AP polyubiquitinates p53, targeting it for 26S proteasome degradation. Loss of p53 disables the G1/S checkpoint (removing arrest after DNA damage), prevents apoptosis in genomically damaged cells, and impairs nucleotide excision repair. E6 additionally activates hTERT (telomerase reverse transcriptase) transcription, conferring replicative immortality by preventing telomere shortening. E6 also degrades PDZ-domain proteins Dlg and Scribble, disrupting cell polarity and tight-junction organisation — enabling the invasive phenotype. High-risk HPV E6 has higher E6AP affinity than low-risk types — the molecular correlate of oncogenic risk.

3 · E7 oncoprotein — Rb inactivation and S-phase deregulation

E7 (98 aa) drives cell cycle entry by inactivating all three Rb family members: pRb, p107, and p130. E7's LXCXE motif binds the pocket domain of pRb, disrupting the pRb–E2F repressor complex and releasing free E2F transcription factors that activate S-phase gene expression (cyclin E, DHFR, PCNA, thymidine kinase). High-risk HPV-16 E7 has ~100-fold higher pRb affinity than low-risk HPV E7 — the molecular basis of oncogenic selectivity. E7 also promotes pRb proteasomal degradation and independently inactivates p21 (CDKN1A) and p27 (CDKN1B) cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitors, eliminating all remaining brakes on cell proliferation. The result is constitutive, checkpoint-free cell cycle progression.

4 · Immune evasion — no viraemia, no inflammation

HPV evolved an exceptionally stealthy strategy. There is no systemic viraemia — replication is confined to the epithelial surface; virus is shed only from terminally differentiated (dying) superficial keratinocytes. This eliminates danger signals (DAMPs, necrosis) that would trigger DC maturation and innate immune activation. E7 degrades IRF-3 and inhibits TLR9-mediated IFN production; E6/E7 suppress NF-κB activation, reducing IL-1α, IL-8, and TNF-α. Langerhans cells in HPV-infected epithelium are functionally impaired. Despite these evasion mechanisms, most infections are cleared within 1–2 years by CD4+/CD8+ T cells and HPV-specific IgG; persistence occurs in ~10% and is the necessary condition for oncogenesis.

5 · Integration and the oncogenic switch

In productive lytic infection, HPV DNA is maintained as an episome replicating under E1/E2 control. The transition to malignancy is triggered by integration of the viral genome into host chromosomal DNA, preferentially disrupting the E2 ORF. Loss of E2 eliminates its repressor function on the E6/E7 promoter, causing persistent high-level E6/E7 overexpression — the molecular oncogenic switch. Chromosomal instability driven by E6/E7-mediated checkpoint loss then selects for additional somatic mutations (TP53, CDKN2A) in the evolving CIN→carcinoma progression.

Treatment & Prevention

Gardasil-9 (9-valent VLP vaccine)Covers HPV 6, 11, 16, 18, 31, 33, 45, 52, 58; L1 virus-like particles (VLPs) generate potent neutralising IgG; >95% efficacy against CIN2/3 in seronegative recipients; recommended ages 9–26 (up to 45 in select populations); 2-dose schedule (ages 9–14) or 3-dose (age 15+ or immunocompromised)
Cervarix (bivalent)Covers HPV 16 and 18; AS04 adjuvant (MPL + alum); ~93% efficacy against CIN2/3; cross-protection against 31, 33, 45; used in some national programmes
Cervical screeningPrimary HPV DNA testing (preferred in most guidelines) or cervical cytology (Pap smear); primary HPV testing every 3–5 years from age 21–25; p16/Ki-67 dual stain as triage for equivocal results; cotesting option in some guidelines
Precancerous lesion treatmentCIN 1: observation (most regress); CIN 2–3: LEEP (loop electrosurgical excision), cold coagulation, cryotherapy, or cone biopsy to remove transformation zone. No approved antivirals directly target HPV replication.
Invasive cervical cancerEarly stage: radical hysterectomy ± pelvic lymph node dissection or concurrent chemoradiotherapy (cisplatin + radiation); advanced/metastatic: platinum-based chemotherapy ± bevacizumab; pembrolizumab (anti-PD-1) approved for recurrent/metastatic PD-L1-positive cervical cancer
HPV+ OPSCCConcurrent cisplatin-based chemoradiotherapy (definitive or adjuvant); TORS (transoral robotic surgery) for surgical cases; de-escalation trials (reduced-dose chemoradiotherapy) ongoing given excellent prognosis of HPV+ OPSCC
