Alveolus

Terminal air sac of the lung respiratory zone — the site of O₂/CO₂ exchange by passive diffusion across the air-blood barrier. ~300 million alveoli in the adult provide ~70 m² total surface. Each alveolus is ~0.2 mm diameter and lined by a continuous epithelium of AT1 cells (flat, ~95% surface, gas exchange) and AT2 cells (cuboidal, ~5% surface, surfactant + stem cell). The epithelium rests on a shared basement membrane fused at points to the pulmonary capillary endothelium, reducing the air-blood barrier to as little as 0.2–0.5 µm. Surfactant (from AT2 lamellar bodies) reduces surface tension to <5 mN/m, maintaining alveolar patency against the LaPlace collapse pressure.

Overview

The alveolus (plural: alveoli) is the functional unit of pulmonary gas exchange — the terminal air sac at the end of the branching airways where inhaled oxygen diffuses into pulmonary capillary blood and CO₂ diffuses out. The lungs contain approximately 300 million alveoli in the adult human, with a total internal surface area of approximately 70 m² — an extraordinary surface-to-volume ratio engineered by evolution for maximal gas exchange.

Each alveolus is lined by a continuous epithelium composed of AT1 cells (~95% of surface area) and AT2 cells (~5% of surface area, ~60% of epithelial cells by count). The epithelium rests on a shared basement membrane apposed directly to the pulmonary capillary endothelium — fused at many points to reduce the air-blood barrier to as little as 0.2–0.5 µm. Pulmonary surfactant secreted by AT2 cells covers the alveolar surface, reducing surface tension from ~70 mN/m (water) to <5 mN/m at end-expiration and preventing alveolar collapse (atelectasis).

Alveolar macrophages (AMs): Resident macrophages patrol the alveolar surface — the most abundant leukocyte in the lung (~93% of BAL cells). First cellular responder to inhaled pathogens including M. tuberculosis. Phagocytose particles, debris, and apoptotic cells. SP-A and SP-D (collectins from AT2 cells) act as opsonins, facilitating AM phagocytosis.

Function — Gas Exchange and Ventilation-Perfusion Matching

Gas exchange by passive diffusion follows Fick's law (flux = D × A × ΔP / T). At rest: O₂ diffuses from alveolar air (PAO₂ ~100 mmHg) into capillary blood (PvO₂ ~40 mmHg) along a ~60 mmHg gradient; CO₂ diffuses in the opposite direction (PvCO₂ ~46 mmHg → PACO₂ ~40 mmHg) along only a ~6 mmHg gradient (CO₂ is ~20× more soluble than O₂ — smaller gradient sufficient). Equilibration is virtually complete within ~0.25 s of the ~0.75 s capillary transit time — a 3-fold safety margin at rest.

V̇gas = D × A × ΔP / T
D = diffusivity (CO₂ ~20× O₂), A = ~70 m² total surface, T = 0.2–0.5 µm (thin side barrier), ΔP = partial pressure gradient at rest: O₂ ~60 mmHg, CO₂ ~6 mmHg

LaPlace law and surfactant: P = 2γ/r. At r ~0.1 mm (fully deflated alveolus), without surfactant (γ = 70 mN/m) → collapsing pressure ~1,400 Pa — incompatible with breathing. AT2 surfactant reduces γ to <5 mN/m → collapsing pressure falls to <100 Pa — easily overcome by inspiratory muscles. Normal lung compliance: ~200 mL/cmH₂O (↓ dramatically in surfactant deficiency — neonatal RDS, ARDS).

Ventilation-Perfusion (V̇/Q̇) matching: Each alveolus must receive matched air (ventilation) and blood (perfusion). Ideal V̇/Q̇ ~0.8–1.0. Gravity distributes both V̇ and Q̇ unevenly (Q̇ is more gravity-dependent → V̇/Q̇ higher at apex, lower at base). Hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction (HPV) — local arteriolar constriction in response to low PAO₂ — redirects blood flow away from poorly ventilated alveoli, actively matching perfusion to ventilation. V̇/Q̇ mismatch is the most common cause of hypoxaemia in respiratory disease.

Pathology

ARDS (Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome)

Diffuse alveolar damage (DAD): epithelial (AT1 + AT2) and endothelial injury → alveolar flooding with protein-rich exudate, hyaline membrane formation, loss of surfactant → alveolar collapse → V̇/Q̇ mismatch + shunt → severe bilateral hypoxaemia. Berlin criteria: PaO₂/FiO₂ <300, bilateral infiltrates on CXR/CT, non-cardiogenic. Mortality 30–50% in severe ARDS. Treatment: lung-protective ventilation (6 mL/kg IBW, plateau P <30 cmH₂O), prone positioning, conservative fluid strategy.

Pneumonia (Alveolar Consolidation)

Bacterial or viral invasion → inflammatory exudate fills alveoli → consolidation (air replaced by fluid/cells) → impaired ventilation while perfusion maintained → V̇/Q̇ mismatch → hypoxaemia. Classic lobar pneumonia: Streptococcus pneumoniae; bronchopneumonia: Staphylococcus aureus, Klebsiella; atypical: Mycoplasma, Legionella; viral: COVID-19 bilateral interstitial pattern.

Pulmonary Oedema

Alveolar flooding from elevated capillary hydrostatic pressure (cardiogenic: LV failure → ↑pulmonary venous pressure → capillary leak) or capillary leak (non-cardiogenic: inflammation, ARDS). Clinical: frothy pink sputum, bilateral rales, bat-wing infiltrates on CXR. Starling equation imbalance: capillary hydrostatic pressure (Pcap, normally ~8 mmHg) rises above oncotic pressure (π, ~25 mmHg) → net filtration into alveolus.

Emphysema

Protease-mediated destruction of alveolar walls (septal destruction) → fewer, larger air spaces → ↓total surface area → ↓gas exchange capacity → impaired O₂ diffusion (↓DLCO) + loss of radial airway traction → airflow obstruction. Cause: cigarette smoke → oxidative stress → neutrophil/macrophage elastase overactivity → protease-antiprotease imbalance. Also: α1-antitrypsin deficiency (SERPINA1 mutation — panacinar emphysema at younger age).

Idiopathic Pulmonary Fibrosis (IPF)

Progressive fibrotic replacement of normal alveolar architecture (UIP pattern: honeycombing + traction bronchiectasis) → loss of gas-exchange units → restrictive ventilatory defect (↓TLC, ↓FVC) + ↓DLCO → progressive hypoxaemia. Median survival 3–5 years from diagnosis. AT2 injury and aberrant repair underlie the fibrotic cascade. Anti-fibrotics: nintedanib (↓fibroblast proliferation), pirfenidone (anti-TGF-β).

Pulmonary TB (Alveolar Macrophage Infection)

Mycobacterium tuberculosis infects alveolar macrophages → intracellular replication → granuloma formation (Langhans giant cells, caseating necrosis) → cavitation and destruction of alveolar structure. Inhaled droplets of 1–5 µm reach alveoli. Ghon focus (primary complex) in subpleural alveoli.
