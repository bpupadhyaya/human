Cardiac Conduction System

The specialized tissue network routing each cardiac impulse from its SA node origin through the AV node, Bundle of His, bundle branches, and Purkinje fibers to every cardiomyocyte in the ventricles — ensuring sequential AV activation and near-simultaneous ventricular depolarization for efficient ejection.

Aliases: specialized conduction tissue, cardiac impulse system Scale: 05-Tissue Status: Draft · reviewed 2026-06-03

Overview

The cardiac conduction system is a network of specialized cardiac muscle tissue whose principal function is to generate, route, delay, and distribute the electrical impulse that initiates each heartbeat. It is distinct from the working myocardium in electrophysiology, gene expression, and architecture: conduction-system cells are optimized for either automaticity (pacemaking) or rapid impulse propagation, not for generating mechanical force.

The total time from SA node firing to complete ventricular activation is approximately 160–200 ms in the healthy heart — the PR interval on the ECG.

Hierarchical safety: Each level of the conduction system has an intrinsic escape rate: SA node 60–100 bpm, AV node 40–60 bpm, Purkinje 20–40 bpm. Failure of a higher pacemaker allows a lower one to escape — preventing asystole from any single-point failure.

SA Node

The SA node (~15 mm × 5 mm × 1–2 mm) resides in the crista terminalis of the right atrium. It contains leading pacemaker cells (see SA node cell) and transitional cells. Its spontaneous rate (60–100 bpm intrinsic) suppresses all downstream pacemakers through overdrive suppression.

The AV node delay (~100–150 ms) is physiologically essential: it ensures atria contract and fill the ventricles before ventricular contraction begins.

Purkinje fibers are the largest cardiomyocyte-lineage cells (~80 µm diameter), with abundant glycogen, sparse myofibrils, and very high Cx40 (connexin-40) expression — enabling rapid cell-to-cell conduction.

Fibrous Skeleton as Electrical Insulator

The cardiac fibrous skeleton (collagen rings at AV and arterial orifices, plus central fibrous body) electrically insulates the atria from the ventricles everywhere except the Bundle of His. This isolation makes the AV delay possible and makes accessory pathways (as in Wolff-Parkinson-White) pathological.

Function

Sequential AV Activation

  1. SA node fires → atria depolarize and contract → blood moves from atria to ventricles (~20–25% of EDV at rest, the "atrial kick").
  2. AV node delay (~120 ms) → ventricles are filled before they contract.
  3. Purkinje system → ventricles depolarize near-simultaneously apex-upward → organized wringing contraction → efficient ejection into great vessels.

Overdrive Suppression

When the SA node fires at 75 bpm, it constantly overrides downstream pacemakers. This occurs because repeated rapid depolarizations accumulate Na⁺ inside lower pacemaker cells, activating Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase more aggressively, which hyperpolarizes them and suppresses their automaticity. When the SA node fails, the suppressed pacemaker takes a moment (up to several seconds in severe SSS) to escape — producing the pause characteristic of sinus arrest.

Disruption of Sequencing

  • Loss of AV synchrony (complete AV block, atrial fibrillation) → loss of atrial kick → up to 20–25% reduction in CO at rest, more at high HR.
  • Left bundle branch block (LBBB) → delayed LV activation → dyssynchronous contraction → reduced EF → cardiac resynchronization therapy (CRT) can restore synchrony.
