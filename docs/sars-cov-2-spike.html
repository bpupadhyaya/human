SARS-CoV-2 Spike Glycoprotein

The homotrimeric fusion machine that enabled a pandemic — and the antigen that stopped it.

The spike (S) protein is the sole surface glycoprotein of SARS-CoV-2 responsible for host cell recognition and membrane fusion. S1 receptor-binding domain (RBD) engages human ACE2 with ~15 nM affinity — the obligate first step of infection. S2 executes membrane fusion via an irreversible 6-helix bundle collapse. Every approved COVID-19 vaccine worldwide delivers spike as its immunogen, exploiting proline substitutions (K986P/V987P, the “2P mutation”) to lock spike in the prefusion conformation targeted by neutralizing antibodies.

SARS-CoV-2 Spike (S) Glycoprotein

Homotrimeric class I fusion glycoprotein (~180 kDa/protomer). The S1 subunit contains the N-terminal domain (NTD) and receptor-binding domain (RBD); the S2 subunit mediates membrane fusion via a 6-helix bundle (6HB). A furin site at S1/S2 (PRRAR↓S, absent in SARS-CoV-1) is processed in the Golgi of the producer cell, pre-activating spike on released virions. TMPRSS2 cleaves S2′ (R815) at the target cell surface to activate fusion. ~22 N-linked glycans per protomer mask ~40% of the surface, providing immune evasion while permitting ACE2 binding. Every approved COVID-19 vaccine encodes or delivers spike, most using 2P (K986P/V987P) proline substitutions to stabilize the immunogenic prefusion conformation.

The glycan shield: Each spike protomer carries ~22 N-linked glycans and 3 O-linked glycans, covering ~40% of the protein surface. This shield masks conserved epitopes from antibody access (immune evasion) while still permitting ACE2 binding. The N343 glycan acts as a “glycan gate” modulating RBD up/down conformational dynamics. Vaccine-elicited antibodies that penetrate the glycan shield to reach conserved S2 HR1 or FP epitopes have broad neutralization potential against multiple SARS-CoV-2 variants.

Infection Mechanism

Step 1 · Furin processing in producer cell (S1/S2 cleavage)

During virion assembly, spike traverses the secretory pathway. The furin recognition sequence PRRAR (residues 681–685) is cleaved by furin in the trans-Golgi network, generating non-covalently associated S1 (N-terminal) and S2 (C-terminal, membrane-anchored) subunits. This pre-activation — absent in SARS-CoV-1 — equips virions with partially activated spike, accelerating entry kinetics in the lung. The Omicron lineage progressively acquired enhanced furin site processing efficiency.

Step 2 · ACE2 binding via RBD “up” conformation

The RBD spontaneously samples “up” and “down” conformations. In the up conformation, the receptor-binding motif (RBM, residues 437–508) is exposed and engages the ACE2 N-terminal peptidase domain in a concave binding surface (~1,700 Å2 buried surface area). Critical RBD contacts include Y449, G496, T500, N501, G502, Y505; key ACE2 contacts include K31, E35, D38, Y41, Q42, L79, M82, Y83, K353. Binding affinity Kd ~15 nM (vs. ~325 nM for SARS-CoV-1 RBD), reflecting adapted contact residues driving pandemic transmissibility.

Step 3 · TMPRSS2 cleavage at S2′ (R815) — preferred lung pathway

ACE2-bound spike exposes the S2′ cleavage site (R815). Host TMPRSS2 (a type II serine protease highly expressed on type II alveolar epithelial cells and airway epithelium co-expressed with ACE2) cleaves S2′, releasing S1 and activating the S2 fusion peptide. This is the kinetically rapid, endosome-independent entry pathway dominant in the lung. Alternatively, endocytosed virus is cleaved by cathepsin B/L in the late endosome (slower; dominant in TMPRSS2-low cell types).

Step 4 · 6-helix bundle formation and membrane fusion

After S2′ cleavage: the fusion peptide (residues ~816–833) inserts into the host cell membrane. HR1 and HR2 are then free to interact. HR2 folds antiparallel against HR1 in a thermodynamically irreversible collapse, forming the trimeric coiled-coil 6-helix bundle (6HB). This jackknife motion physically draws the viral envelope and host cell membrane together, generating the energy for membrane merger and fusion pore formation. Genome-containing ribonucleoprotein complex is then delivered to the cytoplasm for replication.

Step 5 · Immune evasion and variant antigenic evolution

SARS-CoV-2 spike evolves primarily under immune selection pressure. VOC mutations cluster in the RBM (Class I/II antibody epitopes) and NTD (NTD supersite) to escape vaccine-elicited and infection-elicited antibodies while maintaining ACE2 affinity. Alpha N501Y: ~10-fold increased ACE2 affinity; Beta K417N/E484K: escape Class I/II; Delta L452R/T478K: enhanced fusogenicity; Omicron BA.1: 37 spike mutations (15 in RBD) achieving both high ACE2 affinity and near-complete escape of ancestral-vaccine antibodies through compensatory mutations.

The 2P mutation strategy: The K986P/V987P substitutions in the HR1 hinge region introduce prolines at positions that would otherwise undergo a conformational change during the prefusion→postfusion transition. Prolines cannot adopt the phi/psi angles required for the postfusion conformation, locking spike in the prefusion state at 37°C. Without 2P, recombinant spike spontaneously collapses to the non-immunogenic postfusion hairpin — which is not recognized by most potent neutralizing antibodies. The 2P concept was pioneered for RSV F by McLellan et al. (2013) and adapted for SARS-CoV-2 by Wrapp et al. in weeks during early 2020.

Spike-Mediated Pathology Beyond Entry

ACE2 downregulationSpike binding → ACE2 internalization and shedding (ADAM17-mediated) → local Ang II excess → vascular inflammation, ROS production, cardiac injury; exacerbates ARDS via loss of ACE2 RAAS counter-regulation in the lung
Endothelial activationSpike (at subinfectious concentrations) activates TLR4 on endothelial cells → NF-κB → IL-6, IL-8, ICAM-1, VCAM-1; impairs eNOS-dependent NO signaling → vasoconstriction; promotes mitochondrial fission → mitochondrial ROS; endotheliitis is a hallmark of severe COVID-19 autopsy findings
Platelet activationSpike activates platelets via ACE2 and via FcγRIIA (antibody-dependent in ADE contexts) → platelet aggregation → microthrombi; contributes to the COVID-19 coagulopathy phenotype (elevated D-dimer, fibrinogen, factor VIII)
Neurological effectsSpike interacts with pericyte ACE2 at the blood-brain barrier → pericyte injury → BBB dysfunction; spike-induced neuroinflammation and complement activation may contribute to Long COVID cognitive symptoms (brain fog); spike RNA detected in brain endothelium and neurons in autopsy studies
