COVID-19 Disease

The multi-system illness caused by SARS-CoV-2 infection — spanning a spectrum from asymptomatic carriage to fatal acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS). Pathophysiology is driven by direct viral cytopathic injury to ACE2-expressing epithelia, dysregulated innate immune activation, coagulopathy, and in a subset of patients, a devastating hyperinflammatory cytokine storm.

Scale: L05 Tissue/Organ / Systems Pathogen: SARS-CoV-2 (Betacoronavirus) Type: Respiratory / Multi-system Disease Status: Draft · reviewed 2026-06-05

Overview

COVID-19 (Coronavirus Disease 2019) emerged from Wuhan, China in late 2019 and was declared a pandemic by the WHO on March 11, 2020. The causative agent, SARS-CoV-2, is a positive-sense single-stranded RNA betacoronavirus that enters human cells via high-affinity binding of its spike protein to angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 (ACE2), primed by the transmembrane serine protease TMPRSS2.

Clinical severity is remarkably heterogeneous: roughly 80% of infections are mild or asymptomatic, 15–20% develop moderate-to-severe pneumonia requiring medical attention, and ~5% progress to critical illness with ARDS, multi-organ failure, and requirement for mechanical ventilation. Age, obesity, diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and immunosuppression are the dominant risk factors for severe disease.

The pandemic directly caused an estimated 7–20 million deaths globally through 2023 depending on excess mortality methodology, and produced over 770 million confirmed cases. It transformed antiviral drug development, immunology research, and respiratory medicine within an unprecedented three-year timeframe.

Key insight: COVID-19 is not a single disease but a spectrum of overlapping syndromes: a viral illness, a pneumonia, an endotheliitis/coagulopathy, a hyperinflammatory syndrome, and a post-acute sequelae syndrome (Long COVID) — each with distinct pathophysiological drivers and treatment targets.

Disease Phases

COVID-19 progresses through temporally distinct phases, with different organs and immunological processes dominant at each stage. Clinical management decisions are phase-dependent: antivirals have maximal benefit in early infection, whereas anti-inflammatory agents (dexamethasone, tocilizumab) are harmful in early disease and beneficial only once hyperinflammation is established.

Phase 1 — Incubation

The incubation period — from viral exposure to symptom onset — is typically 2–14 days with a median of 5 days for the original Wuhan strain and early variants. Omicron sub-lineages have a shorter median incubation (~3 days), consistent with predominantly upper airway replication. The incubation period reflects the time required for the virus to replicate from the initial inoculum in nasal/pharyngeal epithelium to a viral load sufficient to trigger innate immune activation and tissue damage.

Transmission is possible 1–2 days before symptom onset (pre-symptomatic transmission), which substantially limited the effectiveness of symptom-based isolation strategies and drove the pandemic’s spread.

Phase 2 — Early Infection (Mild/Asymptomatic)

Approximately 80% of individuals develop mild or no symptoms. Viral replication peaks in the nasopharynx within the first 3–5 days of symptoms, with peak viral RNA titers of 107–1010 copies/mL nasal swab. Dominant symptoms in this phase reflect upper respiratory tract involvement: fever, dry cough, fatigue, myalgia, headache, and anosmia/ageusia (loss of smell/taste, affecting up to 60% of early pandemic patients; less prevalent in Omicron due to reduced olfactory epithelium tropism).

In this phase, innate immune responses (type I/III interferon, NK cells, innate T cells) are the primary defense. Patients who mount a robust early IFN response typically limit viral spread to the upper airways and recover without progression. Conversely, delayed or impaired IFN responses — due to TLR7 mutations, age-related innate immune senescence, or pre-existing autoantibodies against IFN-α — are strongly associated with progression to severe disease.

Phase 3 — Pulmonary Phase (Moderate)

In ~15–20% of patients, approximately 7–10 days after symptom onset, virus descends to the lower respiratory tract. SARS-CoV-2 infects ACE2/TMPRSS2-expressing type II pneumocytes in the alveolar epithelium, inducing direct cytopathic effects: syncytium formation, programmed cell death (apoptosis and pyroptosis), and barrier disruption. Loss of type II pneumocyte function impairs surfactant production, reducing alveolar surface tension and compliance.

Clinical hallmarks include dyspnea, progressive oxygen desaturation (SpO2 94–95%), bilateral ground-glass opacities on CT chest, and elevated inflammatory markers (CRP, ferritin, D-dimer, IL-6). A pathognomonic and clinically important phenomenon is “happy hypoxia” (silent hypoxemia): patients with SpO2 as low as 80–85% may remain subjectively comfortable and non-dyspneic, reflecting blunted hypoxic ventilatory drive and rapid deterioration risk. Pulse oximetry monitoring during home isolation became standard practice because of this phenomenon.

Phase 4 — Hyperinflammatory / Cytokine Storm (~5% severe)

A subset of patients, predominantly those with established risk factors, develop a hyperinflammatory state approximately 10–14 days after symptom onset. This is characterized by:

  • Rapid clinical deterioration: SpO2 <94%, PaO2/FiO2 (P/F ratio) <300 mmHg defining ARDS (Berlin criteria)
  • Markedly elevated IL-6 (>100 pg/mL), ferritin (>500 μg/L), CRP (>150 mg/L), LDH, D-dimer
  • Lymphopenia (CD4+ and CD8+ T cell depletion) with relative neutrophilia
  • Endothelial injury, microvascular thrombosis, and systemic coagulopathy

The immunopathology overlaps with macrophage activation syndrome (MAS) / hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH): hyperactivated monocyte-derived macrophages in alveoli and liver drive uncontrolled cytokine secretion. Key effector cytokines include IL-6 (trans-signaling), TNF-α, IL-1β (via NLRP3), IFN-γ, and GM-CSF (macrophage-T cell amplification loop). Diffuse alveolar damage (DAD) is the histopathological hallmark: hyaline membrane formation, fibrin deposition, and loss of alveolar architecture.

Phase 5 — Recovery

Patients who survive the hyperinflammatory phase enter a recovery phase characterized by viral clearance (usually within 3–4 weeks of symptom onset for immunocompetent individuals), gradual resolution of radiological abnormalities, and restoration of oxygenation. Pulmonary fibrosis develops in a minority of severe cases. However, a substantial proportion of patients — including those with initially mild disease — enter a post-acute sequelae phase (Long COVID; see below).

Pathophysiology

Viral Entry and Direct Cytopathic Effects

SARS-CoV-2 uses a two-receptor, two-protease mechanism for cell entry. The spike protein RBD binds ACE2 (Kd ~4–35 nM for ancestral strain). TMPRSS2 cleaves the S2′ site at the cell surface, enabling direct membrane fusion and bypassing the endosomal pathway. In ACE2/TMPRSS2-expressing cells (type II pneumocytes, nasal goblet cells, enterocytes), this allows rapid, highly efficient infection.

Direct cytopathic effects on type II pneumocytes include: syncytium formation (spike-mediated cell-cell fusion), mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress, ER stress and unfolded protein response (UPR), caspase-mediated apoptosis, and NLRP3/caspase-1-mediated pyroptosis. Pyroptosis releases IL-1β, IL-18, and DAMPs that trigger neighboring innate immune cells, amplifying local inflammation disproportionate to viral load.

The virus also infects macrophages and dendritic cells via ACE2-independent mechanisms, driving innate immune dysregulation without productive replication — contributing to an “abortive infection” pattern of hyperactivation without viral clearance in professional immune cells.

Diffuse Alveolar Damage (DAD) Pattern

Autopsy series from severe COVID-19 consistently demonstrate diffuse alveolar damage (DAD) as the predominant histopathological finding. DAD progresses through two phases:

  • Exudative phase (days 1–7 of ARDS): Alveolar capillary leak, interstitial and alveolar edema, hyaline membrane formation (fibrin + cellular debris), desquamation of type II pneumocytes, intraalveolar fibrin. Corresponds to the CT finding of bilateral ground-glass opacification.
  • Proliferative/fibrotic phase (days 7–21): Type II pneumocyte hyperplasia (attempted regeneration), myofibroblast proliferation, interstitial and intra-alveolar fibrosis. In survivors this resolves partially; in a minority it progresses to organizing pneumonia or established pulmonary fibrosis.

A distinctive feature of COVID-19 DAD vs. other viral pneumonias is the pronounced vascular involvement: extensive microthrombi in pulmonary capillaries and small vessels, thrombotic microangiopathy, and endothelialitis (direct ACE2-mediated infection of endothelial cells + macrophage-driven endothelial activation). This vascular tropism underlies the coagulopathy (see below) and the frequent cardiac, renal, and hepatic involvement in severe COVID-19.

Endothelial Injury and COVID-19 Coagulopathy

SARS-CoV-2-driven endothelial injury is a central feature of severe COVID-19 and distinguishes it from other viral pneumonias. Mechanisms include: direct ACE2-mediated endothelial infection, cytokine-mediated endothelial activation (IL-6, TNF-α), complement deposition (MAC and C3b on endothelial surfaces), and NET (neutrophil extracellular trap) formation by activated neutrophils.

The resulting coagulopathy is characterized by:

  • Markedly elevated D-dimer (fibrin degradation product; D-dimer >1000 ng/mL at admission predicts ~18-fold increased mortality in one Wuhan cohort)
  • Elevated factor VIII, vWF, and fibrinogen (acute phase reactants; reflect endothelial activation)
  • Venous thromboembolism (VTE) risk: 25–31% incidence of DVT/PE in ICU patients in early pandemic cohorts, far exceeding standard ICU VTE rates
  • Arterial thrombosis: Stroke, myocardial infarction, and limb ischemia, particularly in young patients without traditional risk factors
  • Prolonged PT/aPTT and thrombocytopenia in severe/DIC-like presentations

Despite having the appearance of DIC, most COVID-19 coagulopathy is a hypercoagulable (not consumptive) state — fibrinogen and factor VIII are elevated rather than depleted, and thrombocytopenia is typically moderate rather than severe. This pattern distinguishes it from classic sepsis-associated DIC and informed therapeutic anticoagulation trials.

Macrophage Activation Syndrome Overlap

A subset of the most severe COVID-19 cases meets diagnostic criteria for secondary HLH/MAS: ferritin >10,000 μg/L, cytopenias, splenomegaly, elevated liver enzymes, and bone marrow hemophagocytosis. The IFN-γ → macrophage activation → IL-18/IL-6 → NK/CTL impairment amplification loop (see cytokine storm page) is the key driver. HScore ≥169 has ~93% sensitivity and ~86% specificity for HLH in non-COVID-19 settings; application in COVID-19 is more complex due to overlapping inflammatory markers.

Antiviral Treatments

Antiviral efficacy is highly time-dependent and greatest when initiated within 5 days of symptom onset, when viral replication is maximal. Once patients reach the hyperinflammatory phase, antivirals offer diminishing returns and immunomodulation becomes the therapeutic priority.

Anti-inflammatory and Supportive Treatments

Anti-inflammatory agents are the mainstay of treatment for moderate-to-severe COVID-19 once the hyperinflammatory phase begins. Critically, dexamethasone and IL-6R blockers are harmful (or show no benefit) when given to patients who do not require supplemental oxygen, reinforcing the phase-specific nature of COVID-19 therapy.

Long COVID (Post-Acute Sequelae of SARS-CoV-2)

Long COVID (PASC) is defined by the WHO as continuation or development of new symptoms 3 months after initial SARS-CoV-2 infection, lasting for at least 2 months with no alternative explanation. Prevalence estimates range from 10–30% across cohort studies, depending on definition, time point, and variant — though Omicron-era studies suggest lower risk (~4–10%) compared to pre-Omicron waves, and vaccination reduces risk by 50–60%.

Key Symptoms

  • Post-exertional malaise (PEM): Worsening of symptoms following physical or cognitive exertion — the hallmark feature, distinguishing Long COVID from simple deconditioning; mechanistically similar to myalgic encephalomyelitis/CFS
  • Fatigue: Present in 50–70% of Long COVID cases; can be severe and disabling
  • Cognitive impairment (“brain fog”): Memory deficits, word-finding difficulty, concentration impairment; associated with neuroimaging abnormalities in some patients; PASC patients show ~3-point reduction in IQ equivalent in cognitive testing (Taquet et al., NEJM 2024)
  • Dysautonomia / POTS (Postural Orthostatic Tachycardia Syndrome): Heart rate increase ≥30 bpm on standing; lightheadedness; palpitations; reported in 10–14% of Long COVID patients; possibly autoantibody-mediated (anti-α1-adrenergic, anti-β2-adrenergic antibodies)
  • Breathlessness and chest pain (even with cleared pulmonary CT)
  • Sleep disturbance, anxiety, depression

Proposed Mechanisms

Multiple non-mutually-exclusive mechanisms have been proposed and have supporting evidence:

  • Persistent viral reservoir: SARS-CoV-2 RNA and antigen detected in gut epithelium, lymph nodes, mesenteric tissue, and CNS up to 18 months after acute infection in autopsy and biopsy studies; viral antigen detected in blood of Long COVID patients; supports ongoing immune activation
  • Autoantibody generation: Molecular mimicry between SARS-CoV-2 antigens and host proteins generates autoantibodies against adrenergic receptors, ACE2, coagulation factors, and type I interferons; partially explain POTS, dysautonomia, and immune dysregulation
  • Microbiome dysbiosis: Profound alterations in gut microbiome composition (reduced Faecalibacterium prausnitzii, Bifidobacterium) persist >6 months; gut dysbiosis correlates with neuropsychiatric symptom severity; reduced short-chain fatty acid production may affect vagal nerve tone and CNS function
  • Endothelial dysfunction and microclots: Fibrin amyloid microclots resistant to fibrinolysis detected in blood of Long COVID patients (Pretorius et al.); platelet hyperactivation; endothelial dysfunction on flow-mediated dilation testing
  • T cell exhaustion / immune dysregulation: Persistent CD8+ T cell exhaustion (PD-1hi phenotype), altered NK cell function, reduced class-switched memory B cells in Long COVID vs. recovered controls
  • Reactivation of latent herpesviruses: EBV and HHV-6 reactivation detected in a subset of Long COVID patients; may contribute to neurological symptoms and T cell dysfunction

As of 2026, there are no FDA/EMA-approved treatments specifically for Long COVID. Clinical trials have investigated antivirals (low-dose naltrexone, nirmatrelvir/ritonavir), anticoagulants, SSRI/SNRI (venlafaxine in RECOVER-VITAL), and immunomodulators. Pacing strategies (activity management to prevent PEM) are recommended based on ME/CFS experience.
