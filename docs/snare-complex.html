SNARE Complex

SNARE (Soluble NSF Attachment Protein Receptor) proteins are the minimal machinery for intracellular membrane fusion — including synaptic vesicle exocytosis, secretory granule fusion, and intracellular trafficking. A synaptic SNARE complex consists of three proteins (VAMP-2, SNAP-25, syntaxin-1) that coil into a 4-helix bundle generating ~65 kBT of free energy to drive membrane merger.

SNARE Complex

SNARE proteins are the universal engine of intracellular membrane fusion. Three synaptic SNAREs — VAMP-2, SNAP-25, and syntaxin-1 — zipper into a stable 4-helix bundle that mechanically drives bilayer merger in submillisecond response to Ca²⁺ influx, releasing neurotransmitter into the synaptic cleft.

Overview

The SNARE hypothesis (Söllner, Rothman, 1993) proposes that every membrane fusion event in eukaryotic cell biology is mediated by cognate v-SNARE (vesicle membrane) and t-SNARE (target membrane) protein pairs. Approximately 36 SNARE proteins exist in humans, each sharing a conserved ~60 amino acid coiled-coil SNARE domain. Classification: the central “0 layer” of the assembled bundle contains either an arginine (R-SNARE) or a glutamine (Qa, Qb, Qc-SNARE), and cognate fusion requires one R + three Q SNAREs to form a functional complex. This R/Q rule governs fusion specificity across all membrane compartments.

The Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine 2013 was awarded to James Rothman, Randy Schekman, and Thomas Südhof for the discovery of the machinery regulating vesicle transport and the SNARE mechanism. The synaptic SNARE complex is the most extensively characterised fusion machine in biology — its 3D crystal structure was solved in 1998 (Südhof/Brunger groups), revealing an extended parallel 4-helix bundle with 16 layered hydrophobic contacts.

Fusion Mechanism

  1. Docking and template assembly: Munc18-1 binds syntaxin-1 in closed conformation; Munc13 (MUN domain) catalyses opening of syntaxin and templates partial SNARE assembly; RIM1α tethers synaptic vesicle via Rab3, co-organises Ca²⁺ channels at active zone
  2. Priming: SNARE zippering initiates N-terminally, forming a partially assembled complex (~4 of 16 hydrophobic layers); complexin I/II binds the partially assembled complex and inserts a short “accessory helix” as a clamp, arresting zippering and holding the vesicle in the readily releasable pool (RRP)
  3. Ca²⁺ triggering: AP → Cav2.1 Ca²⁺ influx → local [Ca²⁺] rises to ~100 µM at active zone microdomains → synaptotagmin-1 C2A + C2B domains bind 3–5 Ca²⁺ ions (Kᵈ ~200 µM in vitro; effectively ~10 µM near Ca²⁺ channels in vivo) → C2B domain inserts into the plasma membrane bilayer → displaces complexin accessory helix clamp
  4. Full SNARE zippering (zipper model): C-terminal zippering completes the 4-helix bundle → generates ~65 kBT per complex; force drives membrane approximation → hemifusion stalk → fusion pore opening → neurotransmitter floods the cleft within <0.5 ms
  5. Post-fusion disassembly: cis-SNARE complex (all four helices on same membrane) is highly stable (T½ ~5 h at 37°C); NSF (N-ethylmaleimide-sensitive factor; AAA⁺ ATPase hexamer) + α-SNAP (soluble NSF attachment protein) hydrolyse ATP → disassemble cis-SNARE → free monomeric SNAREs recycled for next round
  6. Vesicle recycling: kiss-and-stay (fusion pore closes; SV retains identity; rapid re-use) vs. full-collapse fusion (SV membrane integrates into plasma membrane; retrieved by clathrin/dynamin/AP2-mediated endocytosis; SV reformed via endosome)

Beyond the Synapse: SNARE Proteins in Intracellular Trafficking

The synaptic SNARE complex is the best-characterised example of a conserved mechanism used throughout all intracellular membrane trafficking:

NSF and α/β/γ-SNAP are ubiquitous disassembly factors across all these events — NSF is an essential housekeeping gene. In yeast, the homologues are Sec18 (NSF) and Sec17 (α-SNAP).

Clinical and Pharmacological Relevance

Botulinum Toxin Therapeutics (BoNT/A — BOTOX)

BoNT/A (serotype A; Botox®, Dysport®, Xeomin®): cleaves SNAP-25 at Gln197–Arg206 → blocks ACh release at NMJ and autonomic cholinergic synapses → dose-dependent flaccid paralysis or glandular inhibition. FDA-approved: cervical dystonia, blepharospasm, spasticity (limb, upper/lower), chronic migraine, hyperhidrosis, overactive bladder, strabismus, cosmetic glabellar lines. Reversal requires sprouting of new nerve terminals (~3 months). BoNT/B (Myobloc®/Neurobloc®) cleaves VAMP-2; approved for cervical dystonia.

Tetanus Toxin (TeNT) vs Botulinum Toxin

Both are Zn²⁺-endoproteases cleaving VAMP-2 (TeNT) or multiple SNAREs (BoNT). Key difference: BoNT remains at the NMJ presynaptic terminal after retrograde transport → blocks ACh at motor nerve → flaccid paralysis. TeNT undergoes transcytosis through motor neurons to inhibitory interneurons in spinal cord → blocks GABA/glycine release → loss of inhibitory control → spastic (rigid) paralysis (trismus/lockjaw, opisthotonus). Prevention: tetanus toxoid vaccine (DTP/Tdap).

STXBP1 Epileptic Encephalopathy (Ohtahara / West Syndrome)

Loss-of-function mutations in STXBP1 (encodes Munc18-1) → destabilisation of syntaxin-1 → reduced presynaptic secretory capacity; STXBP1 is one of the most commonly mutated genes in early infantile epileptic encephalopathy (EIEE). Phenotypes: Ohtahara syndrome (neonatal-onset tonic spasms, burst-suppression EEG), West syndrome (infantile spasms, hypsarrhythmia), Dravet syndrome overlap. Treatment: phenobarbital, levetiracetam — response variable; mTOR pathway is being investigated as a therapeutic target.

NSF and Synaptic Plasticity (LTD)

NSF is required not only for SNARE disassembly but also for AMPA receptor trafficking: NSF directly interacts with the GluA2 subunit C-terminus, maintaining surface AMPAR expression. Blocking NSF activity (NEM or GluA2-NSF interference peptides) triggers AMPAR internalisation → LTD-like synaptic depression. NSF activity is thus a constitutive requirement for maintaining basal synaptic strength, independent of its role in vesicle recycling.
