Streptococcus pneumoniae

The leading cause of community-acquired pneumonia and adult bacterial meningitis worldwide. Over 100 polysaccharide serotypes; colonises the nasopharynx asymptomatically before invading the host when defences slip.

Gram-positive, lancet-shaped diplococci living in the nasopharynx of 20–40% of healthy adults. Pneumococcus is a master of opportunistic pathogenesis: it transitions from commensal coloniser to lobar pneumonia, bacteremia, and meningitis when physical or immune barriers are compromised. Its polysaccharide capsule — the primary virulence factor and vaccine target — exists in >100 antigenically distinct serotypes, driving vaccine design toward broad coverage. Penicillin resistance occurs not through β-lactamase but through mosaic mutations in penicillin-binding protein genes (pbp1a, pbp2b, pbp2x) acquired from commensal streptococci.

Streptococcus pneumoniae (the pneumococcus)

Alpha-haemolytic Gram-positive diplococci, facultative anaerobe, optochin-sensitive, bile-soluble. 2.0 Mb genome; no flagella; non-motile. The polysaccharide capsule is the master virulence determinant — anti-phagocytic, anti-complement, anti-opsonin — and the primary vaccine antigen. Pneumolysin (Ply), a cholesterol-dependent cytolysin, is the key toxin: it forms transmembrane pores in host cells and activates complement. Below the capsule, surface proteins PspA, PspC/CbpA, and PiuA mediate adherence, complement evasion, and iron acquisition. After nasopharyngeal colonisation, pneumococcus invades when mucosal barriers are disrupted (viral infection, anatomical defect, immune deficiency), causing lobar pneumonia → bacteremia → meningitis — a stereotyped progression exploiting the same surface-protein toolkit at each anatomical site.

Virulence Factors

Polysaccharide capsule>100 distinct serotypes (CPS). Anti-phagocytic: prevents complement C3b opsonisation and Fc receptor recognition. The dominant vaccine target. Encapsulated strains required for invasive disease; unencapsulated strains cause mucosal-only disease (e.g. otitis media, conjunctivitis).
Pneumolysin (Ply)Cholesterol-dependent cytolysin; forms β-barrel pores in host membranes (lung epithelium, endothelium, erythrocytes). Activates the complement classical pathway; stimulates TNF-α and IL-1β via TLR4. Causes ALI in pneumonia and BBB disruption in meningitis. Ply is cytoplasmic — released via autolysin-mediated autolysis.
PspA (pneumococcal surface protein A)Blocks classical complement activation by binding C-reactive protein and factor H. Highly immunogenic; included in experimental protein vaccines. PspA family 1 (clades 1–2) and family 2 (clades 3–5).
CbpA / PspC (choline-binding protein A)Binds secretory IgA and complement factor H — dual complement evasion and mucosal adhesion. Mediates transcytosis across nasopharyngeal epithelium via polymeric immunoglobulin receptor (pIgR). Essential for invasion.
Autolysin (LytA)N-acetylmuramyl-L-alanine amidase; lyses pneumococcal cells during stationary phase or antibiotic-induced lysis, releasing pneumolysin, DNA, and teichoic acids — amplifying the inflammatory cascade. The biochemical basis of bile solubility and optochin sensitivity.
HyaluronidaseDegrades hyaluronic acid in connective tissue matrix, facilitating spread from alveolar space into interstitium and blood.
IgA1 proteaseCleaves the hinge region of secretory IgA1, destroying mucosal immunity and enabling colonisation despite local antibody. Key colonisation factor shared with H. influenzae and N. meningitidis.

Pathogenesis — Colonisation to Invasive Disease

Stage 1 · Nasopharyngeal colonisation (asymptomatic)

CbpA/PspC binds carbohydrate moieties on nasopharyngeal epithelial cells. IgA1 protease destroys mucosal antibody. Pneumococcus forms biofilms on epithelial surfaces (facilitated by LytA-mediated release of eDNA). Colonisation rates: 20–40% adults, up to 65% in children; highest in winter. Colonisation is the obligate precursor to all invasive disease and the transmission reservoir.

Stage 2 · Lobar pneumonia — alveolar invasion

Aspiration of colonising organisms past epiglottic/mucociliary defences (impaired by influenza, alcohol, smoking, or immunosuppression) delivers pneumococci to lower airways. Bacteria bind platelet-activating factor receptor (PAF-R) on type II pneumocytes via phosphorylcholine on teichoic acid, triggering transcytosis. Alveolar macrophages initiate phagocytosis — overwhelmed when capsule load is high. IL-8/CXCL1 recruits neutrophils; massive exudate fills alveolar spaces. Classic lobar consolidation on CXR reflects confluent alveolar flooding — the radiographic hallmark distinguishing pneumococcal from atypical CAP.

Stage 3 · Bacteremia

Bacteremia occurs in 15–30% of pneumococcal pneumonia; mortality rises sharply. Capsule enables intravascular survival by resisting complement-mediated killing and neutrophil phagocytosis. Bacteremic pneumococcal pneumonia is associated with sepsis, DIC, and multiorgan failure. The spleen is the primary site of pneumococcal clearance — asplenic/hyposplenic patients (sickle cell disease, post-splenectomy) are at dramatically elevated risk of fulminant sepsis ("overwhelming post-splenectomy infection," OPSI).

Stage 4 · Meningitis — CNS invasion

Bacteremia seeds the choroid plexus; pneumococcal surface adhesins (PavB, CbpA) bind fibronectin and laminin on brain endothelium. Transcytosis across the blood-brain barrier initiates CSF invasion. Pneumolysin and cell wall components (peptidoglycan, teichoic acid) activate TLR2/TLR4 on microglia and astrocytes, driving IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-6 surges. Neutrophil infiltration into CSF produces H₂O₂ and proteases that damage neurons, basilar cistern adhesions causing CN palsies, and aquaporin-mediated brain oedema. Case fatality rate 20–30% despite antibiotics; 30–50% of survivors have neurological sequelae (hearing loss, cognitive deficit).

Penicillin Resistance — pbp Mutations, Not β-Lactamase

Mechanism: altered penicillin-binding proteins (PBPs)

Unlike Staphylococcus aureus, pneumococcus does not produce β-lactamase. Resistance arises through point mutations and mosaic gene sequences in the PBP genes — pbp1a, pbp2b, and pbp2x — encoding cell wall transpeptidases. These mutations (often acquired from commensal streptococci via natural transformation and recombination) reduce the affinity of PBPs for β-lactam antibiotics. The clinical breakpoints: penicillin MIC <0.06 μg/mL = susceptible; 0.12–1 = intermediate; ≥2 = resistant (non-meningitis); ≥0.12 = non-susceptible for meningitis (lower threshold due to poor CSF penetration).

Epidemiology and clonal spread

Global spread driven by successful resistant clones (e.g. Spain²⁻-23F, Taiwan¹⁹F-14, multidrug-resistant 19A — ST320 clone) disseminated internationally. Penicillin non-susceptibility varies geographically: <5% in most of Europe, >30% in some East Asian and Middle Eastern countries. Conjugate vaccines targeting 19A have significantly reduced resistant-clone prevalence where widely used.

Clinical impact and treatment adjustments

For non-meningitis CAP: amoxicillin remains effective for most intermediate-resistance strains (pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic target attainment). For meningitis: use high-dose ceftriaxone (2 g IV q12h) ± vancomycin empirically until susceptibilities known — CSF penetration of penicillin/amoxicillin is inadequate against resistant strains. For MDR strains: moxifloxacin (respiratory fluoroquinolone), linezolid, or ceftaroline as alternatives.
