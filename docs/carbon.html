Carbon

Symbol: C  ·  Z: 6  ·  Mass: 12.011 u  ·  Config: [He] 2s² 2p²  ·  Valence: 4

Carbon is the universal backbone element of organic chemistry and the second most abundant element in the human body by mass (18%). Its tetravalence, hybridisation flexibility (sp³ → tetrahedral; sp² → planar; sp → linear), bond stability under physiological conditions, and oxidation state range from −4 (methane) to +4 (CO₂) make it uniquely suited as the architectural scaffold of all biomolecules. The progressive oxidation of carbon atoms — from highly reduced fats through glucose to CO₂ — is the bioenergetic engine of aerobic life, yielding ~32 ATP per glucose molecule.

Overview & Atomic Properties

Carbon (symbol C, Z=6, mass 12.011 u) is a nonmetal in Period 2, Group 14 of the periodic table with electron configuration [He] 2s² 2p². Its four valence electrons permit four covalent bonds of comparable strength — to itself, H, N, O, S, and P — making it the universal organic scaffold. A 70 kg adult contains approximately 12.6 kg of carbon, distributed primarily in proteins (~43%), lipids (~33%), carbohydrates (~2%), nucleic acids (~1%), and small metabolites.

Hybridisation and geometry: sp³ (tetrahedral, 109.5°) — saturated C in amino acid α-carbons, fatty acid chains, sugar rings. sp² (trigonal planar, 120°) — peptide bonds (partial double bond character constrains protein backbone), purine/pyrimidine bases (aromatic stacking in DNA), carbonyl groups (C=O in aldehydes, ketones, carboxylic acids). sp (linear, 180°) — CO₂ (O=C=O); the end-product of aerobic metabolism and the substrate for bicarbonate buffering.

Structural roles of carbon: (1) Proteins: every amino acid contains a sp³ α-carbon stereocentre; 299 planar sp² peptide bonds in a 300-residue protein constrain protein backbone geometry and define secondary structure. (2) Nucleic acids: deoxyribose/ribose C1–C5 scaffold carries bases and phosphodiester linkages; purine/pyrimidine flat aromatic carbons enable π–π stacking in B-DNA. (3) Membranes: saturated fatty acid C–H₂ chains pack tightly (gel phase); cis double bonds (sp² carbons) introduce kinks preventing crystallisation — membrane fluidity depends on sp³ vs. sp² carbon ratio.

Absorption & Metabolism

Dietary carbon arrives primarily as carbohydrates (starch, sucrose), proteins (amino acids), and fats (triglycerides). Digestive enzymes (amylase, proteases, lipases) hydrolyse these macromolecules to absorbable units (monosaccharides, amino acids, fatty acids). Carbon balance is governed by energy balance: in a eucaloric state, carbon input (food) = carbon output (CO₂ exhaled + urea/uric acid excreted). In obesity, dietary carbon exceeds oxidative demand and is stored as triglyceride (C–H chains) in adipocytes.

One-carbon metabolism (folate/SAM cycle): Single-carbon units (methyl, methylene, formyl) carried by tetrahydrofolate (THF) and S-adenosylmethionine (SAM) are critical for nucleotide synthesis (purines, thymidylate), methylation of DNA/histones, and synthesis of neurotransmitters. Deficiency of folate or B12 (which regenerates THF via methionine synthase) causes megaloblastic anaemia and neural tube defects.

Isotopic tracers: ¹⁴C (radioactive, t½ 5,730 y) labels metabolites for autoradiography and flux studies. ¹³C NMR traces metabolic routes non-destructively in perfused organ preparations. Radiocarbon dating measures ¹⁴C decay in organic remains; applied clinically to measure cell turnover rates (¹⁴C from atmospheric nuclear tests as a historical pulse label).
