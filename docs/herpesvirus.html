L01 Molecular · Pathogen · dsDNA Virus

Herpesviridae

The eight human herpesviruses share a conserved architecture — an enveloped, icosahedral dsDNA virion with a characteristic tegument layer — yet occupy remarkably diverse biological niches. Their defining feature is lifelong latency in specific host cell populations, punctuated by periodic reactivation that drives transmission and disease. Herpesviruses have co-evolved with vertebrates for over 180 million years and have devised elaborate strategies to subvert innate immunity, MHC-I antigen presentation, and complement.

Family: Herpesviridae Genome: ~125–235 kb dsDNA (linear) Virion: ~200 nm enveloped Subfamilies: Alpha-, Beta-, Gammaherpesvirinae

The Eight Human Herpesviruses

All eight members infect humans as their primary or a significant host. They are subdivided by biology and genomic relatedness into three subfamilies: Alphaherpesvirinae (HSV-1, HSV-2, VZV) are neurotropic with rapid lytic cycles; Betaherpesvirinae (CMV, HHV-6A, HHV-6B, HHV-7) are lymphotropic and have slow replication cycles; Gammaherpesvirinae (EBV, KSHV) are lymphotropic and are associated with lymphoid malignancies and solid tumours.

Virion Architecture

All herpesviruses share a four-layered structure that is conserved across the family, with a diameter of approximately 150–200 nm for the enveloped virion.

  • Core (innermost): The linear dsDNA genome (125–235 kb depending on species) is packaged in a toroidal or liquid-crystalline array within the capsid interior, under high pressure (~20 atmospheres in some estimates). The genome is packaged with spermidine and other polyamines that partially neutralize the negative charge of DNA.
  • Icosahedral capsid: T=16 icosahedral symmetry; 162 capsomers (150 hexons + 12 pentons) assembled from the major capsid protein (VP5/UL19 for HSV). Each penton sits at an icosahedral vertex and is surrounded by 5 hexons. Triplexes (VP23/VP19C heterotrimers) stabilize hexon–hexon and hexon–penton interfaces. Capsid diameter is ~125 nm.
  • Tegument: An amorphous protein layer occupying the space between capsid and envelope. Contains ~25 viral proteins in HSV; functionally critical for immediately post-entry events. Key tegument proteins include:
    • VP16 (UL48/α-TIF): Transactivator; recruits Oct-1 and HCF-1 to form a complex that activates all immediate-early (IE) viral genes via TAATGARAT elements
    • UL41 (vhs, virion host shutoff protein): RNase that degrades cellular mRNAs non-selectively immediately after entry, blunting the early innate response
    • VP22 (UL49): Most abundant tegument protein; nuclear and cytoplasmic roles; facilitates capsid transport
    • UL36/UL37: Inner tegument proteins that directly contact the capsid and are required for retrograde transport along microtubules
  • Lipid envelope: Derived from host cell membranes (trans-Golgi network for secondary envelopment) and studded with viral glycoproteins. For HSV, the essential entry glycoproteins are gB (fusion protein, class III), gD (receptor binding; binds HVEM, nectin-1, or 3-O-sulfated heparan sulfate), and the gH/gL heterodimer (fusogen activator). gC is non-essential but binds heparan sulfate proteoglycans (initial attachment) and complement C3b (immune evasion).
Envelopment is complex: HSV capsids undergo primary envelopment at the inner nuclear membrane then de-envelopment at the outer nuclear membrane to reach the cytoplasm; they then re-envelop into trans-Golgi-derived vesicles (secondary envelopment) before exocytosis. This unusual dual-envelopment pathway is conserved across Herpesviridae.

Latency Mechanisms

HSV Neuronal Latency

Following primary orofacial infection, HSV-1 replicates in mucosal epithelial cells and infects sensory nerve terminals. Viral capsids undergo retrograde axonal transport at ~2 μm/s along microtubules (driven by dynein/dynactin motors via UL36/UL37 tegument adapters) to the cell body of sensory neurons in the trigeminal ganglia (for HSV-1) or sacral DRG (for HSV-2).

In the neuronal nucleus, the linear genome circularizes and is maintained as a circular episome associated with nucleosomes in a chromatin state that silences all lytic genes. The only abundant viral transcript during latency is the LAT (latency-associated transcript), a stable ~2 kb intron-containing non-coding RNA derived from a longer (~8.3 kb) primary transcript. LAT is not translated into protein but plays multiple roles:

  • Promotes epigenetic silencing of ICP0, ICP4, and other lytic genes via heterochromatin marks (H3K27me3)
  • Encodes multiple small RNAs (miRNAs) that suppress ICP0 and ICP34.5 expression
  • May promote neuron survival (anti-apoptotic function)

The key lytic transactivators ICP0 (ubiquitin ligase, disrupts ND10/PML bodies), ICP4 (major transcriptional activator), and VP16 (tegument transactivator) are all absent or severely suppressed during latency. CD8+ T cells accumulate around latently infected neurons and provide ongoing immune surveillance, expressing granzyme B and IFN-γ without killing neurons — an immunological equilibrium called "non-cytolytic control."

VZV Latency

VZV establishes latency in dorsal root ganglia, trigeminal ganglia, and geniculate ganglia following primary varicella. Unlike HSV, VZV latency involves expression of multiple viral genes including VLT (VZV latency-associated transcript) and ORF63 protein. VZV genome is maintained as a circular episome but is less extensively silenced than HSV, possibly reflecting evolutionary adaptation to VZV's unique need to maintain competence for reactivation across decades.

EBV Latency in Memory B Cells

EBV infects naïve B cells via CD21 (complement receptor 2) in the oropharynx, drives them into a growth-transformed latency III state expressing all 9 EBNAs and LMPs (Epstein-Barr nuclear antigens and latent membrane proteins). Transformed B blasts are eliminated by CD8+ cytotoxic T lymphocytes (this process generates the classic mononucleosis syndrome). Cells that survive differentiate through a germinal-centre reaction to long-lived memory B cells, in which EBV establishes latency I: only EBNA1 is expressed. EBNA1 is required for episome maintenance and segregation at cell division; it evades CD8+ T cell recognition via its Gly-Ala repeat domain, which inhibits its own proteasomal degradation and thereby prevents peptide presentation on MHC class I.

EBV and autoimmunity: Epidemiological and molecular evidence links prior EBV infection to an estimated 32-fold increased risk of multiple sclerosis (MS). Molecular mimicry between EBNA1 (residues 411–426) and GlialCAM, an MS autoantigen, may be a contributing mechanism (Lanz et al., Science 2022).

Reactivation

Reactivation from latency is triggered by diverse stimuli that converge on transcriptional de-repression of lytic gene promoters. For HSV, stimuli include UV light exposure (facial skin), fever, psychological stress, hormonal changes, and immunosuppression. Mechanistically, activation likely involves:

  1. Disruption of heterochromatin silencing on IE gene promoters — loss of H3K27me3 and gain of H3K4me3 marks
  2. Local neuronal activity changes or stress-induced neuropeptides influencing VP16 availability or nuclear factor activity
  3. VP16 in incoming virion tegument (or newly expressed after initial de-repression) recruits the cellular coactivator HCF-1 and Oct-1 to form a tripartite complex on TAATGARAT motifs in IE promoters, driving robust transcription of ICP0 and ICP4
  4. ICP0 ubiquitin E3 ligase activity disrupts PML nuclear bodies (ND10 domains), removes repressive chromatin factors, and broadly de-represses the viral genome
  5. Full lytic replication proceeds; newly assembled capsids undergo anterograde axonal transport (kinesin-dependent) to the mucosal surface where progeny virus is shed, often without clinical symptoms (subclinical shedding)

For VZV, reactivation is strongly associated with declining cell-mediated immunity, particularly VZV-specific CD4+ T cells, which normally maintain quiescence. Age-related immune senescence and immunosuppressive therapy (corticosteroids, biologics, post-transplant regimens) are the dominant risk factors for clinical zoster.

Immune Evasion Strategies

Herpesviruses encode numerous proteins dedicated to subverting both innate and adaptive immune responses, reflecting the evolutionary pressure of lifelong coexistence with immunocompetent hosts.
