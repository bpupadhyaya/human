Guillain-Barré Syndrome

Guillain-Barré syndrome (GBS) is an acute immune-mediated polyradiculoneuropathy and the most common cause of acute flaccid paralysis in the post-polio era, affecting 1–2 per 100,000 people annually worldwide across all age groups.

GBS is triggered in most cases by molecular mimicry — a prior infection (most commonly Campylobacter jejuni) generates cross-reactive antibodies against ganglioside epitopes shared between the pathogen and peripheral nerve axons or myelin, causing rapidly progressive ascending weakness, areflexia, and in 20–30% of patients, respiratory failure requiring mechanical ventilation.

Guillain-Barré Syndrome

GBS is an acute autoimmune polyradiculoneuropathy in which molecular mimicry to infectious antigens (most often C. jejuni lipooligosaccharide) generates IgG antibodies against gangliosides (GM1, GD1a, GQ1b) that attack peripheral nerve components at nodes of Ranvier. AIDP (demyelinating) dominates in Western populations; AMAN (pure axonal, anti-GM1/GD1a) predominates in Asia. Treatment: IVIG 2 g/kg over 5 days or plasma exchange (4–6 sessions), equivalent in efficacy; no role for steroids. Outcome: 80% walk independently by 6 months; 5% mortality; 10% permanent disability.

Epidemiology and Antecedent Infections

GBS has a global incidence of 1–2 cases per 100,000 person-years, with a slight male preponderance (male-to-female ratio ~1.5:1) and a bimodal age distribution (peaks in young adulthood and in the elderly). It is the most common cause of acute flaccid paralysis since the near-eradication of poliomyelitis, with ~100,000 new cases annually worldwide. Approximately two-thirds of patients have a documented antecedent infection in the 4–6 weeks preceding neurological symptoms:

Pathophysiology — Molecular Mimicry and Nerve Injury

Molecular mimicry is the central immunological mechanism in GBS. The ganglioside hypothesis (Yuki et al., 1993) established that C. jejuni lipooligosaccharide (LOS) bears terminal oligosaccharide sequences structurally identical to mammalian gangliosides on peripheral nerve membranes:

  1. Infection: C. jejuni gastroenteritis (typically 1–4 weeks before GBS onset); bacterial LOS contains terminal Galβ1-3GalNAc (GM1-like) or Neu5Acα2-3Galβ1-3GalNAc (GD1a-like) trisaccharides — genetically determined by cst-II / LOS locus; only specific C. jejuni strains (HS:19, HS:41, PEN 19) are GBS-associated
  2. Immune activation: Mucosal B cells and gut-associated lymphoid tissue mount IgA/IgG responses against LOS → cross-reactive antibodies generated that recognise GM1 and GD1a on peripheral nerve axolemma (AMAN) or GQ1b on oculomotor nerve paranodal myelin (MFS)
  3. Antibody-mediated nerve injury (AMAN): IgG anti-GM1/GD1a antibodies bind to ganglioside-rich domains at nodes of Ranvier of motor axons → C1q binding → classical complement activation → C3b deposition → C5b-9 membrane attack complex (MAC) formation on axolemma at nodes → macrophage FcγR-mediated recognition of complement-opsonised node → macrophages enter periaxonal space via paranodal junctions → strip periaxonal myelin from axon and may damage axon directly
  4. AIDP demyelination: More heterogeneous; T cell and macrophage infiltration of peripheral nerve roots and nerve trunks; macrophage-mediated stripping of intact compact myelin (bystander demyelination); T cell-dependent mechanisms including epitope spreading to myelin proteins (P2, PMP22, MBP); in anti-NF155 IgG4 or anti-CASPR1/contactin-1 antibody cases: specific paranodal disruption without macrophage infiltration (“nodopathy”)
  5. Complement at the node (AMAN pathological series): C3d and C5b-9 deposits demonstrated immunohistochemically at nodes of Ranvier of motor nerves in AMAN autopsy material; eculizumab (anti-C5 complement inhibitor) reduced nerve injury and improved outcome in pilot GBS case series, providing proof-of-concept

Clinical Features

GBS classically presents as a rapidly progressive, ascending, symmetric weakness beginning in the distal lower limbs and advancing proximally over days (nadir reached within 4 weeks by definition; typically 1–3 weeks). The full clinical picture:

FeatureDetailFrequency
Ascending weakness Begins in distal legs (foot drop, difficulty climbing stairs); advances to proximal legs, arms, respiratory muscles, and cranial nerves; in AMAN, upper limb involvement may be minimal >95%
Areflexia Loss of deep tendon reflexes; typically begins in ankles and extends proximally; often universal by peak disease; earliest and most reliable sign >95%

Sensory symptoms Paraesthesiae and numbness in glove-and-stocking distribution; objective sensory loss relatively mild in AMAN (motor-selective); loss of proprioception and vibration in AIDP; large-fibre sensory ataxia ~80% subjective; less objective deficit in AMAN
Respiratory failure Diaphragm and intercostal muscle weakness; FVC <20 mL/kg, NIF < −30 cmH&sub2;O, or peak cough flow <270 L/min indicate impending failure (the “20-30-40 rule”); requires ICU monitoring; intubation in 20–30% 20–30%

Cranial nerve involvement Bilateral facial palsy (most common cranial nerve sign, >50%); oropharyngeal weakness (dysphagia, aspiration risk); ophthalmoplegia (in MFS and AIDP); rarely papilloedema 50–75% facial; <5% ophthalmoplegia in AIDP

Diagnosis

Diagnosis rests on the clinical picture, CSF findings, and nerve conduction studies. Anti-ganglioside serology provides subtype confirmation and prognostic information:

InvestigationFindingInterpretation
CSF (cerebrospinal fluid) Cytoalbuminous dissociation: elevated protein (50–200 mg/dL; sometimes higher) with normal or <10 cells/µL white cell count; elevated protein may be absent in the first week (20% at 1 week; 80% at 3 weeks) Reflects protein leak from inflamed nerve roots into the CSF space in the absence of meningitis; pleocytosis (>50 cells) suggests HIV-associated GBS, Lyme, CMV, or West Nile GBS — or not GBS
Nerve conduction studies (NCS/EMG) AIDP: prolonged distal motor latencies (DML), reduced NCV (<70% LLN), conduction block, temporal dispersion, absent or prolonged F-waves, preserved or mildly reduced CMAP amplitude; AMAN: low CMAP amplitude with relatively normal DML, NCV, and sensory responses; sural sparing pattern Serial NCS (weeks 1, 2, 3) needed; early studies may be normal or show only F-wave abnormality; nerve root conduction block distinguishable from Wallerian degeneration by recovery pattern
Anti-ganglioside serology Anti-GM1/GD1a IgG (AMAN); anti-GQ1b IgG (MFS, >90% sensitivity); anti-NF155 IgG4, anti-CASPR1/contactin-1 IgG4 (AIDP paranodopathy subtype) Positive serology supports specific subtype; influences prognosis (anti-NF155 → consider CIDP; anti-GQ1b → MFS); negative serology does not exclude GBS
MRI spine/nerve roots Gadolinium enhancement of lumbosacral nerve roots and cauda equina (T1 post-Gad); found in ~80% of AIDP in first 2 weeks Supports diagnosis; not required routinely; useful when NCS equivocal; nerve root enhancement reflects BBB disruption
Brighton criteria (2011): Four levels of diagnostic certainty based on bilateral flaccid limb weakness, decreased/absent DTRs in weak limbs, monophasic illness, and interval 12 h–28 days between onset and nadir. Level 1 (highest certainty) requires all features plus CSF cytoalbuminous dissociation and electrodiagnostic evidence of PNS pathology.

Treatment

TreatmentRegimenEvidence and mechanism
IVIG (intravenous immunoglobulin) 2 g/kg over 5 days (0.4 g/kg/day × 5); standard of care worldwide Dutch GBS study (van der Meché & Schmitz, NEJM 1992) showed IVIG equivalent to plasma exchange at 4 weeks; Dutch IGOS study (2022) confirms equivalence. Mechanisms: FcRn saturation accelerates clearance of pathogenic IgG; FcγRIIb (inhibitory) engagement on macrophages/B cells; anti-idiotype neutralisation of ganglioside antibodies; complement inhibition
Plasma exchange (PE) 4–6 exchanges over 1–2 weeks (total 200–250 mL/kg); removes circulating anti-ganglioside IgG French Cooperative Group (Greenfield et al., NEJM 1992) established PE benefit over supportive care; French & North American trials showed 4–6 exchanges superior to 2; combination IVIG + PE no better than either alone (Dutch trial); PE logistically more demanding; preferred in settings lacking IVIG
Eculizumab Anti-C5 complement inhibitor; IV; meningococcal vaccination required before initiation Japanese pilot RCT (Misawa et al., Ann Neurol 2018): 8 patients; significantly better MRC sum score at 24 weeks; phase II/III trials (NCT04475874) ongoing; rationale: blocks C5a and MAC (C5b-9) formation at nodes of Ranvier in AMAN
Corticosteroids No role in GBS Multiple RCTs including the Dutch IV methylprednisolone trial (van Koningsveld et al., Lancet 2004): steroids alone inferior to IVIG; combination IVIG + IV methylprednisolone showed no benefit over IVIG alone at 4 weeks; accelerated short-term recovery claimed in older studies not confirmed; steroids contraindicated in GBS
Supportive care ICU monitoring for respiratory failure (FVC <20 mL/kg → elective intubation); cardiac monitoring (telemetry for dysautonomia); early tracheostomy if ventilated >2 weeks; VTE prophylaxis (subcutaneous heparin or LMWH); early physiotherapy; pain management (gabapentin, opioids) Mortality has fallen from >10% to ~5% with modern ICU care; respiratory failure management is the dominant determinant of early outcomes; early enteral nutrition; DVT/PE prophylaxis essential (patients immobile for weeks)

Prognosis and Outcome Prediction

Outcome in GBS is variable. The Erasmus GBS Outcome Scale (EGOS) and modified EGOS (mEGOS) are validated prognostic tools using age, preceding diarrhoea, and GBS disability score at admission to predict ambulatory status at 6 months:

Prognostic markerDirection of effectNotes
Age >60 years Worse Reduced axon regenerative capacity; delayed immune resolution
Preceding diarrhoea (C. jejuni) Worse (in AMAN with Wallerian degeneration) AMAN with axon loss vs. reversible conduction failure: former has much slower recovery
Rapid progression to nadir Worse <1 week to nadir → more axon loss; longer to remyelinate
Requirement for mechanical ventilation Worse Correlates with severe axonal involvement; prolonged ICU stay → ICU-acquired weakness compounds GBS weakness
CMAP amplitude <20% LLN Worse Low CMAP → severe axon loss; prolonged recovery; not applicable to reversible AMAN
Anti-GQ1b (MFS) Better MFS: excellent prognosis; near-universal full recovery by 6 months

Overall outcomes at 6–12 months: ~80% walk unaided; ~5% die (mainly in elderly from dysautonomia, sepsis, or VTE); ~10% have moderate-to-severe permanent residual disability; ~5–10% relapse (raises concern for CIDP). The MRC sum score (sum of Medical Research Council scores in 12 muscle groups, max 60) tracks recovery; improvement of ≥1 GBS disability scale point at 4 weeks with IVIG signals response.

Distinguishing GBS from CIDP

CIDP (Chronic Inflammatory Demyelinating Polyneuropathy)

GBS is monophasic by definition (nadir ≤4 weeks, then plateau ≥2 weeks, then improvement). Patients who continue to worsen beyond 4 weeks, or who relapse ≥3 times, or who require ongoing immunotherapy for sustained improvement, are reclassified as CIDP. Anti-NF155 IgG4 antibodies (and anti-CASPR1, anti-contactin-1) strongly associate with a CIDP-like chronic relapsing course. CIDP is treated with long-term IVIG, plasma exchange, or corticosteroids; anti-NF155 patients respond poorly to IVIG and may require rituximab.
