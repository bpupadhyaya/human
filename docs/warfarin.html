Warfarin (Coumadin)

Vitamin K antagonist anticoagulant — inhibits VKORC1 to deplete the vitamin K hydroquinone required for γ-carboxylation of clotting factors II, VII, IX, and X, leaving them functionally inactive.

The dominant oral anticoagulant for 60 years. Narrow therapeutic window, significant drug/food interactions, and the landmark RE-LY trial that launched the DOAC era. Still irreplaceable for mechanical heart valves.

Warfarin

Class: Vitamin K Antagonist (VKA) / Coumarin Anticoagulant  ·  Route: Oral  |  Brand: Coumadin, Jantoven, Marevan  |  Monitoring: INR (target 2.0–3.0 most indications; 2.5–3.5 mechanical valves)

Warfarin inhibits VKORC1 (vitamin K epoxide reductase), blocking recycling of vitamin K epoxide to vitamin K hydroquinone — the obligate cofactor for hepatic γ-glutamyl carboxylase (GGCX). Without γ-carboxylation, clotting factors II, VII, IX, X lose their Gla domain functionality and cannot bind Ca²⁺/phospholipid surfaces. The drug interacts with CYP2C9 (metabolism) and VKORC1 polymorphisms (sensitivity) — explaining 40–50% of dose variability. INR monitoring is mandatory. First-line for mechanical valves; DOACs preferred for non-valvular AF and VTE in most patients.

Vitamin K Cycle & VKORC1 Inhibition

  Dietary Vitamin K1 (phylloquinone) / K2 (menaquinone)
          │  Absorbed in gut, delivered to liver
          ▼
  Vitamin K Hydroquinone (KH₂) — ACTIVE cofactor form
          │  γ-Glutamyl Carboxylase (GGCX) uses KH₂ as cofactor
          │  Adds -COOH to Glu residues on clotting factor Gla domains
          │  KH₂ → Vitamin K Epoxide (KO) during carboxylation
          ▼
  Factors II, VII, IX, X — γ-CARBOXYLATED = FUNCTIONAL
  (Gla domain binds Ca²⁺ → phospholipid membrane → activation)

          ┌──────────────────────────────────────┐
          │ WARFARIN BLOCKS THIS RECYCLING STEP  │
          └──────────────────────────────────────┘
          │
  Vitamin K Epoxide (KO)
          │  VKORC1 (Vitamin K Epoxide Reductase Complex Subunit 1)
          │  Normally reduces KO → Vitamin K → KH₂
          │  WARFARIN inhibits VKORC1 (competitive)
          ▼
  KO accumulates; KH₂ depleted
          ▼
  GGCX cannot carboxylate new clotting factor molecules
          ▼
  Non-functional factors (PIVKA) secreted → INR rises (3–5 days)

Why 3–5 days to full effect? Factors already in circulation retain full function until cleared. Factor VII has the shortest half-life (~6h) → INR begins rising within 24h. Factors II and X (half-lives 60–72 h and 40–45 h) take 3–5 days to deplete sufficiently for full anticoagulation — requiring bridging therapy in urgent situations.

Pharmacogenomics — CYP2C9 & VKORC1

Warfarin dose variability is among the highest of any commonly prescribed drug. Two genes explain ~40–50% of variance:

Gene / VariantEffectDose Implication
CYP2C9*2 (R144C)~70% normal S-warfarin metabolism~20% dose reduction
CYP2C9*3 (I359L)~5% normal S-warfarin metabolism~50% dose reduction; major bleeding risk
VKORC1 -1639G>A (A allele)↓ VKORC1 expression → greater warfarin sensitivityDose requirement ↓ ~25–50%; common in Asians (~89% A allele)
CYP4F2*3 V433M↓ vitamin K oxidation → higher intrahepatic K → warfarin resistanceDose requirement ↑ ~10–15%

Pharmacogenomic dosing algorithms (CPIC guidelines, EU-PACT) incorporating CYP2C9 + VKORC1 + clinical factors improve time-in-therapeutic-range (TTR), particularly in European and Asian ancestry patients.

Major Drug Interactions

  • Amiodarone (↑ INR, major): Potent CYP2C9 and CYP3A4 inhibitor; warfarin levels rise 30–50%; may take weeks to reach new equilibrium; reduce warfarin dose ~30–50% empirically and monitor INR weekly
  • Azole antifungals — fluconazole, voriconazole (↑ INR, major): CYP2C9 inhibition; single-dose fluconazole can raise INR to dangerous levels; avoid if possible or reduce warfarin dose and monitor INR daily
  • Rifampicin (↓ INR, major): Potent CYP2C9 and CYP1A2 inducer; warfarin levels fall dramatically (2–5× dose increase may be required); INR must be monitored closely on starting and stopping rifampicin
  • Aspirin/NSAIDs (↑ bleeding risk, pharmacodynamic): Do not raise INR but increase GI mucosal bleeding risk; aspirin also inhibits platelets; avoid concurrent use unless specifically indicated (e.g., post-MI with mechanical valve — use lowest aspirin dose)
  • Increased dietary vitamin K (↓ INR): Large increases in leafy green vegetables (kale, spinach, broccoli — vitamin K1-rich) can lower INR; advise consistent intake rather than complete avoidance

Reversal

  • Minor bleeding / supratherapeutic INR without bleeding: Hold warfarin ± oral vitamin K1 (phytomenadione) 1–2.5 mg PO; recheck INR in 24–48h
  • Major bleeding: 4-factor PCC (prothrombin complex concentrate: factors II, VII, IX, X) 25–50 units/kg IV + vitamin K1 10 mg IV slow infusion — immediate reversal of INR to near normal within 15–30 min; vitamin K prevents rebound anticoagulation as PCC wears off (half-life ~6–24h)
  • Life-threatening / intracranial hemorrhage: 4-factor PCC is now preferred over fresh frozen plasma (FFP) — faster, lower volume, no infectious risk, more predictable factor levels; target INR <1.5
